# Babylon — Contested City and Restoration

Civilization: Babylon and Babylonia across Chaldean, Assyrian, appointed, rebel, and Nabopolassar regimes  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point III, Glaüshouse  
Date focus: 722–612 BCE  
Status: Reviewed dossier; regime chronology, archive survival, and scholarly attribution require continuing audit; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Chronicles, king lists, royal inscriptions, legal and administrative tablets,
letters, temple and family archives, scholarly texts, building records, and
archaeology document different actors. Assyrian and Babylonian royal accounts
remain political genres rather than neutral damage or recovery reports.

### Scholarly interpretation

Regime legitimacy, the reach of local institutions, the archaeological extent
of Sennacherib's 689 destruction, Esarhaddon's rebuilding, archive continuity,
scholarly custody, and the internal chronology of the final Assyrian years
require continuing research.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Glaüshouse receives longitudinal custody, damage-and-restoration provenance,
institutional continuity analysis, and comparison among plural scholarly
witnesses. It receives no divine-punishment diagnosis, temple Clearance,
royal rebuilding absolution, or claim that continuity proves integrity.

## Historical overview

Babylon remained a civic, cultic, scholarly, and political center while its
kingship changed repeatedly. Marduk-apla-iddina II challenged Assyria; Sargon
II and Sennacherib intervened; several local or appointed kings ruled briefly;
and conflict with Elam continued. Sennacherib destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE.
Esarhaddon sponsored rebuilding and institutional restoration. The civil war
between Ashurbanipal and Babylonian king Shamash-shuma-ukin ended in 648.
Nabopolassar took power in 626 and, with Median allies, helped destroy Nineveh
in 612. His dynasty continues into Point IV.

## Chronology

- **721–710 BCE:** Marduk-apla-iddina II rules Babylon before Sargon II retakes
  it; he briefly returns amid the 703 crisis.
- **early seventh century:** Sennacherib cycles through direct intervention,
  appointed kings, and war with Babylonian-Elamite coalitions.
- **689 BCE:** Sennacherib captures, sacks, and destroys Babylon.
- **680–669 BCE:** Esarhaddon initiates rebuilding and cult restoration;
  royal narratives recast causation and responsibility.
- **668–648 BCE:** Shamash-shuma-ukin rules Babylon; rebellion and civil war
  end in siege and death.
- **626 BCE:** Nabopolassar establishes independent rule amid Assyrian crisis.
- **614 and 612 BCE:** Assur and Nineveh fall to Babylonian and Median forces.
- **after 612 BCE:** Nabopolassar's state continues as the Neo-Babylonian
  assignment in Point IV.

## Major figures

Marduk-apla-iddina II, Sargon II, Sennacherib, Bel-ibni, Ashur-nadin-shumi,
Mushezib-Marduk, Esarhaddon, Shamash-shuma-ukin, Ashurbanipal, Nabopolassar,
and Cyaxares. Temple personnel, scholars, merchants, landholders, urban
families, tribal leaders, soldiers, and displaced residents prevent a
king-only history.

## Major cities

Babylon and Esagil; Borsippa, Sippar, Kutha, Nippur, Uruk, and other southern
cities; the Sealand and Bit-Yakin; Assur and Nineveh; Elamite and Median
frontiers. `Babylon` cannot flatten southern Mesopotamia into one city or one
unchanging political constituency.

## Government

Babylonian kingship, Assyrian direct rule, appointed rulers, local assemblies
or elders where attested, temples, governors, scribes, merchant and landholding
families, tribal leaders, and military coalitions shared or contested
implementation. King lists preserve succession conventions, not a complete
constitution or unanimous recognition.

## Religion

Marduk and Esagil carried exceptional civic and royal significance alongside
Nabu, Ishtar, Shamash, and other city cults. Royal destruction and rebuilding
texts frame catastrophe through divine anger and reconciliation. Glaüshouse
records this historical worldview but does not treat it as clinical cause,
consent, or moral clearance.

## Economy

Canal agriculture, dates, grain, livestock, reeds, textiles, land tenure,
temple and palace income, merchant credit, taxes, rents, labor, and urban craft
continued unevenly through political rupture. Surviving contracts may reflect
specific archives and communities rather than the whole population.

## Trade

Rivers, canals, Gulf routes, and roads linked Babylonia with Assyria, Elam,
Iranian regions, the Gulf, and the Levant. Timber, stone, metals, grain, dates,
textiles, livestock, crafted goods, people, and information moved as trade,
tribute, tax, booty, conscription, forced movement, or institutional transfer.

## Science

Cuneiform mathematical, astronomical, calendrical, medical, divinatory,
lexical, historical, and literary traditions continued through temple, family,
court, and intercity scholarly networks. Exact tablet date, copy history,
profession, place, and patron must accompany any knowledge claim.

## Technology

Canals, quays, walls, gates, temples, domestic buildings, boats, brick
production, storage, tablets, seals, weights, metallurgy, textiles, and estate
systems required maintained materials and labor. Destruction and rebuilding
are service histories, not just monumental episodes.

## Architecture

Esagil, Etemenanki, city walls, gates, quays, temples, streets, and houses were
damaged, rebuilt, and later monumentalized in several phases. The dossier does
not backdate Nebuchadnezzar II's best-known cityscape into this Point. Hollow
Grove translates visible repair provenance rather than copying an Ishtar Gate.

## Military

Babylonian, Chaldean, Aramaean, Elamite, Assyrian, and Median actors formed
changing coalitions. Siege, sack, starvation, the 689 destruction, the 648
civil-war ending, and the 626–612 revolt reveal severe civilian and
institutional costs. Those actions are cautions, never Glaüshouse Gifts.

## Art

Inscribed bricks, seals, cult objects, temple furnishings, glazed materials,
sculpture, tablets, and scribal layouts survive unevenly. Royal building and
destruction texts are designed self-presentations whose claims must be tested
against chronicles, archives, and archaeology.

## Language

Akkadian in Babylonian and Assyrian forms, Sumerian learned usage, Aramaic
speech and alphabetic writing, and specialist scribal registers coexist.
Language, script, profession, city, and political allegiance are not treated
as interchangeable identities.

## Major texts

- Babylonian chronicles and king lists.
- Legal, administrative, family, temple, and commercial archives.
- Scholarly tablets, colophons, omen reports, and literary copies.
- RINAP inscriptions of Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal, and
  their Babylonian contexts.
- Esarhaddon's Babylon rebuilding inscriptions.

## Major discoveries or innovations

- Durable but changing civic and scholarly institutions across regime change.
- Multiple textual genres that can reconstruct catastrophe and rebuilding
  without accepting one royal theology.
- An unusually explicit destruction/rebuilding cycle with responsibility,
  surviving custody, and later political reuse available for comparison.

## Historical relationships

Babylonian cities, Chaldean and Aramaean groups, Assyria, Elam, and the Medes
alternately allied, fought, ruled, rebelled, traded, and exchanged specialists.
Shared scholarship did not imply political unity; Assyrian participation in
rebuilding does not erase Assyrian responsibility for destruction.

## House interpretation

Babylon answers Glaüshouse's question by showing that integrity after
catastrophe requires more than reconstruction. A living system must preserve
responsibility, people, records, services, missing evidence, and unresolved
harm while distinguishing continuity from health.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Babylonian chronicles, king lists, legal and administrative archives,
scholarly tablets and colophons, and royal destruction or rebuilding records.

### Archaeological evidence

Babylon and southern cities; architecture, damage and reconstruction phases,
tablets, seals, ceramics, canals, domestic contexts, temples, and material
provenance.

### Major modern scholarship

- Political histories of Babylonia under Assyrian domination.
- Editions and studies of Babylonian chronicles and RINAP inscriptions.
- Research on first-millennium scholarly, temple, family, and urban networks.

### Recommended House library placement

Glaüshouse Age II historical wing, adjacent to conservation, archival science,
longitudinal memory, disaster recovery, and scholarly-provenance collections.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

The collection gives historical cases for evolution, ecology, cognition,
feedback, preservation, and living-system repair studied by *On the Origin of
Species*, *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, and *The Tree of Knowledge*. Those
works remain modern interpretive companions, not ancient Babylonian texts.

## Research notes

- Keep Babylon, Babylonia, Chaldean groups, and Neo-Babylonian dynasty distinct.
- Keep destruction, royal rhetoric, archaeological damage, and later
  reconstruction as separate evidence layers.
- Do not backdate Nebuchadnezzar II's monumental city into this Point.
- Track whether each scholarly tablet is contemporary, copied, imported, or
  known chiefly from a later context.
