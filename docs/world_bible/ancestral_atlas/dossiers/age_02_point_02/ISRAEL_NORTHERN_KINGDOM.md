# Israel — Northern Kingdom

Civilization: Northern Kingdom of Israel  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point II, Stonebend — Israel  
Date relevance: c. 900–722 BCE; northern territory substantially annexed in 732 BCE  
Status: Reviewed dossier; terminal chronology and construction attribution need continuing audit; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

- The capital at Samaria, regional architecture and water systems, the
  Samaria ostraca, seals, ivories, ceramics, botanical and faunal remains.
- Moabite, Aramaic, and Assyrian monuments and texts that name Israelite
  rulers, the “House of Omri,” conflicts, tribute, annexation, and conquest.
- Biblical books preserving northern royal and prophetic traditions, read as
  later literary corpora rather than transparent contemporary annals.

### Scholarly interpretation

Chronology, royal attribution, administrative reconstruction, prophetic-text
formation, and the exact sequence of Samaria's fall remain debated. Agreement
between two hostile royal inscriptions is not neutral corroboration, and
archaeological style alone does not name a builder.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Stonebend inherits methods of multi-source concordance, attributable
administrative records, phased infrastructure, and honest terminal memory. It
does not inherit ancient kingship, territorial Claim, extraction, siege,
deportation, covenant as constitutional law, or a biblical identity test.

## Historical overview

Within this Point, `Israel` means the northern kingdom centered at Samaria,
not Judah, a united monarchy, or every use of Israel as a people-name. Omri's
dynasty established Samaria in the ninth century. The kingdom participated in
regional coalitions, fought neighbors, supported elite centers, and developed
administrative and military capacity visible in local and external records.

Assyrian pressure changed from distant campaigning and tribute to territorial
annexation. Tiglath-pileser III took northern territory in 732 BCE and left a
reduced kingdom under Hoshea. Samaria then fell near the supplied 722 BCE
boundary. Shalmaneser V, Sargon II, biblical narratives, later chronicles, and
archaeology preserve different parts of that terminal sequence.

## Chronology

- c. 900 BCE: the kingdom continues from its late tenth-century formation.
- c. 880s–840s: Omride dynasty; Samaria is established as the capital.
- 853 BCE: Ahab of Israel appears in the Assyrian account of Qarqar.
- c. 840s: Mesha's Moabite inscription contests Israelite control; the Tel Dan
  fragments belong near this regional conflict horizon under debated attribution.
- 841 BCE: Shalmaneser III's Black Obelisk depicts Jehu's tribute.
- early eighth century: Samaria ostraca and the reign of Jeroboam II belong to
  a period of renewed prosperity and administration.
- 738/737 BCE: Menahem pays tribute to Tiglath-pileser III.
- 732 BCE: northern territories are annexed; Pekah is replaced by Hoshea.
- c. 725–722 BCE: siege and conquest of Samaria under Shalmaneser V's terminal
  reign; Sargon II later claims deportation, reorganization, or renewed conquest.
- 722/720 BCE: event sequence and dating require continuing audit; the
  supplied Point ends at 722 BCE.

## Major figures

- Omri and Ahab: dynasty, capital, regional coalition, and external attestations.
- Jehu: dynastic rupture and tribute represented on the Black Obelisk.
- Joash and Jeroboam II: eighth-century recovery and administration.
- Menahem, Pekah, and Hoshea: rulers under escalating Assyrian pressure.
- Jezebel, Elijah, Elisha, Amos, and Hosea: major literary and religious-memory
  figures whose textual sources and editing histories remain explicit.
- Mesha of Moab, Hazael and Rezin of Damascus, Shalmaneser III,
  Tiglath-pileser III, Shalmaneser V, and Sargon II: neighboring or imperial
  actors and adversarial witnesses.

## Major cities

- Samaria: capital, palace complex, ostraca, ivories, and elite material culture.
- Jezreel: royal center with debated architectural relationship to Samaria.
- Megiddo and Hazor: administrative, fortified, and water-system evidence with
  contested dates and dynastic attribution.
- Dan and Bethel: political, cultic, and inscriptional importance.
- Tirzah, Dothan, Dor, and the Jezreel and Samaria regions: changing regional
  administration, agriculture, route access, and settlement histories.
- Kuntillet Ajrud: geographically outside the kingdom's core but relevant to
  connected inscriptional and religious evidence.

## Government

Dynastic kingship operated through royal households, officials, scribes,
regional centers, military forces, cult institutions, landholding, commodity
collection, building, and storage. The Samaria ostraca record deliveries,
names, places, commodities, and regnal dates. Whether they document taxation,
estate management, provisioning, gifts, or some combination remains debated;
the Atlas does not reconstruct a total fiscal constitution from them.

## Religion

Yahwistic traditions coexisted with household practices, regional cult
centers, royal policy, Baal and Asherah traditions, and prophetic critique.
Bethel and Dan are prominent in later literature. Kuntillet Ajrud and other
material evidence complicate any projection of later normative monotheism
backward. No single official or popular theology can be generalized across
the population and the whole period.

## Economy

Agriculture centered grain, olives, wine, livestock, and household production.
Palace and regional institutions collected and redistributed at least some
produce. Elite construction and luxury goods depended on unequal access to
land, labor, routes, and imported material. Administrative visibility does not
make every extraction legitimate or every resident equally represented.

## Trade

Coastal access and inland routes connected Israel to Phoenician cities,
Damascus, Moab, Judah, Egypt-linked networks, Assyria, and other southern
Levantine communities. Oil, wine, grain, horses, livestock, timber, metals,
ivory, pottery, textiles, and crafted luxuries moved through trade, tribute,
taxation, gift, booty, and household exchange that must remain distinct.

## Science

Agricultural observation, measurement, scribal notation, water management,
building practice, and route knowledge are materially visible. Later
prophetic and royal texts preserve environmental and agricultural metaphors,
but they are not direct technical manuals. No modern scientific discipline is
projected into the kingdom.

## Technology

Stone, ashlar, mud brick, plaster, gates, walls, storage facilities,
underground water systems, terracing where securely dated, roads, pottery,
metal tools and weapons, textiles, seals, weights, chariots, and horse
management supported urban and rural life. Construction phase, repair, reuse,
destruction, and modern attribution remain separate data fields.

## Architecture

Samaria's palace platform and elite buildings, Jezreel's enclosure, and major
works at Megiddo and Hazor demonstrate substantial planning and labor. Their
precise builders and relationship to Omride or earlier phases remain debated.
Stonebend therefore inherits phased attribution and public-works provenance,
not a copied palace, gate, “Solomonic” label, or monumentality as authority.

## Military

Israel fielded infantry, fortifications, and a notable chariot force. It joined
the Qarqar coalition, fought Damascus and Moab, and alternated resistance,
alliance, and tribute under Assyrian expansion. Siege, dynastic violence,
forced movement, imperial annexation, and royal victory claims are recorded as
failure and harm conditions, never as Stonebend Gifts.

## Art

Samaria ivories, fine wares, seals, inscriptions, cult objects, architecture,
and imported or Phoenician-made goods join local production to elite and
international networks. They cannot define one Israelite style or stand for
ordinary life. Hollow Grove translation uses visible maker, material, date,
patron, route, find context, and later interpretation rather than royal décor.

## Language

Hebrew and closely related Northwest Semitic language forms appear in ostraca,
seals, and inscriptions. Assyrian Akkadian, Moabite, Aramaic, and later Hebrew
literary witnesses provide external and retrospective perspectives. Language,
script, polity, religion, and ethnic identity are stored as related but
non-identical evidence.

## Major texts

- Samaria ostraca: palace-context administrative records, early eighth century.
- Mesha Stele: Moabite royal account of conflict with the House of Omri.
- Tel Dan fragments: Aramaic victory monument with damaged and debated readings.
- Kurkh Monolith and Black Obelisk: Assyrian representations of Ahab and Jehu.
- Tiglath-pileser III's inscriptions: annexation, deportation, and Hoshea's installation.
- Shalmaneser V, Sargon II, and Babylonian chronicle evidence for the terminal transition.
- Kings, Amos, and Hosea: later literary, prophetic, and editorial witnesses.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not a claim of unique invention. Its design value
lies in the survival of mutually checking source types: administrative ostraca,
large architecture, water works, neighboring royal monuments, Assyrian records,
and later critical literature. Together they make attribution and state memory
inspectable without forcing one source to control the others.

## Historical relationships

Israel's relationship with Damascus shifted between coalition and rivalry;
Moab contested its control; Phoenician cities supplied goods and dynastic or
commercial connections; Judah was a neighbor, rival, and occasional partner;
and Assyria moved from campaigning and tribute to annexation and conquest.
This Point records an interaction network, not four isolated lineages.

## House interpretation

Stonebend asks what gives civilization durable form. Israel contributes the
harder lesson that durable identity requires attributed records, phased works,
competing witnesses, and an honest account of institutional ending. The Law
of Identity and Claim / Title / Yield boundaries remain the only current
authority; ancestry explains design vocabulary but grants nothing.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Samaria ostraca; Mesha and Tel Dan monuments; Kurkh Monolith; Black Obelisk;
Tiglath-pileser III, Shalmaneser V, and Sargon II records; archaeology and
later Kings, Amos, and Hosea texts with genre and composition history attached.

### Archaeological evidence

Samaria, Jezreel, Megiddo, Hazor, Dan, Bethel, Tirzah, Dothan, regional survey,
water systems, fortifications, storage, pottery, seals, ivories, inscriptions,
botanical and faunal remains, and destruction or reuse phases.
