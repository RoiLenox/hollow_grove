# Phoenicia — Ninth and Eighth Centuries BCE

Civilization: Independent Phoenician city-states and connected settlements  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point II, Sandmanor — Phoenicia  
Date relevance: c. 900–722 BCE; cities and networks continue beyond the Point  
Status: Reviewed dossier; city, settlement, and transmitted-king-list chronologies need continuing audit; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

- City-specific inscriptions, settlements, harbors, sanctuaries, burials,
  workshops, ships, tools, raw materials, and mobile craft objects.
- Assyrian inscriptions, images, and correspondence recording tribute,
  diplomacy, monitoring, taxation, restriction, and intervention.
- Later king lists and foundation stories retained as later witnesses rather
  than substituted for missing contemporary archives.

### Scholarly interpretation

`Phoenicia` does not name one state. The chronology, political form, local
relationships, and population of early western settlements vary by site.
Traditional dates such as Carthage's 814/813 BCE foundation must be compared
with archaeology and later literary transmission rather than accepted as one
uncomplicated event.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Sandmanor receives city-specific diplomacy, accountable network identity,
settlement provenance, script transmission, and named cultural intermediaries.
It does not inherit colonial entitlement, tribute as consent, merchant
innocence, a unified Phoenician identity, or literal port-city replicas.

## Historical overview

Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad, and neighboring centers maintained distinct
political institutions while sharing related language, cult, craft, and
maritime practices. During c. 900–722 BCE their connections in Cyprus and the
central and western Mediterranean became increasingly permanent. Each
foundation or settlement involved its origin city, destination communities,
local resources, migration, exchange, competition, and sometimes domination.

Assyrian expansion intensified relationships with the coast. Some cities paid
tribute, obtained access or protection, resisted, or were monitored and taxed.
Tyre could benefit relative to competitors while accepting unequal Assyrian
interference. No one diplomatic posture characterizes all cities or reigns.

## Chronology

- early ninth century: Tyre and Sidon remain major independent coastal powers;
  transmitted Tyrian king lists require source-specific handling.
- 870s BCE: Ashurnasirpal II reaches the Mediterranean and records coastal tribute.
- 858–824 BCE: Shalmaneser III repeatedly campaigns west; Balawat and other
  records depict Tyrian tribute or gifts.
- ninth century: Kition develops as an important Tyrian-linked center in Cyprus;
  permanent Phoenician settlements emerge unevenly farther west.
- traditional 814/813 BCE: Carthage's foundation date in later transmission;
  archaeological formation is a separate question.
- 744–727 BCE: Tiglath-pileser III's administration monitors coastal rulers,
  commerce, timber, tribute, and access.
- c. 734–732 BCE: Hiram of Tyre appears among rulers under Assyrian pressure.
- 722 BCE: the Point ends with Samaria, not with a Phoenician terminal event.

## Major figures

- Ithobaal I, Mattan I, Pygmalion, and Elissa/Dido: important in transmitted
  Tyrian royal and foundation histories whose sources and dates remain explicit.
- Baal-Eser and Hiram of Tyre: rulers appearing in Assyrian interaction records.
- Local rulers, priests, merchants, craftspeople, sailors, settlers, and
  destination communities are historically essential even when unnamed.
- Ashurnasirpal II, Shalmaneser III, Adad-nirari III, and Tiglath-pileser III:
  imperial actors and biased external witnesses.

## Major cities

- Tyre: island and mainland center, maritime network, Assyrian diplomacy, and
  major role in Cypriot and western settlement.
- Sidon: independent port, craft and exchange center, and competitor or partner.
- Byblos: long-lived city with royal inscriptions and older Egyptian connections.
- Arwad, Sarepta, and Beirut: distinct coastal communities and production sites.
- Kition and other Cypriot centers: local kingdoms and Phoenician-linked settlement.
- Carthage and settlements in North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, and Iberia:
  locally developing communities whose early chronology and relationships vary.

## Government

Each city possessed its own king and locally specific elite, council, temple,
household, merchant, craft, harbor, and dependent-settlement institutions where
evidence permits. Related script and language do not establish a federation.
Assyrian vassalage, tribute, resident oversight, or preferential access could
alter decision-making without making a city an ordinary Assyrian province.

## Religion

Melqart, Astarte, Eshmun, Baalat Gebal, Baal, and other local divine forms
appear in city-specific cults, names, sanctuaries, votives, funerary texts, and
later accounts. Deity, title, city, and period must remain attached. Sandmanor
does not receive one Phoenician theology or a reconstructed ancient ritual.

## Economy

Maritime and inland economies joined agriculture, timber, fishing, ship work,
murex dye, textiles, metal, ivory, glass, pottery, building, household labor,
and settlement. Wealth and mobility were unequal. Tribute and Assyrian market
access could enrich some actors while constraining others.

## Trade

Cedar and other timber, purple-dyed cloth, wine, oil, grain, copper and other
metals, ivory, glass, pottery, jewelry, crafted luxuries, people, and information
moved among the Levant, Cyprus, Egypt, Anatolia, the Aegean, North Africa,
Sicily, Sardinia, and Iberia. Trade, tribute, taxation, diplomatic gift,
settlement provisioning, piracy, and seizure are separate relationships.

## Science

Navigation, seasonal observation, route memory, weights, material knowledge,
dye practice, ship and timber selection, accounting, and multilingual literacy
were cumulative technical traditions. The dossier does not attribute modern
navigation science or a uniquely Phoenician invention without evidence.

## Technology

Seagoing ships, harbor and anchorage use, woodworking, joinery, stone building,
murex processing, textile production, metal and ivory work, glass, pottery,
weights, seals, and alphabetic inscriptions supported connected city economies.
Evidence quality differs sharply by coast, island, settlement, and century.

## Architecture

Island-city edges, harbors, walls, temples, workshops, houses, tombs, quays,
and overseas settlement plans belong to different local environments. Modern
Sandmanor may inherit several entries, layered thresholds, relationship maps,
and visibly attributed materials—not a cedar palace, ship-shaped building, or
generic Mediterranean bazaar.

## Military

Fortifications, ships, tribute, vassalage, competition, local defense, and
Assyrian campaigning shaped the coast. Assyria later drew heavily on
Phoenician ships, but cooperation under imperial pressure is not proof of
equal alliance. Colonization, enslavement, piracy, and imperial violence remain
historical problems rather than Sandmanor Gifts.

## Art

Ivories, metal bowls, jewelry, sculpture, textiles, seals, pottery, architecture,
and votive or funerary objects combine local and mobile forms. Each object
requires maker or workshop where known, material source, patron, route,
adaptation, audience, findspot, and modern custody. “Phoenicianizing” style is
an analytical label, not permission to erase intermediaries.

## Language

Phoenician was a Northwest Semitic language recorded in a consonantal
alphabetic script. Local forms and inscriptional habits varied and interacted
with Aramaic, Hebrew, Greek, Cypriot, Egyptian, and Assyrian language systems.
The script was inherited and adapted from earlier alphabetic practices and
transmitted through wider networks; it was not invented from nothing.

## Major texts

- Royal, votive, funerary, and dedicatory inscriptions from individual cities.
- Cypriot and western inscriptions, including early records whose dates remain audited.
- Assyrian annals naming rulers and tribute.
- Balawat gate bands showing Tyrian diplomatic or tributary presentation.
- Tiglath-pileser III-era letters and inscriptions concerning Tyre, Sidon,
  coastal trade, timber, taxation, and monitoring.
- Later Tyrian king lists and Elissa/Dido traditions, retained at their dates.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The concrete inheritance is not a claim that Phoenicians invented the alphabet
or maritime exchange. It is the accountable linking of economical alphabetic
inscription, city-specific identity, seaborne settlement, craft mediation, and
cross-cultural transmission across a network with visible power differences.

## Historical relationships

Phoenician cities competed and collaborated with one another; interacted with
Israel, Aramaean and Anatolian polities, Egypt, Cyprus, Greek-speaking
communities, and destination populations; and negotiated increasingly close
relations with Assyria. Tyre's policy and advantages did not represent every
city, and connected settlements developed identities beyond their origin city.

## House interpretation

Sandmanor asks how people recognize, relate to, and transform one another.
Phoenician history contributes the discipline of keeping relationship visible:
who moved, who received, who adapted, who benefited, who was constrained, and
how local identity changed. Existing Law of Pattern, Court, and Restitution
boundaries alone authorize present action.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

City inscriptions; Cypriot and western inscriptions; Assyrian annals, images,
and correspondence; harbor, settlement, workshop, sanctuary, burial, ship,
and object evidence; later king lists and foundation stories with date labels.

### Archaeological evidence

Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad, Sarepta, Beirut, Kition, Cyprus, Carthage, and
selected central and western Mediterranean settlements; ships, anchors,
harbors, timber, murex processing, workshops, inscriptions, ceramics, metals,
ivories, glass, sanctuaries, houses, and tombs.
