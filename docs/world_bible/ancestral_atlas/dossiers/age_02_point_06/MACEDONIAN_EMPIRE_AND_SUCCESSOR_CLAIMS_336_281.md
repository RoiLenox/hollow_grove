# Macedonian Empire and Successor Claims, 336–281 BCE

Civilization: Macedonia, Alexander's empire, and Macedonian successor claims  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point VI / Sandmanor — Macedonia  
Status: Reviewed transforming-referent dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working  
Temporal fit: Full horizon / Referent transforms

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Babylonian chronicles and diaries, inscriptions, decrees, coins, papyri,
archaeology, and later Greek and Roman narratives record different regions and
audiences. Much detailed narrative is late, elite, and ruler-centered.

### Scholarly interpretation

`Macedonia` changes from homeland kingdom and conquering court to a claimed
imperial succession and then rival kingdoms. Succession legitimacy, local
responses, royal-title dates, settlement foundations, and individual motives
remain source-dependent.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Sandmanor receives audience-specific identity translation, contested-succession
records, coalition change, and place-name provenance. Conquest, charisma,
coerced alliance, dynastic violence, and settler entitlement are not Gifts.

## Historical overview

Alexander inherited Macedonia in 336 and led campaigns from 334 until his death
in 323. Philip III and Alexander IV then served as nominal joint kings under
rival regents until their deaths. Dynasts repeatedly repartitioned territories,
formed and broke coalitions, adopted royal titles from 306/305, and fought
through Ipsus in 301 and Corupedium in 281. Macedonian identity became a
homeland, court, army, settler, and legitimating claim across many local worlds.

## Chronology

- 336 BCE: Alexander III succeeds Philip II.
- 334–330 BCE: conquest dismantles Achaemenid kingship through staggered campaigns.
- 330–323 BCE: eastern campaigns, return, and improvised imperial administration.
- 323 BCE: Alexander dies; Philip III and the future Alexander IV become joint kings.
- 321 BCE: Triparadeisus reallocates commands after Perdiccas's death.
- 317–310/305 BCE: royal family members are killed; nominal Argead rule ends.
- 306/305 BCE: leading successors adopt royal titles.
- 301 BCE: Ipsus defeats and kills Antigonus I; territories are repartitioned.
- 281 BCE: Corupedium ends Lysimachus's rule; Seleucus is killed later that year.

## Major figures

Alexander III, Olympias, Philip III, Eurydice, Roxane, Alexander IV,
Antipater, Perdiccas, Craterus, Eumenes, Polyperchon, Cassander, Antigonus I,
Demetrius I, Lysimachus, Ptolemy I, and Seleucus I belong to changing claims.
Interpreters, envoys, local rulers, soldiers, settlers, and civic communities
prevent the dossier from becoming dynastic biography alone.

## Major cities

Pella and Aigai, Greek cities, Alexandria, Babylon, Susa, cities and regions of
Asia Minor, the Levant, Iran, Central Asia, and the Indus world, plus the
settlement landscapes of Gaza, Ipsus, and Corupedium. Every royal foundation
or renaming has a prior local geography.

## Government

Kingship, court, army constituencies, regencies, nominal joint kings,
partition conferences, satrapies, local rulers, cities, leagues, garrisons,
and independent successor courts distributed authority. A conference grant
did not guarantee local acceptance or operational control.

## Religion

Macedonian and Greek cults, Egyptian royal forms, Babylonian temple recognition,
Iranian court practices, local religions, heroic ancestry, and emerging dynastic
cult served different audiences. Adaptation does not prove common belief.

## Economy

Captured treasuries, taxation, agriculture, ports, mines, mints, cities,
military pay, requisition, plunder, and local exchange sustained war and courts.
Royal wealth remains inseparable from extraction and disrupted communities.

## Trade

Grain, timber, animals, metals, coin, textiles, ships, texts, luxury goods, and
people moved through reconfigured routes. Trade, tax, tribute, royal gift,
requisition, confiscation, enslavement, and settlement are distinct movements.

## Science

Geography, surveying, medicine, natural inquiry, navigation, multilingual
interpretation, and local expertise traveled with armies and courts. Ruler-led
accounts often obscure coerced or unnamed knowledge holders.

## Technology

Field and siege systems, ships, ports, roads, bridges, camps, treasuries,
mints, and settlements depended on local infrastructure, supply, labor, and
maintenance. Capacity claims need phase and civilian-cost records.

## Architecture

Royal, civic, military, funerary, and settlement forms varied by region.
Hollow Grove inherits prior-place and audience provenance—not a Hellenistic
palace, phalanx-shaped plan, or Alexandrian façade.

## Military

Conquest and successor wars used battle, siege, garrison, coalition, forced
movement, recruitment, and plunder. The Lamian War, regency conflicts, Gaza,
Ipsus, and Corupedium are relational rupture records, not Sandmanor spectacle.

## Art

Portraits, coins, royal emblems, decrees, funerary works, architecture, and
Alexander imagery were made and reused for different audiences. Each reuse
requires speaker, patron, date, audience, and contested reception.

## Language

Greek and Macedonian court usage operated among many local languages and
scripts. Translation was performed by specific intermediaries under unequal
conditions; it was not a frictionless spread of one culture.

## Major texts

Babylonian chronicles and diaries, decrees, treaties, coins, inscriptions,
papyrus documents, and later Arrian, Diodorus, Curtius, Plutarch, and Justin
must retain genre and source distance.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is a provenance method for public identity under
rapid relationship change: compare audience-specific claims, record contested
succession, map coalition revisions, and preserve the prior identity of renamed places.

## Historical relationships

Macedonian courts and armies interacted with Greek, Egyptian, Babylonian,
Iranian, Central and South Asian, Phoenician, Cypriot, Thracian, and many local
communities through translation, negotiation, marriage, settlement, coercion,
resistance, alliance, and war. `Fusion` cannot summarize these unequal relations.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Babylonian chronicles, inscriptions, decrees, coins, papyri, archaeology, and
later narratives belong in parallel collections organized by date, audience, and genre.

### Archaeological evidence

Royal centers, cities, renamed settlements, ports, forts, battle landscapes,
coins, burials, and local material continuities require prior-place provenance.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize source-critical studies of Alexander, the succession, monarchy,
local communities, settlement, identity, translation, and successor chronology.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Sandmanor's Age II wing beside diplomacy, translation,
succession, public performance, place memory, and coercion-aware design.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*Poetics* may support formal analysis of representation. Jung, Campbell, and
Goffman offer later comparative lenses, not evidence for royal intention,
audience response, succession legitimacy, or local identity.

## Research notes

Further work should deepen local and non-elite evidence, distinguish homeland
Macedonia from empire and successor courts, and audit every foundation and
renaming claim. Antigonid stabilization belongs chiefly after 281.
