# Achaemenid End and Institutional Continuity, 336–281 BCE

Civilization: Late Achaemenid Empire and attributed institutional legacy  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point VI / Stonebend — Achaemenid  
Status: Reviewed terminal-and-legacy dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working and Needs date refinement  
Temporal fit: Political 336–330 / Institutional legacy thereafter

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Darius III's reign and the campaigns of 334–330 are known mainly through later
Greek and Roman narratives. Babylonian astronomical and king-list evidence,
archaeology, coins, Iranian regional evidence, and reconstructed administrative
continuities provide essential but uneven controls. No Persian royal inscription
narrates the final reign.

### Scholarly interpretation

Dynastic end, territorial conquest, regional resistance, institutional survival,
Alexander's succession claims, and later successor reuse are distinct problems.
Retention of a governor or office does not prove unchanged authority, public
consent, or a seamless Achaemenid-to-Hellenistic transition.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Stonebend receives separated terminal chronology, authority-handoff records,
and changed-sovereign institutional provenance. Conquest, appropriation,
execution, destruction, extraction, and succession by force are never Gifts or Title.

## Historical overview

Darius III acceded in 336 BCE and remained king until his death in 330.
Alexander crossed into Asia in 334; victories and sieges brought western regions,
Egypt, Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis under his control, while Darius and later
eastern claimants and communities continued resistance. Alexander and his
successors retained, replaced, and adapted regional personnel, routes, records,
and institutions. After 330, this dossier uses `Achaemenid` only for sourced
institutional inheritance and memory, not a surviving polity.

## Chronology

- 336 BCE: Darius III accedes; Philip II dies and Alexander III succeeds.
- 334 BCE: Alexander enters Asia; battle of Granicus.
- 333 BCE: battle of Issus.
- 332 BCE: sieges of Tyre and Gaza; Alexander enters Egypt.
- 331 BCE: Gaugamela; Babylon and Susa pass under Alexander's control.
- 330 BCE: Persian Gates resistance, Persepolis occupation and destruction,
  Darius III's death, and Bessus's royal counterclaim.
- 330–327 BCE: continuing eastern resistance and changing use of Iranian personnel.
- 323–281 BCE: successor regimes reuse and repartition parts of the inherited system.

## Major figures

Darius III, Stateira, Sisygambis, Memnon, Mazaeus, Ariobarzanes,
Bessus/Artaxerxes V, Spitamenes, Alexander III, Parmenion, and regional
officials, soldiers, scribes, temple personnel, and communities appear under
different source conditions. Later biography must not erase non-royal agency.

## Major cities

Babylon, Susa, Persepolis, Pasargadae, Ecbatana, Bactrian and Sogdian centers,
regional roads, and the battle and siege landscapes of Granicus, Issus, Tyre,
Gaza, Gaugamela, and the Persian Gates mark different kinds of transition.

## Government

The royal court, regional governors, military commanders, garrisons, cities,
temples, local dynasts, scribes, and fiscal systems continued unevenly during
war. Alexander sometimes retained and sometimes replaced personnel while
changing sovereign and military authority. Successor partitions changed the
framework again.

## Religion

Earlier Achaemenid royal traditions, local cults, Babylonian and Egyptian
temple relationships, and Alexander's claims of vengeance and succession
intersect. Later language of fusion or legitimate succession cannot substitute
for evidence of how local communities understood the change.

## Economy

Royal treasuries, taxes, estates, temples, cities, households, markets, and
military supply continued under capture, requisition, reassignment, or loss.
Seized treasure and continued tax collection document capacity and coercion,
not neutral administrative survival.

## Trade

Road, river, canal, port, relay, and market networks moved food, animals,
metals, textiles, coin, documents, and people. Trade, ration, tax, tribute,
requisition, plunder, and forced movement retain separate provenance.

## Science

Babylonian astronomy, Egyptian and regional medicine and surveying,
multilingual accounting, local law, and route knowledge survived through
people and institutions. Each continuity claim needs a named custodian,
language, office, date, changed authority, and evidence of loss.

## Technology

Roads, relays, bridges, canals, fortifications, ports, ships, siege systems,
treasuries, tablets, papyri, seals, weights, and coins were captured, damaged,
maintained, or reassigned. Reuse does not mean unchanged service or benign conquest.

## Architecture

Palaces, terraces, tombs, roads, city and temple fabric, and regional buildings
experienced occupation, reuse, destruction, repair, and new patronage.
Persepolis remains a destruction and appropriation record—not a decorative motif.

## Military

Granicus, Issus, Tyre, Gaza, Gaugamela, the Persian Gates, Darius's pursuit and
death, Bessus's claim, and Bactrian-Sogdian resistance establish staggered
terminal thresholds. Battle outcome, capital loss, dynastic end, and local
resistance are never collapsed into one event.

## Art

Late Achaemenid relief, seals, coinage, court forms, architecture, and local
material traditions were destroyed, appropriated, retained, or recombined.
Every later use needs maker, audience, phase, prior context, and power relation.

## Language

Elamite, Babylonian, Aramaic, Egyptian, Old Persian, Greek, and regional
languages had different institutional lives. The final reign's Greek narrative
imbalance and the absence of a Persian royal account remain visible metadata.

## Major texts

Babylonian astronomical diaries and king lists, late administrative evidence,
coins and inscriptions belong beside Arrian, Diodorus, Curtius, Plutarch, and
Justin. Later narrative is evidence with source distance, not eyewitness fact by default.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is a method for recording institutional survival
without pretending that sovereignty survived: separate terminal states,
explicit authority handoffs, and changed attribution for every continuing service.

## Historical relationships

Regional authorities and communities fought, negotiated, defected, resisted,
or served under unequal conditions. Alexander and later successors inherited
parts of an administrative landscape while changing its rulers and coercive systems.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Babylonian diaries and king lists, coins, archaeology, administrative evidence,
and later classical narratives belong in parallel, source-distance-marked collections.

### Archaeological evidence

Persepolis, Pasargadae, Susa, Babylon, Ecbatana, regional routes, destruction
layers, reused institutions, coins, and settlement evidence require phase and custody metadata.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize source-critical histories of Darius III, regional resistance,
Achaemenid administration, Alexander's conquest, local continuity, and the successors.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Stonebend's Age II historical wing beside institutional
continuity, public records, conquest transition, jurisdiction, and provenance studies.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

The foundational philosophical shelf may frame questions about polity, identity,
change, and duty. It cannot make conquest lawful, recover a lost Persian voice,
or convert institutional continuity into constitutional legitimacy.

## Research notes

Further review should identify region-specific office continuities, distinguish
Alexander's claims from implementation, deepen Babylonian and Iranian evidence,
and map which successor allocations retained late Achaemenid structures.
