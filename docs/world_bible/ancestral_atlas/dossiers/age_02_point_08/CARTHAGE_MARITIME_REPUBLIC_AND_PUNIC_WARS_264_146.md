## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Treaties, coins, harbors, ship facilities, inscriptions, Punic and Neo-Punic
evidence, agriculture and settlement archaeology, Greek and Roman narratives,
and North African material remains document different parts of Carthage. Roman
sources are substantial but adversarial and retrospective; one site cannot
represent all Punic society.

### Scholarly interpretation

Carthage was a republic and maritime power with civil and military institutions,
agricultural hinterlands, ports, fleets, commercial networks, colonies, allied
communities, and subject territories. Its internal constitution, command
relationships, and social structure remain partly reconstructed from outside
testimony.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Flynt inherits maritime logistics, port and hinterland provenance, civil-military
accountability, and defeat-consequence audit. It does not inherit colonization,
slavery, war, naval reach, or defeat as Law of Capacity.

## Historical overview

Carthage dominated important western Mediterranean routes before and during the
Punic Wars. The First War centered on Sicily, the Second on Italy and Spain,
and the Third ended with Carthage's destruction in 146 BCE. Carthaginian
resilience and defeat emerged from political decisions, command structures,
allies, manpower, fleets, resources, and Roman persistence—not a simple naval
stereotype or Roman moral narrative.

## Chronology

- 264–241 BCE: First Punic War; Sicily and naval war transform the balance.
- 241–237 BCE: Mercenary War and Rome's seizure of Sardinia and Corsica.
- 237–218 BCE: Barcid expansion in Iberia; treaties and contested spheres with Rome.
- 218–201 BCE: Second Punic War; Hannibal's invasion of Italy and Roman recovery.
- 201–149 BCE: Carthage rebuilds under treaty restrictions while Numidian conflict and Roman suspicion intensify.
- 149–146 BCE: Third Punic War.
- 146 BCE: Carthage is destroyed; Punic statehood ends, though Punic communities and material culture continue in other forms.

## Major figures

Hannibal Barca, Hamilcar Barca, Hasdrubal, Mago, Carthaginian sufetes,
councils, generals, merchants, farmers, sailors, allied and subject communities,
mercenaries, Numidian rulers, and Roman commanders.

## Major cities

Carthage, Byrsa, its harbors, North African agricultural districts, Sicily,
Sardinia, Corsica, Iberia, Malta, coastal Libya, and western Mediterranean
routes. Metropolitan, colonial, allied, and subject status require separate records.

## Government

Sufetes, councils, citizen bodies, generals, merchant and landed interests,
allied communities, subject territories, and military commands shaped policy.
Civil and military authority were not identical; command accountability and the
role of elite families remain historically contested.

## Religion

Punic religious traditions, North African practices, Greek and Sicilian contacts,
civic memory, household ritual, and military oaths coexisted. Later Roman
moralizing cannot stand in for Carthaginian belief or make the tophet a total portrait.

## Economy

Agricultural estates, grain, olive products, ports, markets, metals, tribute,
tax, labor, enslaved people, coin, and military finance connected city and
hinterland. Commercial capacity depended on unequal labor and political relationships.

## Trade

Grain, metals, timber, wine, ceramics, slaves, coin, tribute, ships, agricultural
products, and information moved through ports, estates, colonies, allies, and
western Mediterranean routes.

## Science

Navigation, agriculture, harbor management, finance, diplomacy, siege, shipbuilding,
languages, and route intelligence supported Carthage's networks. Evidence is
uneven and often mediated by outsiders.

## Technology

Harbors, shipyards, ships, roads, storage, estates, fortifications, weapons,
coinage, and port infrastructure connected Carthage to its hinterlands and
overseas partners. Labor, extraction, maintenance, and local relationships remain explicit.

## Architecture

Harbors, walls, ship facilities, roads, estates, sanctuaries, houses, storage,
and civic spaces organized maritime and agricultural life. Hollow Grove
translates logistics and maintenance logic rather than recreating Byrsa or a
Punic harbor.

## Military

The First, Second, and Third Punic Wars, Mercenary War, Sicilian and Iberian
campaigns, naval operations, siege, allied mobilization, enslavement,
displacement, and Roman destruction shaped the polity. Strategic sophistication
and defeat are both bounded historical conditions, not Gifts of superiority.

## Art

Punic stelae, coins, jewelry, ceramics, architecture, harbor works, funerary
material, and connected Mediterranean forms express local and networked identities.
Maker, patron, site, date, audience, and later reception remain attached.

## Language

Punic and related Phoenician scripts, Greek, Latin, and local North African
languages occupied different commercial, religious, civic, and administrative
settings. Surviving language evidence is not socially complete.

## Major texts

Treaties, inscriptions, coin legends, Punic and Neo-Punic remains, Greek and
Roman narratives, agricultural and settlement archaeology, and harbor evidence
form a plural corpus. The Roman narrative requires explicit source-interest labels.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The design inheritance is a logistics-and-defeat audit that keeps harbor,
vessel, cargo, crew, supply, command, allied status, civilian harm, defeat
terms, and residual capacity visible instead of reducing Carthage to naval power.

## Historical relationships

Carthage interacted with Rome, Greek Sicily, Syracuse, Iberian communities,
Numidian kingdoms, Sardinia, Corsica, North African peoples, Ptolemaic Egypt,
and Hellenistic powers through treaty, commerce, colonization, alliance, war,
and coercion.
