## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Inscriptions, treaties, colonies, roads, milestones, coins, laws, archaeology,
Polybius, Livy, and later literary witnesses expose different institutions and
periods. Republican narrative is often later and elite-centered; citizenship,
allied status, provincial life, enslaved people, and local voices are unevenly
visible.

### Scholarly interpretation

Roman expansion was neither one constitutional plan nor one uniform citizenship
policy. Senate, magistracies, assemblies, allied communities, colonies,
commanders, and provincial arrangements changed as Rome acquired overseas
territories. Roads and institutions can be durable while their labor and
coercive context remain morally and historically consequential.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Stonebend inherits treaty-and-status provenance, public-works records, and
command and fiscal accountability. It does not inherit conquest, slavery,
provincial extraction, triumph, or republican procedure as automatic legitimacy.

## Historical overview

Rome entered the First Punic War as an Italian power and by 146 BCE had fought
across Sicily, Africa, Spain, Greece, and the Aegean, acquiring overseas
provinces and Mediterranean supremacy. The expansion rested on alliances,
citizenship gradations, colonies, magistracies, roads, ports, legions, naval
adaptation, indemnities, taxation, and local negotiation as well as force.

## Chronology

- 264–241 BCE: First Punic War; Sicily becomes Rome's first overseas province.
- 238–237 BCE: Rome seizes Sardinia and Corsica amid Carthaginian weakness.
- 218–201 BCE: Second Punic War; Hannibal's invasion, Italian alliance crisis, and Roman recovery.
- 215–205 BCE: First Macedonian War intersects with the wider conflict.
- 201–190s BCE: Roman intervention expands in Spain, Greece, and the eastern Mediterranean.
- 192–188 BCE: War with Antiochus III; Apamea limits Seleucid power.
- 171–168 BCE: Third Macedonian War; Perseus is defeated at Pydna.
- 149–146 BCE: Third Punic War and Achaean War.
- 146 BCE: Carthage and Corinth are destroyed; Roman Mediterranean supremacy is consolidated.

## Major figures

Fabius Maximus, Scipio Africanus, Cato the Elder, Scipio Aemilianus,
Flamininus, Aemilius Paullus, Roman magistrates and senators, assembly voters,
Italian allies, provincial soldiers, engineers, contractors, enslaved people,
and subject communities belong to distinct evidence settings.

## Major cities

Rome, Capua, Syracuse, Lilybaeum, Carthage, Corinth, Athens, Tarraco,
Saguntum, Italian colonies, Sicilian and Sardinian settlements, and Roman
military roads and ports. Municipal, allied, colonial, provincial, and military
status must remain separate.

## Government

Consuls, praetors, Senate, assemblies, tribunes, magistrates, allied treaties,
colonies, provincial commanders, taxation, indemnities, and military commands
changed as Rome expanded. Citizenship was graded; provincial administration and
public finance were not one uniform system.

## Religion

Roman civic religion, mos maiorum, triumph, household cult, allied ritual,
Greek philosophical influence, and provincial religious practice coexisted.
Triumph and freedom language are political performances, not proof of justice
or consent.

## Economy

Agriculture, land, mines, indemnities, taxation, contracts, military pay,
enslavement, ports, markets, and public finance supported expansion. Benefit and
burden were distributed unevenly across citizens, allies, provinces, and slaves.

## Trade

Grain, slaves, metals, timber, wine, oil, ceramics, coin, tax, indemnity,
military supply, and information moved through Italian, Sicilian, Iberian,
African, Greek, and eastern Mediterranean routes.

## Science

Surveying, bridge and road engineering, logistics, naval adaptation, medicine,
accounting, geography, rhetoric, and law depended on soldiers, contractors,
allied communities, and local specialists.

## Technology

Roads, bridges, colonies, ports, camps, siege works, ships, weapons, surveying
tools, milestones, coins, and records linked infrastructure to military and
fiscal power. Labor, extraction, repair, and local custody remain visible.

## Architecture

Roads, bridges, walls, camps, colonies, ports, temples, forums, and civic works
organized movement and authority. Hollow Grove translates their provenance and
maintenance logic rather than copying a Roman forum, legionary camp, or triumphal arch.

## Military

The Punic Wars, Macedonian and Seleucid wars, Spanish campaigns, siege, naval
adaptation, allied manpower, enslavement, displacement, and provincial
extraction transformed the Mediterranean. Organizational success is not a
permanent Gift of virtue or capacity.

## Art

Triumphs, monuments, coins, inscriptions, temples, portraiture, funerary works,
and imported Greek art expressed civic and elite identity. Roman appropriation
and later narrative must not erase makers, patrons, or conquered communities.

## Language

Latin and Greek operated alongside local languages and administrative practices.
Legal, military, civic, and provincial records require language, audience,
translator, forum, and custody metadata.

## Major texts

Polybius, treaties, inscriptions, laws, coin legends, roads and milestones,
Livy, later Roman histories, and archaeological evidence form a layered corpus.
Livy and other later narratives must be read with contemporary material evidence.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The design inheritance is a public provenance system for incorporation: treaty,
status, road, colony, province, command, finance, labor, and maintenance remain
linked without making incorporation equal consent.

## Historical relationships

Rome interacted with Carthage, Greek cities and leagues, Macedonia, Seleucid
Syria, Ptolemaic Egypt, Iberian and North African communities, and Italian allies
through treaty, war, arbitration, colonization, commerce, patronage, and coercion.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Place inscriptions, treaties, laws, roads, milestones, coinage, Polybius,
Livy, and archaeological reports in phase- and status-specific collections.

### Archaeological evidence

Link Rome, Italy, Sicily, Sardinia, Spain, North Africa, Carthage, Corinth,
colonies, ports, roads, camps, and battle landscapes to excavation, dating,
conservation, labor, and later reuse metadata.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize Roman institutions, citizenship and Italian allies, provincial
formation, the Punic Wars, Roman logistics, roads, slavery, imperialism, and
the 146 settlements.
