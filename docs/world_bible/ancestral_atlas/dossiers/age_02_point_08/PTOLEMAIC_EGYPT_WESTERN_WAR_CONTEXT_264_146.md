## Historical overview

Ptolemaic Egypt remained a major eastern Mediterranean state during the Punic
Wars and Roman rise. Its Nile agriculture, ports, dockyards, temples, fiscal
records, military settlements, and overseas possessions were connected to a
larger Hellenistic system. The Point VII record remains primary for Ptolemaic
political chronology; this nested coordinate records contemporaneity and
western-war context only.

## Chronology

- 264–241 BCE: First Punic War overlaps Ptolemy II's reign and Ptolemaic naval and Aegean competition.
- 246–221 BCE: Ptolemy III and the Third Syrian War connect Egypt to wider Hellenistic conflict.
- 221–205 BCE: Ptolemy IV; Raphia and later Upper Egyptian revolt reshape service and military systems.
- c. 205–186 BCE: Great Revolt and rival authority in Upper Egypt; local continuity varies.
- 204–180 BCE: Ptolemy V and priestly decrees amid fiscal and dynastic negotiation.
- 180–145 BCE: Ptolemy VI and changing co-rule; Roman intervention in Egyptian affairs in 168.
- 146 BCE: western branch endpoint; Ptolemaic rule continues.

## Major figures

Ptolemy II, Ptolemy III, Ptolemy IV, Ptolemy V, Ptolemy VI, Arsinoe II,
Egyptian priests, Greek and Demotic scribes, military settlers, petitioners,
sailors, farmers, merchants, and communities in Egypt, Cyprus, Cyrene, and the Aegean.

## Major cities

Alexandria, Memphis, the Fayum, Delta ports, Cyprus, Cyrene, Nile temples,
Elephantine, Philae, and eastern Mediterranean naval stations. Court, temple,
village, port, and overseas possession remain distinct.

## Government

Court, strategoi, local officials, temples, banks, tax systems, cleruchs,
cities, villages, and military commands interacted across regions. War and
dynastic conflict changed services without producing one simple state model.

## Religion

Egyptian temple practice, dynastic cult, Greek and Macedonian ritual, local cults,
and royal representation served different audiences. Trilingual decrees and
temple honors are situated agreements, not neutral proof of universal loyalty.

## Economy

Agriculture, canals, rents, taxation, temples, banks, markets, military
settlement, households, and royal finance interacted. War, naval building, and
fiscal extraction imposed unequal burdens.

## Trade

Grain, papyrus, textiles, oil, wine, metals, timber, coin, taxes, port duties,
ships, labor, and information connected Nile, Red Sea, Aegean, Cyrene, Cyprus,
and eastern Mediterranean routes.

## Science

Surveying, accounting, medicine, astronomy, geography, translation, temple
scholarship, papyrology, and naval logistics operated through several institutions.

## Technology

Canals, irrigation, Nile and sea transport, ports, dockyards, ships, papyrus,
mints, storage, temples, and military supply connected Egypt to war and trade.
Tax, requisition, labor, and maintenance remain distinguishable.

## Architecture

Temples, ports, storage, administrative spaces, houses, and waterworks
accumulated across phases. Hollow Grove translates service, custody, and
interruption histories rather than reproducing a temple or Alexandrian palace.

## Military

Syrian Wars, Raphia's aftermath, Cyprus and Cyrene, naval competition, dynastic
conflict, the Great Revolt, and Roman intervention affected Egypt while
Ptolemaic rule continued. Military reach is not Integrity.

## Art

Temples, decrees, statuary, coins, papyri, royal portraits, and Egyptian and
Greek forms conveyed dynastic and local relationships. Makers, patrons, place,
language, and audience remain attached.

## Language

Greek, Demotic, hieroglyphic, hieratic, and community languages occupied
different legal, administrative, religious, and everyday settings. Each record
requires language, writer, forum, translation, custody, and survival metadata.

## Major texts

Zenon and other papyri, priestly decrees, Greek and Demotic petitions, temple
inscriptions, accounts, coins, Polybius, and archaeology. Point VII's source
collection remains the primary historical record.
