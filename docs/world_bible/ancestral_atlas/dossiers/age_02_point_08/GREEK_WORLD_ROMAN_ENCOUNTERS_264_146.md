# Greek World and Roman Encounters, 264–146 BCE

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Federal decrees, treaties, arbitration, honorific inscriptions, hostage and
citizenship evidence, sanctuaries, schools, theaters, Polybius, Livy, later
Plutarch, philosophical and theatrical texts, coins, and destruction archaeology
document different communities unevenly.

### Scholarly interpretation

`Greek World` is a deliberately broad nested label. It is not a state, ethnicity,
uniform culture, or Athens-centered identity. Roman declarations of Greek
freedom could coexist with pressure, garrisons, hostage-taking, arbitration,
and destruction.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Sandmanor receives situated diplomacy, civic reception, festival and sanctuary
networks, and displacement-aware memory. It does not receive Roman cultural
ownership, elite gratitude, citizenship exclusion, or one universal Greek style.

## Historical overview

Greek poleis, koina, sanctuaries, schools, courts, and communities encountered
Roman expansion while remaining internally diverse. Rome negotiated, rewarded,
constrained, garrisoned, declared freedom, took hostages, and destroyed cities;
local civic identities and institutions adapted, resisted, or reinterpreted
these pressures. Corinth's destruction in 146 is a civic rupture, not the end
of Greek cultural life.

## Chronology

- 264–241 BCE: First Punic War changes Roman western reach while Greek states remain entangled with Hellenistic kingdoms.
- 229 BCE: Rome intervenes across the Adriatic and begins a durable eastern presence.
- 215–205 BCE: First Macedonian War overlaps the Second Punic War.
- 200–196 BCE: Second Macedonian War; Roman victory and the 196 freedom proclamation reshape Greek diplomacy.
- 192–188 BCE: War with Antiochus III and Roman settlement in Greece and Asia Minor.
- 171–168 BCE: Third Macedonian War; Macedonian monarchy ends at Pydna.
- 167 BCE: Achaean hostages are taken to Italy; Polybius is among them.
- 149–146 BCE: Achaean War and Corinth's destruction.
- 146 BCE: Roman supremacy expands, while Greek civic and cultural institutions continue.

## Major figures

Polybius, Philopoemen, Aristaenus, Flamininus, Antiochus III, Macedonian and
Achaean officials, civic envoys, priests, teachers, artists, merchants,
displaced people, enslaved people, and unnamed residents represent different
positions rather than one Greek voice.

## Major cities

Corinth, Athens, Sparta, Argos, Megalopolis, Rhodes, Delphi, Delos, Achaea,
Aetolia, Macedonia, Thessaly, Asia Minor, and Roman military and diplomatic
sites. Membership, autonomy, patronage, and archive status vary by city.

## Government

Poleis and koina retained differing assemblies, councils, magistracies, laws,
finances, and diplomatic mechanisms. Rome could promise freedom, supervise,
reward, constrain, garrison, take hostages, or destroy; no proclamation
represented all Greeks equally.

## Religion

Civic cults, sanctuaries, festivals, ruler honors, philosophical ethics,
household religion, and Roman ritual coexisted. Cultural admiration and political
subordination could occur at the same time.

## Economy

Agriculture, markets, sanctuaries, workshops, ports, taxes, benefactions,
credit, labor, enslavement, and military finance supported civic life. Public
honors and monumental sponsorship do not prove equal prosperity.

## Trade

Grain, wine, oil, metals, timber, books, artworks, coin, labor, credit, and
information moved through ports, sanctuaries, leagues, households, and Roman routes.

## Science

Rhetoric, philosophy, history, theater, medicine, mathematics, education,
translation, and diplomacy circulated through cities, courts, sanctuaries, and schools.

## Technology

Walls, roads, harbors, theaters, gymnasia, sanctuaries, ships, siege equipment,
inscriptions, coinage, and civic archives supported public life and war. Destruction
and rebuilding require local provenance.

## Architecture

Agoras, stoas, theaters, gymnasia, sanctuaries, walls, harbors, and civic
monuments organized public encounters in locally distinct ways. Hollow Grove
translates membership and reception logic rather than reproducing an agora.

## Military

Macedonian, Achaean, Aetolian, Seleucid, Roman, and local wars produced siege,
hostage-taking, displacement, enslavement, confiscation, and civic destruction.
Federal cooperation did not remove coercion.

## Art

Drama, sculpture, portraiture, mosaics, coins, architecture, sanctuaries, and
public inscriptions were shaped by local makers, patrons, and changing audiences.
`Greek culture` is not one transferable style.

## Language

Koine Greek enabled communication while local dialects, inscriptions,
translation, and multilingual communities continued. Shared language did not
erase local identity or unequal legal membership.

## Major texts

Polybius, federal decrees, treaties, arbitrations, honorific inscriptions,
citizenship records, philosophical fragments, drama, poetry, coins, and
archaeological context form a plural archive. Later Roman narratives require
source and political-interest labels.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The design inheritance is a reception record: every freedom claim, treaty,
festival, school, destruction, survivor memory, and later interpretation keeps
its speaker, audience, excluded group, and enforcement context.

## Historical relationships

Greek communities negotiated with Rome, Macedonia, Carthage, Ptolemaic Egypt,
Seleucid Syria, Rhodes, Pergamon, and one another through embassies, treaties,
honors, arbitration, alliance, resistance, and war.

### Primary historical records

citizenship evidence, sanctuaries, Polybius, inscriptions, coins, and local
destruction layers with city and date metadata.

### Archaeological evidence

Link Corinth, Athens, Sparta, Achaea, Aetolia, Rhodes, Delphi, Delos, theaters,
gymnasia, sanctuaries, walls, harbors, and Roman military sites to local phases.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize Greek federalism, Roman diplomacy, civic religion, citizenship,
hostages, cultural reception, the Achaean War, and local archaeology.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Sandmanor's nested western wing beside diplomacy,
performance, sanctuary networks, public memory, and displacement studies.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

Poetics, Jung, Campbell, and Goffman remain unchanged. They cannot make Roman
declarations consent, make citizenship universal, decode ancient intention, or
turn one elite civic archive into the whole Greek World.
