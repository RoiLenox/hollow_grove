# Historical Dossier — Early Church, Networks, Debate, and Persecution

Status: Working; Atlas assignment remains Working.

## Evidence boundary
Christian letters, treatises, church orders, martyr narratives, manuscripts, inscriptions, meeting places, and imperial texts are partial and often argumentative. Later accounts may exaggerate early uniformity, hierarchy, orthodoxy, or continuous persecution.
## Historical overview
Between 180 and 313, diverse Christian communities expanded across cities and frontiers, exchanged letters and texts, developed pastoral and charitable practices, debated scripture and authority, and built stronger regional networks. Local and empire-wide persecutions punctuated rather than wholly defined the period; toleration in 311 and 313 changed legal conditions without instantly producing one imperial church.
## Chronology
c. 180–220 Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement, Origen’s early career, and growing textual and episcopal networks; 249–251 Decian requirement for public sacrifice creates conflict and later restoration disputes; 257–260 Valerianic persecution; c. 232–256 Dura-Europos Christian meeting house; 303–311 Great Persecution; 311 Galerius issues toleration; 313 Constantine and Licinius order religious liberty and restoration of confiscated Christian corporate property.
## Major figures
Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Hippolytus, Cyprian, Perpetua and Felicity as represented in the martyr tradition, bishops, deacons, teachers, patrons, widows, confessors, readers, household hosts, copyists, and unnamed congregants.
## Major cities
Rome, Carthage, Alexandria, Antioch, Caesarea, Edessa, Dura-Europos, Lyons, Asia Minor, Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia, and Mediterranean and frontier towns.
## Government
Communities used varied combinations of bishops, presbyters, deacons, teachers, councils, households, patrons, charity rolls, correspondence, and shared property. The evidence does not support one organization everywhere.
## Religion
Christian worship, baptism, Eucharist, prayer, fasting, martyr memory, scripture reading, teaching, and care practices developed amid continuing Jewish, civic, philosophical, and other religious worlds.
## Economy
Household support, gifts, alms, burial associations, travel hospitality, copying, and emerging communal property sustained communities; evidence for scale and ownership varies sharply.
## Trade
Roads, ports, merchants, travelers, couriers, pilgrims, teachers, exiles, and books linked congregations while carrying local differences as well as common language.
## Science
Exegesis, rhetoric, grammar, philosophy, textual comparison, medicine and healing language, calendrical debate, and legal argument shaped Christian intellectual life.
## Technology
Codices, papyrus, parchment, ink, correspondence, adapted houses, baptisteries, lamps, burial spaces, and road and port networks supported teaching and assembly.
## Architecture
Household rooms, adapted meeting houses such as Dura-Europos, baptisteries, cemeteries, and catacombs precede the monumental basilica landscape of later imperial Christianity.
## Military
Christians held diverse views and roles concerning military service and imperial obligation. Persecution, imprisonment, exile, confiscation, and execution require local chronology rather than a single story of constant victimhood.
## Art
Catacomb painting, sarcophagi, lamps, seals, manuscripts, and the Dura-Europos baptistery show emerging visual practices without one universal Christian style.
## Language
Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, and other languages carried scripture, teaching, correspondence, and local worship; translation was constitutive, not merely secondary.
## Major texts
Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement, Origen, Cyprian, martyr accounts, church orders, biblical codices, inscriptions, papyri, Eusebius, Lactantius, and the 311/313 toleration texts.
## Major discoveries or innovations
Durable inheritances include pastoral correspondence, regional consultation, text comparison, community care, property restitution records, and ways of negotiating disagreement under pressure.
## Historical relationships
Christian communities engaged Jewish communities, Roman officials, philosophers, households, urban associations, soldiers, rival Christian teachers, and communities across Roman and Sasanian frontiers.
## Permanent Gifts
- pastoral correspondence and care networks
- communal identity with local difference
- theological debate and text-comparison practice
## Modern Hollow Grove manifestation
Pastoral Correspondence and Difference Forum: a Sandmanor teaching and memory space that shows letters, care obligations, translation, disagreement, persecution evidence, restoration claims, and local community differences without staging doctrinal victory.
## House interpretation
Sandmanor receives care networks, correspondence, and disciplined disagreement, not doctrine, martyrdom as prestige, hierarchy, conversion, or later Christian empire as Proof.
## Library integration
### Primary historical records
Letters, theological works, martyr narratives, church orders, papyri, inscriptions, manuscripts, meeting-place archaeology, and imperial toleration texts.
### Archaeological evidence
Dura-Europos, catacombs, cemeteries, adapted houses, baptisteries, inscriptions, lamps, codices, and papyri.
### Major modern scholarship
Cambridge and Metropolitan Museum scholarship linked below; institutional reach and persecution intensity remain locally bounded.
### Recommended House library placement
Sandmanor Early Church historical wing beside correspondence, community care, rhetoric, translation, religious plurality, and conflict-memory collections.
### Relationship to foundational House classics
The foundational shelf remains unchanged; it frames inherited debate as relational practice without importing theology or ecclesiastical authority.
## Research notes
Do not turn later claims of unity into third-century fact. Preserve differences among household, city, episcopal, scholarly, ascetic, and frontier communities.
