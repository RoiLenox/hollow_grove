# Neo-Babylonian Empire, 612–539 BCE

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Chronicles, royal inscriptions, legal and administrative tablets, temple and
family archives, ration and deportee records, scholarly texts, astronomical
diaries, buildings, and archaeology preserve different institutional views.

### Scholarly interpretation

Archive survival is geographically and socially uneven. Royal construction
claims, labor status, the reach of centralized administration, Nabonidus's
policies, the early diary corpus, and the 539 takeover require source comparison.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Glaüshouse receives long observation, service-archive custody, and maintenance
provenance. Temple office, omen, imperial extraction, deportation, coerced
labor, and royal rebuilding create no Clearance, diagnosis, or Integrity claim.

## Historical overview

Nabopolassar's dynasty became the dominant Mesopotamian and western imperial
power after Assyria's fall. Nebuchadnezzar II's campaigns and royal investment
transformed Babylonian cities, while temple, business, family, legal, and
scholarly archives document complex institutional life. Those resources were
linked to conquest, deprivation, forced movement, extraction, and dependent
labor in imperial territories. Rapid succession followed Nebuchadnezzar;
Nabonidus and Belshazzar formed the final regime. Cyrus captured Babylon in
539, ending the dynasty while many Babylonian institutions continued.

## Chronology

- 626–605 BCE: Nabopolassar; the dynasty begins before this Point.
- 612 BCE: Nineveh falls; Assyrian resistance continues to 609.
- 605–562 BCE: Nebuchadnezzar II; western campaigns and major building programs.
- 597 and 587/586 BCE: staged Babylonian interventions in Judah.
- 562–556 BCE: rapid succession through Amel-Marduk, Neriglissar, and
  Labashi-Marduk.
- 556–539 BCE: Nabonidus; Belshazzar exercises major royal authority during
  Nabonidus's long stay at Tayma.
- 539 BCE: Persian forces defeat the Babylonian regime; Cyrus enters Babylon.

## Major figures

Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar II, Amel-Marduk, Neriglissar, Labashi-Marduk,
Nabonidus, Belshazzar, and Cyrus II form the royal sequence. Jehoiachin and
other deportees, temple administrators, scholarly families, judges, scribes,
merchants, craftspeople, farmers, and laborers prevent royal biography from
standing in for society.

## Major cities

Babylon, Esagil and Etemenanki, Borsippa, Sippar and Ebabbar, Uruk and Eanna,
Nippur, Ur, Larsa, Harran, canal and agricultural settlements, Levantine
provincial centers, and villages represented in deportee archives.

## Government

Kingship operated through crown households, governors, military officers,
temples, cities, scribes, judges, tax and land administration, merchant and
landholding families, and local elites. Nabonidus's absence and Belshazzar's
role show that royal practice could be distributed; royal titles do not reveal
a complete constitution.

## Religion

Marduk and Esagil were central to Babylonian kingship, alongside Nabu, Sin,
Shamash, Ishtar, and local cults. Nabonidus's policies and Cyrus's restoration
narrative are preserved through interested texts. Divine favor and anger are
worldview evidence, not clinical causation.

## Economy

Temple households, crown estates, cities, entrepreneurial families, leases,
loans, taxes, land-service arrangements, craft, agriculture, and labor created
a monetizing economy with substantial inequality. Archive abundance does not
make every resident or region equally visible.

## Trade

Grain, dates, wool, textiles, livestock, metals, timber, stone, wine, oil,
silver, land income, taxes, offerings, rations, and labor moved through river,
canal, Gulf, Iranian, Arabian, Anatolian, and Levantine networks. Tribute,
deportation, assigned service, redistribution, and trade remain distinct.

## Science

Cuneiform mathematical, medical, lexical, divinatory, calendrical, and
celestial scholarship continued. Astronomical diaries built long dated
observation series, but observation, prediction, omen interpretation, later
mathematical models, copy history, and institutional location remain separate.

## Technology

Canals, embankments, quays, walls, gates, temples, processional routes, glazed
brick, domestic and administrative buildings, boats, storage, tablets, writing
boards, seals, weights, metallurgy, textiles, and agricultural systems required
sustained maintenance and varied labor regimes.

## Architecture

Babylon's walls, gates, processional way, palaces, Esagil, Etemenanki, and
works at other cities joined urban service to royal representation. Hollow
Grove receives maintenance, custody, and service-state logic—not an Ishtar
Gate, ziggurat, or processional replica.

## Military

Carchemish, Levantine campaigns, the sieges of Jerusalem and Tyre, garrisons,
deportation, dynastic coups, Persian expansion, Opis, and Babylon's capture
expose imperial reach and human cost. Conquest, deprivation, extraction, and
forced movement are cautions, never Gifts.

## Art

Glazed brick façades, inscribed bricks and cylinders, seals, sculpture, temple
furnishings, metalwork, and staged routes joined craft, cult, and royal
narrative. Their design logic is not a Hollow Grove decoration palette.

## Language

Akkadian cuneiform remained central to formal records and scholarship while
Aramaic was widely used and influenced administrative practice. Sumerian
continued as a scholarly and liturgical language. Script, language, profession,
and community must remain separately described.

## Major texts

- Babylonian Chronicles, including the Nabonidus Chronicle.
- RIBo Babylon 7 royal inscriptions.
- Eanna, Ebabbar, Egibi, and other institutional and family archives.
- Ration tablets and deportee-community legal documents.
- Astronomical diaries, scholarly tablets, colophons, and writing-board records.
- Cyrus Cylinder, treated as Achaemenid royal legitimation after conquest.

## Major discoveries or innovations

- Long-duration, dated celestial-observation series.
- Dense temple-city service archives exposing people, resources, and writing.
- Recoverable provenance for canals, embankments, walls, temples, and repair.
- These inheritances are cumulative practices, not claims that every element
  originated uniquely in the Neo-Babylonian dynasty.

## Historical relationships

The empire fought, ruled, deported, taxed, exchanged, and negotiated across
Judah and the Levant, Egypt, Arabia, Anatolia, Elam, and Iranian powers.
Cyrus's takeover ended the dynasty while reusing Babylonian titles, personnel,
institutions, and religious idioms.

## House interpretation

The Neo-Babylonian record answers Glaüshouse's question by showing that living
and knowledge systems require long observation, maintained services, explicit
custody, and correction across political change. Abundant records also expose
inequality and harm rather than guaranteeing Integrity.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Chronicles, royal inscriptions, temple and family archives, legal and
administrative tablets, ration and deportee records, astronomical diaries,
scholarly tablets, building inscriptions, and the Cyrus Cylinder.

### Archaeological evidence

Babylon, Borsippa, Sippar, Uruk, Nippur, Ur, Larsa, and related canals and
settlements; walls, gates, temples, houses, quays, bricks, tablets, seals,
workshops, environmental evidence, and repair phases.

### Major modern scholarship

- Institutional and economic history of the Neo-Babylonian Empire.
- Studies of temple communities, private archives, labor, and deportation.
- Histories of Babylonian celestial science and diary chronology.
- Archaeological and textual studies of Babylon's urban transformation and 539.

### Recommended House library placement

Glaüshouse Age II historical wing, beside longitudinal evidence, environmental
and infrastructure maintenance, archive custody, living systems, and translation.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*On the Origin of Species*, *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, *The Tree of
Knowledge*, and the living-systems collection remain unchanged. They support
comparison of long evidence and systems but cannot make temple administration
care or translate celestial divination into modern cognition.

## Research notes

- Distinguish dynasty, empire, Babylonia, Babylon, temple, and archive.
- Record archive location, institution, social reach, and end date.
- Distinguish celestial observation, prediction, divination, and later theory.
- Attach every building claim to labor status, service effect, and archaeology.
- Treat the Cyrus Cylinder as royal legitimation, not a modern rights charter.
- Carry Babylonian institutional continuity across 539 without implying no change.
