# Antigonid Macedonia and the Post-Monarchy Transition, 281–146 BCE

Civilization: Antigonid Macedonia and its attributable post-168 institutional legacy  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point VII / Stonebend — Antigonid  
Status: Reviewed phased-polity dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working and Needs date refinement  
Temporal fit: Precursor 281–277/276 / Kingdom 277/276–168 / Post-dynastic transition 168–146

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Coins, inscriptions, decrees, fortifications, settlements, palace and burial
archaeology, Polybius, and later Roman-era narratives illuminate different
institutions and reigns. The surviving record is discontinuous. Livy and
Plutarch write later and from Roman or biographical frames; coin circulation
does not by itself prove direct political control.

### Scholarly interpretation

The relationship among king, court, army, cities, regional institutions,
garrisons, and Greek federal states remains debated. The 168 settlement is
clear as a dynastic break, but continuity in local offices, material systems,
and regional practice must be demonstrated institution by institution.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Stonebend inherits phase-specific authority records, jurisdictional
provenance, and mint accountability. It does not inherit monarchy, royal
territory, garrison coercion, military hegemony, Roman conquest, or a right to
treat coin imagery as constitutional identity.

## Historical overview

The Point opens after the death of Lysimachus and Seleucus, before a durable
Antigonid kingdom existed in Macedonia. Antigonus II Gonatas secured Macedonia
in 277/276 and his dynasty ruled, with changing territorial reach and relations
with cities and leagues, until the Roman defeat of Perseus at Pydna in 168.
Rome abolished the monarchy and imposed a four-district settlement. Andriscus's
brief royal revival in 149–148 was defeated, followed by provincial
reorganization. The supplied `Antigonid` label therefore names three distinct
phases rather than continuous sovereignty from 281 to 146.

## Chronology

- 281 BCE: Seleucus defeats Lysimachus at Corupedium; neither a stable Antigonid Macedonia nor a settled successor order follows immediately.
- 277/276 BCE: Antigonus II Gonatas establishes durable possession of Macedonia after the Galatian crisis.
- 267–261 BCE: the Chremonidean War tests Antigonid power, Athens, Sparta, and Ptolemaic involvement.
- 239–229 BCE: Demetrius II rules amid conflict involving Achaean, Aetolian, Epirote, and Illyrian powers.
- 229–221 BCE: dynastic minority and regency; Antigonus III Doson becomes king.
- 224–222 BCE: the Hellenic League and war against Cleomenes III culminate at Sellasia.
- 221–179 BCE: Philip V; Social War and the First and Second Macedonian Wars.
- 197 BCE: Roman victory at Cynoscephalae sharply limits Macedonian power.
- 179–168 BCE: Perseus reigns; the Third Macedonian War ends at Pydna.
- 168 BCE: monarchy abolished; Macedonia divided into four districts under Roman settlement.
- 149–148 BCE: Andriscus claims the throne and is defeated.
- 148–146 BCE: provincial reorganization closes this dossier's post-dynastic transition.

## Major figures

Antigonus II Gonatas, Demetrius II, Antigonus III Doson, Philip V, Perseus,
Aratus of Sicyon, Cleomenes III, Polybius, Flamininus, Aemilius Paullus,
Andriscus, royal and civic officials, mint workers, soldiers, miners,
craftspeople, farmers, merchants, and residents of garrisoned communities.

## Major cities

Pella, Aigai, Amphipolis, Thessalonica, Demetrias, Chalcis, Corinth and
Acrocorinth, Athens and Piraeus, and communities in Thessaly. Capital, royal
residence, mint, civic jurisdiction, league member, garrison, battlefield, and
post-168 district require separate place records.

## Government

The kings governed through court, army, royal officers, regional revenues,
mints, cities, garrisons, allied structures, and negotiated or coercive
relationships with federal states. These arrangements varied by reign and
place. After Pydna, monarchy ended; Roman regulations altered district,
taxation, movement, and political relationships without erasing every local
institution.

## Religion

Local cults, pan-Hellenic sanctuaries, Macedonian kingship, dynastic ancestry,
victory dedications, civic ritual, ruler honors, and later Roman freedom claims
served different audiences. Honor and cult participation are historical
relationships, not reliable evidence of free consent or modern legitimacy.

## Economy

Agriculture, pastoral production, forests, mines, royal land, civic finance,
taxation, military expenditure, markets, workshops, and household labor
supported the kingdom. The post-168 settlement changed resource controls and
exchange rules; wealth and burden were distributed unevenly.

## Trade

Grain, timber, metals, livestock, wine, ceramics, coin, weapons, military pay,
and information moved through Aegean harbors and Balkan land routes. Coin
issues and hoards can help map chronology and exchange, but neither circulation
nor iconography alone establishes political borders or prosperity.

## Science

Surveying, mining knowledge, medicine, agriculture, mechanics, philosophy,
route intelligence, accounting, and civic and royal recordkeeping depended on
specialists and local institutions. Their continuity across 168 must name the
custodian and changed authority rather than assume an intact state tradition.

## Technology

Mints, dies, weights, fortifications, garrisons, roads, harbors, ships, mines,
siege systems, weapons, palaces, storage, and archives joined material systems
to authority. Each requires labor, supply, repair, and a dated jurisdiction.

## Architecture

Palaces, tombs, fortifications, sanctuaries, civic buildings, harbors, and
garrison works accumulated in different phases. Hollow Grove translates their
documented construction and authority histories into legible civic provenance;
it does not copy a palace, royal tomb, fortress, or Macedonian visual costume.

## Military

The Galatian crisis, Chremonidean War, Demetrian and Cleomenean conflicts,
Social War, Macedonian Wars, Cynoscephalae, Pydna, and Andriscus's revolt
repeatedly altered command and territory. Conscription, garrisoning, siege,
plunder, enslavement, destruction, and punitive settlement are costs and
cautions, never Stonebend Gifts.

## Art

Coins, portraits, monuments, palaces, tombs, mosaics, metalwork, sanctuaries,
and civic honors could cite dynastic ancestry or local identity. Every design
record should identify issuer or patron, maker where known, material, date,
audience, find context, and later reuse.

## Language

Greek dominates the surviving written record, with regional forms and
specialized civic, royal, military, and monetary vocabularies. Modern
translations should retain genre and contested institutional terms rather than
turn every office into a modern constitutional equivalent.

## Major texts

Polybius, royal and civic inscriptions, treaties, decrees, coin legends, and
archaeological records are central. Livy and Plutarch are later witnesses and
must be read beside contemporary epigraphy, numismatics, and material evidence.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed design inheritance is not an invention claim. It is the capacity
to align dated royal, civic, federal, garrison, mint, and post-monarchy records
without pretending that a durable asset proves continuous authority.

## Historical relationships

Antigonid rulers dealt with Macedonian communities, Athens, Sparta, Achaean and
Aetolian federal states, Epirus, Illyrian and Thracian polities, Ptolemaic and
Seleucid courts, Pergamon, Rhodes, and Rome through alliance, arbitration,
marriage, patronage, trade, garrison, revolt, and war. The dynasty's defeat did
not terminate the history of the communities it had governed.

## House interpretation

Stonebend asks what makes public form durable and attributable. Law of
Identity, permanence, Presynce, and Claim / Title / Yield require the Registry
to preserve construction, issue, jurisdiction, revision, and termination. The
historical evidence supplies comparison, not sovereignty or modern Title.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Collect inscriptions, decrees, treaties, coin issues and hoards, seals, and
Polybius in phase-specific groupings. Keep later Livy and Plutarch visibly
classified as later literary witnesses.

### Archaeological evidence

Link Pella, Aigai, Amphipolis, Thessalonica, Demetrias, Chalcis, Corinth,
fortifications, palaces, burials, mints, mines, harbors, and battle landscapes
to excavation context, dating method, conservation, and later alteration.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize Macedonian political institutions, the Greek leagues, numismatics,
regional archaeology, the Macedonian Wars, the 168 settlement, Andriscus, and
provincial formation. Preserve scholarly disagreement about royal control and
civic autonomy rather than resolving it through House doctrine.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Stonebend's Age II wing beside jurisdiction history,
public-works provenance, metrology, numismatics, and institutional termination.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

The Republic, Metaphysics, Meditations, The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions, and Process and Reality remain unchanged. They may frame questions
about form, authority, change, and continuity, but cannot make kingship
constitutional, Roman conquest lawful by inheritance, or coin issue a Title.

## Research notes

Further work should refine the 277/276 establishment, regional variation in
royal and civic power, chronology of particular mint issues, the four-district
settlement, and the legal and institutional steps between 148 and 146.
