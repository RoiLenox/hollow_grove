# Greek World, 539–336 BCE

Civilization: Greek world  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point V / Sandmanor — Greek  
Status: Reviewed broad-label dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working  
Temporal fit: Full regional horizon / Politically plural

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Local inscriptions, laws, decrees, treaties, accounts, monuments, coins,
pottery, sanctuaries, burials, drama, histories, oratory, and philosophy expose
many communities unevenly. Athens supplies unusually abundant evidence and
cannot represent the entire Greek-speaking world.

### Scholarly interpretation

The Point's `Greek` label is a cultural and regional umbrella, not a state.
Polis, ethnos, league, colony, democracy, oligarchy, tyranny, citizenship,
economy, and Panhellenism varied by place and phase. Later literary canons
create strong survival bias.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Sandmanor receives civic argument tied to local records, festival performance
as public interpretation, and diplomacy that retains every party's identity.
Slavery, civic exclusion, allied subordination, colonization, massacre, and
prestige hierarchy remain evidence and cautions—not Gifts.

## Historical overview

Between 539 and 336 BCE, hundreds of communities across mainland Greece, the
Aegean, Anatolian coast, Black Sea, Sicily, southern Italy, and the wider
Mediterranean organized themselves as poleis, ethnē, kingdoms, leagues,
tyrannies, oligarchies, and democracies. They shared mobile languages, cults,
festivals, stories, and practices without political unity. War, alliance,
arbitration, trade, migration, performance, philosophy, and Persian interaction
continually changed relationships.

## Chronology

- Late sixth century BCE: Persian rule reaches many eastern Greek communities;
  political changes occur in Athens and elsewhere under local conditions.
- 499–494 BCE: Ionian Revolt.
- 490 and 480–479 BCE: major Achaemenid invasions of mainland Greece.
- Fifth century BCE: changing Athenian-led alliance and empire, local regimes,
  cultural production, and interstate competition.
- 431–404 BCE: Peloponnesian War and linked conflicts.
- 395–387/386 BCE: Corinthian War and King's Peace.
- 371 BCE: Leuctra and a phase of Theban predominance.
- 338 BCE: Philip II defeats the allied Greek force at Chaeronea.
- 336 BCE: Philip dies and Alexander succeeds; Greek civic life continues.

## Major figures

Cleisthenes, Miltiades, Themistocles, Pericles, Brasidas, Lysander, Epaminondas,
Agesilaus, Dionysius I, Evagoras, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Herodotus,
Thucydides, Xenophon, Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and Aristophanes belong
to different cities, institutions, genres, and dates. Women, resident
foreigners, enslaved people, craftspeople, performers, sailors, farmers, and
soldiers are indispensable despite unequal visibility.

## Major cities

Athens and Attica, Sparta and Laconia, Corinth, Thebes, Argos, Delphi, Olympia,
Miletus, Ephesus, Samos, Chios, Lesbos, Syracuse, Taras, Massalia, Cyrene, and
Black Sea settlements illustrate plural local systems. Each polis included
urban center, countryside, sanctuaries, households, harbors, and dependent populations.

## Government

Assemblies, councils, magistrates, courts, kings, oligarchic boards, tyrants,
leagues, and federal institutions appeared in different combinations.
Athenian democracy, Spartan institutions, Boeotian federalism, Sicilian
tyrannies, and Achaemenid-supervised Greek cities must not be merged.
Formal citizenship excluded much of the population.

## Religion

Civic and household cults, sanctuaries, festivals, oracles, mystery rites,
hero traditions, poetry, and philosophy varied locally. Shared divine names
and Panhellenic sites created channels of recognition without a single theology.

## Economy

Households, farms, workshops, mines, ports, sanctuaries, public finance,
markets, credit, taxation, alliance payments, and enslaved labor formed
different local economies. Surviving Athenian accounts cannot be generalized
without comparison.

## Trade

Grain, wine, oil, timber, metals, ceramics, textiles, enslaved people, coin,
credit, art objects, and information moved across sea and land. Trade, tribute,
cleruchy, migration, military service, colonization, and enslavement are
analytically different relationships.

## Science

Medicine, mathematics, astronomy, natural philosophy, historical inquiry,
rhetoric, political thought, and philosophical schools developed through
argument, apprenticeship, observation, inherited knowledge, and competing
institutions. Named authors represent only the texts that survived.

## Technology

Ships, harbors, triremes, fortifications, water systems, mines, quarries,
theaters, temples, stoas, houses, coins, weights, pottery, metalwork, siege
equipment, and agricultural tools linked household production, public finance,
trade, and warfare.

## Architecture

Agoras, assembly spaces, stoas, theaters, sanctuaries, fortifications,
fountains, roads, houses, workshops, and harbors arranged public interaction
differently in each community. Hollow Grove inherits spatial support for
auditable encounter, not a universal marble-column style.

## Military

The Ionian Revolt, Persian Wars, Athenian imperial coercion, Peloponnesian War,
Corinthian War, Theban hegemony, mercenary service, civil conflict, and
Macedonia's rise reveal war as a recurring organizer of relationships.
Democratic procedures did not eliminate slavery, massacre, displacement, or allied subordination.

## Art

Festival drama, choral performance, epic and lyric reception, sculpture,
painted pottery, architecture, music, monuments, funerary art, and philosophical
dialogue were locally situated and widely mobile. `Classical` form must not
erase regional, non-elite, changing, or contested production.

## Language

Greek dialects, local alphabets, public and private inscriptions, performance,
and manuscript transmission operated alongside other Mediterranean languages.
Attic literary survival is not proof that all communities spoke, wrote, or
argued alike.

## Major texts

Local laws, decrees, treaties, tribute and casualty lists, leases and boundary
stones belong beside Herodotus, Thucydides, Xenophon, drama, oratory, Platonic
dialogues, and Aristotelian works. Each literary work must retain genre, date,
place, transmission history, and social exclusions.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is the repeated use of public record, structured
argument, performance, and inter-community negotiation in diverse civic
settings. It is not a claim that Greece invented politics, reason, theater, or democracy.

## Historical relationships

Greek communities allied, arbitrated, exchanged, colonized, fought, and
competed among themselves and with Achaemenid, Egyptian, Phoenician, Carian,
Lydian, Thracian, Illyrian, Macedonian, Scythian, Italian, and North African
communities. Macedonian hegemony reorganizes many mainland relationships at the
Point boundary but does not end Greek civic life.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Local inscriptions, laws, decrees, treaties, accounts, casualty lists,
dedications, drama, histories, oratory, and philosophy belong in city- and
genre-specific collections rather than one undifferentiated Greek shelf.

### Archaeological evidence

Agoras, theaters, sanctuaries, houses, harbors, fortifications, pottery, coins,
burials, tools, and workshop remains must retain excavation context and local chronology.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize epigraphic corpora, excavation publications, polis and federal-state
studies, economic history, performance studies, Persian–Greek interaction,
slavery, gender, migration, and non-Athenian evidence.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Sandmanor's Age II historical wing, cross-indexed to civic
performance, diplomacy, local law, rhetoric, religion, philosophy, and social
exclusion. Placement grants no Court or diplomatic authority.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

Aristotle's *Poetics* belongs directly to this Point's performance inheritance;
its date, genre, and arguments remain specific. Jung, Campbell, and Goffman can
support later comparative questions about symbol, story, and social role, but
they are not historical sources for Greek institutions.

## Research notes

Future review should deepen non-Athenian, domestic, enslaved, female, colonial,
and eastern Greek evidence; distinguish polis from ethnos and federal systems;
and keep every constitutional or cultural claim tied to a place and phase.
