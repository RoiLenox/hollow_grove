# Late Egypt, 539–336 BCE

Civilization: Late Egypt  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point V / Glaüshouse — Late Egypt  
Status: Reviewed composite-period dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working and Needs date refinement  
Temporal fit: Full regional horizon / Multiple regimes / Continues

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Egyptian inscriptions, Demotic documents, Aramaic papyri, monuments, temple and
funerary material, settlement archaeology, coins, seals, Achaemenid evidence,
and later Greek narratives document different communities unevenly. The Delta's
preservation is especially poor.

### Scholarly interpretation

`Late Egypt` is a regional period label crossing changing sovereignty, not one
state or unchanged society. Persian policy, resistance, collaboration, temple
continuity, ethnic labels, and the dating of uninscribed art require phase- and
site-specific treatment.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Glaüshouse receives custody continuity across institutional handoffs,
multilingual legal-document preservation, and restoration provenance. Foreign
conquest, dynastic legitimacy, taxation, military occupation, and an imagined
timeless Egypt do not become Integrity Gifts.

## Historical overview

The supplied coordinate begins during the Saite Twenty-Sixth Dynasty, enters
first Achaemenid rule after 525 BCE, regains independent kingship under
Dynasties 28–30 after 404, and returns to Achaemenid rule after 343. The Point
ends in 336, while that second Persian phase continues to Alexander's arrival
in 332. Temple, scribal, legal, household, artistic, water, and local
institutions persisted and changed across these political shifts.

## Chronology

- 539–526 BCE: later reign of Amasis within the supplied Point.
- 526–525 BCE: Psamtik III and the end of Saite rule.
- 525–404 BCE: first Achaemenid or Twenty-Seventh Dynasty period, interrupted
  by revolts and changing regional conditions.
- 404–399 BCE: Dynasty 28 under Amyrtaeus.
- 399–380 BCE: Dynasty 29.
- 380–343 BCE: Dynasty 30; Nectanebo II is the final independent pharaoh of the period.
- 343–336 BCE: second Achaemenid rule within this Point.
- 332 BCE: Alexander enters Egypt, inside Point VI.

## Major figures

Amasis and Psamtik III close Saite rule. Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I and
later Achaemenid rulers belong to Persian phases. Amyrtaeus, Nepherites I,
Hakor, Nectanebo I, Teos, and Nectanebo II represent independent dynasties;
Artaxerxes III and Darius III belong to renewed Persian government.
Udjahorresnet, priests, scribes, officials, soldiers, and Elephantine households
expose institutions beyond kingship.

## Major cities

Sais, Memphis and the palace of Apries, Thebes and Karnak, Elephantine, Hibis,
Dakhla and Kharga Oases, Mendes, Sebennytos, Naukratis, Saqqara, Heliopolis,
Delta temples, and Nile routes had distinct political and institutional lives.

## Government

Pharaonic kingship, Achaemenid satrapal administration centered at Memphis,
temples, local authorities, military garrisons, scribes, judges, tax officials,
estates, and Delta dynasties shared implementation differently by phase.
Persian use of Egyptian titulary or officials does not erase conquest or prove
uniform accommodation.

## Religion

Temple cults, local deities, Osirian traditions, animal cults, household
practice, royal ritual, and funerary institutions remained diverse. Royal
restoration and temple patronage were politically situated acts, and particular
inscriptions cannot define the experience of all cult communities.

## Economy

Agriculture, livestock, temple and estate production, taxation, military pay,
craft, transport, household exchange, and coin use varied across region and
regime. Legal documents show particular transactions and communities rather
than a complete national economy.

## Trade

Grain, papyrus, textiles, livestock, metals, timber, stone, faience, ceramics,
incense, coin, taxes, military supply, and information moved through Nile,
desert, Mediterranean, Red Sea, Levantine, Aegean, and imperial networks.
Tax, requisition, offering, pay, gift, and voluntary trade remain distinct.

## Science

Temple scholarship, medicine, ritual knowledge, law, accounting, astronomy,
calendrical practice, surveying, and translation continued in several scribal
traditions. Preservation often depended on institutions whose records survive
unevenly.

## Technology

Nile transport, harbors, irrigation, oasis water systems, enclosure walls,
stone temples, platforms, fortifications, papyrus, pottery, faience, bronze,
stone carving, ships, coinage, and military equipment persisted through repair,
reuse, new sponsorship, and unequal labor.

## Architecture

Temples, enclosure walls, gates, chapels, tombs, palaces, settlements, and
waterworks carry repair and patronage layers from different regimes. Hollow
Grove inherits conservation that exposes each intervention, not a replica
temple district or a claim of unchanged sacred style.

## Military

Cambyses's conquest, revolts, Athenian intervention, restored independence,
repeated Persian invasion attempts, Artaxerxes III's reconquest, garrison life,
and mercenary service brought disruption and unequal power. Resistance,
restoration, and victory are evidence, not gifts of Integrity.

## Art

Artists and patrons selected and recomposed older forms while producing new
royal and non-royal sculpture, temple relief, bronzes, faience, funerary objects,
and multilingual monuments. Each work needs maker, patron, date, material,
find context, and restoration history where known.

## Language

Demotic, hieroglyphic, hieratic, Aramaic, Greek, and imperial documentary
traditions served different legal, administrative, ritual, and community
settings. Multilingual evidence is a custody problem as much as a translation problem.

## Major texts

The Udjahorresnet statue inscription, Egyptian royal titulary and monuments,
Demotic legal and administrative papyri, Aramaic Elephantine papyri and ostraca,
temple and funerary inscriptions, Achaemenid records, and Herodotus require
distinct genre, chronology, and audience labels.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed design inheritance is evidence-rich institutional continuity and
repair through regime change: multilingual record custody, adaptable temple and
local practice, and visible restoration provenance. It is not a claim of static survival.

## Historical relationships

Egyptian institutions interacted with Achaemenid authorities, Greek and Carian
soldiers and communities, Phoenician and Cypriot networks, Kush and Nubia,
Libyan regions, Levantine ports, and internal Delta and Upper Egyptian centers.
Each relationship changed across the Point's four political phases.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Demotic legal records, Egyptian inscriptions, Aramaic papyri, royal titulary,
temple records, coins, seals, and later narratives belong in phase-specific
collections with language, custody, restoration, and find-context metadata.

### Archaeological evidence

Memphis, Sais, Thebes, Elephantine, oasis sites, Naukratis, temples, settlements,
burials, sculpture, papyri, pottery, and water infrastructure belong beside
conservation and environmental records.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize excavation reports, papyrology and epigraphy, Late Period political
history, Persian-period regional studies, temple economy, multilingual law,
conservation science, and work addressing Delta preservation bias.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Glaüshouse's Age II historical wing, connected to
preservation, legal-document custody, translation, restoration, water systems,
and institutional memory. Placement grants no clinical or ritual authority.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

Darwin, Bateson, Maturana, and Varela can support later comparisons about
adaptation, ecology, cognition, and living continuity. They are not sources for
Late Egyptian religion or institutions, and should not biologize cultural survival.

temple and garrison relationships, document custody, legal pluralism, and the
dating of uninscribed objects. Point VI must preserve the 336–332 Persian phase.
