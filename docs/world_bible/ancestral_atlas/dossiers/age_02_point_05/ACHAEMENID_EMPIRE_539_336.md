# Achaemenid Empire, 539–336 BCE

Status: Reviewed dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

### Historical evidence

Royal inscriptions, the Persepolis Fortification and Treasury archives,
Babylonian documents, Aramaic papyri, Egyptian records, archaeology, seals,
coins, and Greek narratives expose different regions and institutions. The
Point ends at Darius III's accession in 336 BCE; Achaemenid kingship continues
until 330.

### Scholarly interpretation

`Satrapy`, administrative uniformity, royal-road organization, labor status,
religious policy, and the representativeness of Persepolis evidence remain
source- and region-dependent. Greek–Persian war is important but cannot stand
for the whole empire.

### Hollow Grove adaptation

Stonebend receives inspectable regional jurisdiction, multilingual document
provenance, and provisioned relay coordination. Empire, conquest, taxation,
forced movement, hierarchy, and royal ideology do not become constitutional
precedent or Gifts.

## Historical overview

From Cyrus II's capture of Babylon in 539 through Darius III's accession in
336, the Achaemenid Empire governed changing territories through royal and
regional authorities, local institutions, archives, roads, provisioning,
taxation, military force, and several written languages. Coordination depended
on adaptation and negotiation as well as extraction, revolt suppression,
unequal labor, and dynastic conflict. No single administrative diagram fits
every province or reign.

## Chronology

- 539 BCE: Cyrus II captures Babylon.
- 525 BCE: Cambyses II conquers Egypt.
- 522–486 BCE: Darius I consolidates rule, suppresses revolts, and sponsors
  the Bisitun inscription and major works at Persepolis and Susa.
- 499–449 BCE, conventionally: Ionian Revolt and major phases of Greek–Persian war.
- 465–359 BCE: successive reigns and recurrent court, Egyptian, Anatolian,
  Cypriot, and western imperial conflicts.
- 343 BCE: Artaxerxes III reconquers Egypt.
- 336 BCE: Darius III accedes; the supplied Point ends.
- 330 BCE: Achaemenid kingship ends, inside Point VI.

## Major figures

Cyrus II, Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I, Darius II,
Artaxerxes II, Artaxerxes III, Arses, and Darius III define the royal sequence.
Atossa, Parysatis, Amestris, regional governors, local rulers, scribes, estate
managers, workers, travelers, temple personnel, and rebels reveal institutions
beyond royal biography.

## Major cities

Pasargadae, Persepolis, Susa, Ecbatana, Babylon, Sardis, Memphis, Elephantine,
Bisitun, and Naqsh-e Rostam had different ceremonial, administrative,
religious, residential, archival, and route functions. The empire also joined
centers across the Levant, Anatolia, Egypt, Iran, Central Asia, and the Indus frontier.

## Government

The Great King, royal household, high officers, regional governors commonly
called satraps, local dynasts, city and temple institutions, garrisons, scribes,
judges, estate managers, and tax or tribute systems shared implementation
unevenly. Local continuity did not equal political autonomy or consent.

## Religion

Royal inscriptions privilege Ahuramazda and a vocabulary of truth, order, and
loyalty. Many local cults and institutions continued. The evidence does not
support one uniformly imposed religion, modern religious liberty, or a simple
identification of all imperial practice with later Zoroastrian orthodoxy.

## Economy

Royal, temple, estate, military, household, and market economies moved produce,
silver, labor, taxes, tribute, rations, and gifts under different legal and
coercive conditions. The Persepolis tablets illuminate provisioning and work
groups in one major archive zone, not a complete imperial economy.

## Trade

Grain, dates, wine, livestock, timber, stone, metals, textiles, horses, crafted
goods, silver, coin, labor, and information moved by river, sea, and road.
Market exchange, tax, tribute, ration, requisition, diplomatic gift, and forced
movement must retain distinct labels.

## Science

Babylonian astronomy and scribal scholarship, Egyptian medicine and surveying,
regional measurement, accounting, calendrical practice, and multilingual
administration continued within the empire. These were distributed traditions,
not a single royal science program.

## Technology

Roads, ferries, bridges, relays, travel authorizations, rations, canals,
fortifications, ships, siege equipment, terraces, stonework, glazed brick,
tablets, papyri, seals, weights, and coins depended on regional expertise and
varied labor statuses.

## Architecture

Pasargadae, Persepolis, Susa, and royal tombs combine ceremonial planning,
terraces, columned halls, relief, masonry, and regionally diverse workshops.
Hollow Grove inherits coordinated provenance and jurisdictional legibility,
not replica palaces, royal costume, or imperial spectacle.

## Military

Conquest, revolt suppression, Aegean campaigns, Egyptian revolts, dynastic
conflict, the revolt of Cyrus the Younger, and fourth-century reconquests show
reach and recurring crisis. Regional recruitment and logistics never erase the
coercion, destruction, execution, or forced service recorded in the evidence.

## Art

Architecture, relief, glazed brick, seals, vessels, metalwork, textiles, and
locally made objects joined royal representation to diverse workshops.
Delegation scenes project imperial order; they do not demonstrate equal or
voluntary participation.

## Language

Elamite, Babylonian, Old Persian, Aramaic, Egyptian, Greek, and other languages
served different documentary communities. Language, script, genre, author,
translator, audience, archive, and date must remain attached to every record.

## Major texts

The Cyrus Cylinder, Darius I's Bisitun inscription and versions, royal
inscriptions, Persepolis Fortification and Treasury tablets, Babylonian legal
and temple archives, Elephantine papyri, and Greek authors such as Herodotus
and Xenophon require genre-specific reading.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not a claim that the empire invented roads,
archives, or multilingual rule. Its design value lies in the documented scale
at which regional jurisdictions, provisioned movement, local institutions, and
multiple documentary traditions were coordinated and made visible.

## Historical relationships

The empire ruled, allied with, taxed, recruited from, exchanged with, and fought
Babylonian, Egyptian, Anatolian, Levantine, Iranian, Central Asian, South Asian,
Cypriot, Phoenician, and Greek communities. Greek communities also lived within
imperial territories and served in imperial systems.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

Royal inscriptions, Persepolis administrative tablets, Babylonian archives,
Aramaic papyri, Egyptian records, coins, and seals belong in a multilingual
imperial-documentation collection with provenance and genre metadata.

### Archaeological evidence

Persepolis, Pasargadae, Susa, Sardis, Babylon, Elephantine, Bisitun, road and
station evidence, seals, vessels, tablets, architecture, and landscape surveys
belong beside conservation and labor-context records.

### Major modern scholarship

Prioritize archival publications, regional administrative studies, annotated
inscription corpora, archaeological reports, and scholarship that tests Greek
literary narratives against documentary evidence.

### Recommended House library placement

Place the dossier in Stonebend's Age II historical wing, connected to public
records, infrastructure, multilingual administration, standards, and
provenance studies. It grants no legal or administrative authority.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*The Republic* and *Metaphysics* can frame questions of polity, category, and
form; *Meditations* can frame office and discipline; *The Structure of
Scientific Revolutions* and *Process and Reality* can test changing explanatory
systems. None is evidence for Achaemenid institutions or a license to idealize empire.

## Research notes

Further review should distinguish each region and reign, labor categories in
the Fortification archive, the changing use of `satrapy`, road reconstruction,
and the limits of royal inscriptions. Point VI must preserve Achaemenid
political reality from 336 to 330.
