# New Kingdom Egypt — Historical Dossier

Civilization: New Kingdom Egypt, reviewed through Dynasties 18 and 19  
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point V, Stonebend  
Date relevance: reviewed slice c. 1550–1200 BCE within the New Kingdom, c. 1550–1070 BCE  
Status: Reviewed dossier; Point endpoint precedes the end of the New Kingdom; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes buildings and their successive phases, royal and
private inscriptions, foundation deposits, tombs, settlements, objects, the
Amarna letters, papyri, and ostraca. Monumental texts and victory scenes present
royal claims; they are not neutral descriptions of administration or events.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs changing dynasties, institutions,
work communities, empire, religious programs, craft, and diplomacy from evidence
concentrated at particular sites and social levels. New Kingdom chronology and
some reign lengths remain debated.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Stonebend receives phase attribution, skilled-work records,
drafting, correction, and maintenance memory—not pharaonic sovereignty,
monumental excess, conquest, dependent labor, or a new source of Claim, Title,
or Yield.

## Historical overview

Ahmose I ended Hyksos rule in the Delta and reunited Egypt around 1550 BCE.
Dynasty 18 rulers then established control in Nubia and influence or direct rule
in parts of the Levant. The resulting wealth supported expanding temples,
palaces, tombs, administrative institutions, craft communities, and far-reaching
diplomatic exchange.

The reviewed slice includes Hatshepsut's kingship, Thutmose III's campaigns,
Amenhotep III's international court, Akhenaten's Aten-centered program at
Akhetaten, its reversal under Tutankhamun and successors, and the Ramesside
reorganization of Dynasty 19. Ramesses II's long reign joined building,
campaigning, diplomacy, and a treaty with Hattusili III.

The Point ends around 1200 BCE, near the close of Dynasty 19. It does not include
the whole New Kingdom. Dynasty 20 and Ramesses III's conflicts occur chiefly
after this dossier's boundary, and the period continues to about 1070 BCE.
Egypt therefore did not simply fall in the supplied terminal event.

## Chronology

- **c. 1550 BCE:** Ahmose I completes reunification and begins Dynasty 18.
- **c. 1479–1458 BCE:** Hatshepsut rules first as regent and then as king and
  senior co-ruler with Thutmose III.
- **c. 1479–1425 BCE:** Thutmose III's long reign includes major Levantine
  campaigns and extensive temple work.
- **c. 1390–1352 BCE:** Amenhotep III's court participates in the great-king
  diplomatic system represented in the Amarna archive.
- **c. 1353–1336 BCE:** Akhenaten establishes Akhetaten and an exceptional
  Aten-centered royal program.
- **Late Dynasty 18:** Tutankhamun and Horemheb restore and reorganize older cult
  and royal institutions.
- **c. 1295 BCE:** Dynasty 19 begins; the administrative center shifts north.
- **c. 1279–1213 BCE:** Ramesses II builds extensively, campaigns against Hatti,
  and later concludes a parity treaty with Hattusili III.
- **Around 1200 BCE:** late Dynasty 19 succession and instability coincide with
  wider regional change; the New Kingdom continues into Dynasty 20.

## Major figures

- **Ahmose I:** reunifying founder of Dynasty 18.
- **Hatshepsut:** king and senior co-ruler whose monuments preserve both female
  titulary and conventional forms of kingship.
- **Thutmose III:** ruler associated with Levantine expansion and large building programs.
- **Amenhotep III:** ruler of a wealthy court connected to international diplomacy.
- **Akhenaten and Nefertiti:** central to the Amarna religious and visual program.
- **Tutankhamun and Horemheb:** associated with restoration and institutional reordering.
- **Seti I and Ramesses II:** Dynasty 19 rulers important for building, war, and diplomacy.
- **Deir el-Medina crews:** named draftsmen, scribes, foremen, quarry workers,
  carpenters, and households provide unusually dense work-community evidence.

## Major cities

- **Thebes:** major religious, mortuary, administrative, and craft landscape.
- **Karnak and Luxor:** temple complexes expanded and altered across many reigns.
- **Deir el-Bahri:** includes Hatshepsut's terraced mortuary temple and later interventions.
- **Valley of the Kings and Deir el-Medina:** royal tomb workplace and specialist settlement.
- **Memphis and Pi-Ramesses:** important northern administrative and royal centers.
- **Akhetaten/Amarna:** purpose-built capital and findspot of the diplomatic tablets.
- **Nubian and Levantine centers:** governed, garrisoned, or influenced unevenly
  and never reduced to extensions of an Egyptian city plan.

## Government

Kingship operated through viziers, treasuries, temples, scribes, royal domains,
army officers, provincial and imperial administrators, and specialized work
crews. Temples held substantial land, labor, and resources, but the relationship
between palace, cult, household, and locality changed across nearly four
centuries. A king's name on a structure identifies a claim of patronage or
authority; it does not by itself disclose designer, workforce, sequence, or cost.

## Religion

Amun-Re, Re, Ptah, Osiris, Hathor, local deities, household practices, royal
mortuary religion, and changing afterlife texts coexisted. Akhenaten's program
elevated the Aten and disrupted older cult institutions; later rulers reversed
much of it. The Atlas does not turn this exceptional episode into a simple story
of monotheistic progress or treat temple building as Stonebend law.

## Economy

Agriculture, landholding, taxation, temple and palace estates, craft, quarrying,
mining, storage, rations, dependent labor, slavery, military provisioning, and
tribute supported the state. Deir el-Medina records show detailed supply and
work life for an exceptional royal crew, not a representative census of all
workers.

## Trade

The Nile, Red Sea, Nubian, Mediterranean, and Levantine routes carried gold,
copper, timber, horses, chariots, glass, stone, grain, textiles, incense, luxury
goods, envoys, and letters. Commercial exchange, diplomatic gift, tribute,
taxation, military seizure, and colonial extraction remain separate categories.

## Science

Papyri, ostraca, tools, and buildings attest scribal education, accounting,
surveying, calendrics, medicine, metrology, architectural planning, and material
knowledge. Surviving texts document practical and learned traditions rather than
one centralized scientific institution. Drafts and corrections are especially
valuable because they expose process behind finished work.

## Technology

Stone and mud-brick construction, quarrying, transport, bronze tools, chariots,
shipbuilding, irrigation, storage, glass and faience, pigment preparation,
papyrus, limestone-flake ostraca, and specialist tomb cutting and painting
supported institutions. Tools and methods must be tied to dated contexts rather
than presented as timeless Egyptian inventions.

## Architecture

Temple precincts, pylons, courts, columned halls, palaces, houses, planned
settlements, rock-cut tombs, roads, quays, forts, and storage buildings were
added, revised, dismantled, and reinscribed across reigns. Stonebend translates
the visible phase logic and attribution problem, not literal pylons, obelisks,
or pharaonic avenues.

## Military

Campaigns, chariot forces, forts, garrisons, river movement, conscription,
tribute, deportation, and control of Nubia and parts of the Levant shaped the
period. Egyptian monumental accounts privilege royal victory. Conquest and
imperial extraction are historical cautions, not Permanent Gifts.

## Art

Royal and private sculpture, relief, painted tombs, jewelry, furniture, glass,
faience, textiles, informal sketches, and architectural programs combined
convention with innovation. Amarna art is a historically bounded royal program,
not a free-standing modern style palette.

## Language

Egyptian was written in monumental hieroglyphic and cursive hieratic forms;
written usage changed toward Late Egyptian in the later New Kingdom. Akkadian
cuneiform served much international diplomacy. Language, script, register,
author, copyist, and audience remain distinct metadata.

## Major texts

- The Amarna diplomatic letters, mostly in Akkadian cuneiform.
- Royal annals and building inscriptions, read as commissioned claims.
- Deir el-Medina ostraca and papyri: work, supply, absence, legal, letter,
  literary, and household records.
- Tomb and temple religious compositions, tied to their specific witnesses.
- Medical, administrative, mathematical, and literary papyri with date and provenance.
- The Egyptian version of the Ramesses II–Hattusili III treaty alongside the
  Hittite archive versions, without calling it the first treaty in history.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not the invention of architecture, labor records,
or drawing. It is the unusually inspectable conjunction of multi-reign building
phases, a named specialist work community, abundant day-to-day records, and
surviving drafts and corrections. Together they let a designer distinguish
commission, contributor, phase, revision, maintenance, erasure, and later reuse.

## Historical relationships

Egypt corresponded, exchanged, and fought with Hatti, Mitanni, Kassite
Babylonia, Assyria, Cyprus, and Levantine polities while governing Nubia and
Levantine territories through unequal systems. The parallel [Hittite dossier](HITTITE_EMPIRE.md)
preserves the other side of some shared diplomatic evidence. Co-placement does
not imply that each power held equal status throughout the Point.

## House interpretation

Stonebend asks what gives a civilization durable form. This evidence shows that
durability is multi-generational and contested: works are commissioned, built,
corrected, altered, attributed, erased, restored, and maintained by many people.
Current authority remains in the [Stonebend Constitution](../../../../../STONEBEND_CONSTITUTION_V2.md).

### Archaeological evidence

Use Thebes, Karnak, Luxor, Deir el-Bahri, Deir el-Medina, the royal valleys,
Amarna, Memphis, Pi-Ramesses, Nubian sites, and Levantine contexts by exact phase.
Do not use a late monument as generic evidence for all c. 1550–1200 BCE Egypt.

### Recommended House library placement

Place in the Stonebend Age I historical wing under public works, construction
records, drafting, measurement, and institutional memory. Object records should
link every phase and Gift to the Successive Works Academy.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*The Republic*, *Metaphysics*, *Meditations*, *The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions*, and *Process and Reality* remain unchanged. They may frame later
questions about form, revision, knowledge, and responsibility but cannot make
pharaonic kingship constitutional or decode Egyptian intentions.
