# Kassite Babylonia — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Kassite-ruled Babylonia / Middle Babylonian period  
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point V, Glaüshouse  
Date relevance: c. 1595–1155 BCE, with consolidated rule especially from c. 1475 BCE  
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs early chronology and archive-distribution refinement; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes administrative tablets, royal inscriptions,
letters, seals, buildings, craft, literary manuscripts, and stone monuments
called kudurrus or narus. Evidence is uneven: Nippur has thousands of tablets,
while Kassite levels at Babylon remain difficult to investigate.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs an obscure early dynasty, conquest of
the Sealand, a long Babylonian territorial state, great-king diplomacy, temporary
Assyrian occupation, and a later Elamite end. Kassite ethnicity, language,
institutions, and exact chronology cannot be inferred from royal names alone.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Glaüshouse receives durable custody, witness comparison,
scribal preservation, and longitudinal resource records—not royal land
privilege, divine curse, forced labor, temple sovereignty, or Clearance.

## Historical overview

The Hittite raid that ended Babylon's First Dynasty around 1595 BCE did not
immediately produce a well-documented Kassite state. The early transition is
obscure, and First Sealand rulers retained southern cities. Kassite-named kings
eventually controlled Babylon and consolidated the south by about 1475 BCE.

For several centuries, Kassite rulers maintained Babylonia as a major territorial
state. They adopted Akkadian and Sumerian scribal conventions, restored temples,
participated in great-king correspondence, and developed a new royal city at
Dur-Kurigalzu. Nippur's archives document land, personnel, grain, livestock,
textiles, rations, and provincial administration in exceptional detail.

Assyrian pressure intensified in the thirteenth century. Tukulti-Ninurta I
defeated Kashtiliashu IV and occupied Babylonian territory, but Kassite rule was
restored and survived beyond the Point's 1200 boundary. Elamite campaigns ended
the dynasty around 1155 BCE. The supplied terminal event is therefore not the
end of Kassite Babylonia.

## Chronology

- **After c. 1595 BCE:** post-First-Dynasty succession is obscure; early Kassite
  rulers and Sealand power overlap in difficult evidence.
- **By c. 1475 BCE:** Kassite rulers consolidate southern Babylonia.
- **Late fifteenth / early fourteenth century BCE:** Kurigalzu I is associated
  with Dur-Kurigalzu and renewed great-power connections.
- **Fourteenth century BCE:** Burnaburiash II and Kadashman-Enlil I correspond
  with Egyptian rulers in the Amarna archive.
- **Later fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE:** conflict and negotiation with
  Assyria become increasingly important.
- **c. 1225 BCE:** Tukulti-Ninurta I defeats Kashtiliashu IV and imposes a period
  of Assyrian control in Babylonia.
- **After 1200 BCE:** Kassite kings continue to rule after Assyrian withdrawal.
- **c. 1155 BCE:** Elamite campaigns conventionally end Kassite rule.

## Major figures

- **Agum II:** connected by a later royal text to restoration of Marduk's image;
  date and historical use require caution.
- **Kurigalzu I:** associated with Dur-Kurigalzu and major building activity.
- **Burnaburiash II and Kadashman-Enlil I:** participants in Amarna diplomacy.
- **Kurigalzu II and Nazi-Maruttash:** rulers involved in Assyrian-Babylonian conflict.
- **Kashtiliashu IV:** defeated and captured by Tukulti-Ninurta I.
- **Tukulti-Ninurta I:** Assyrian conqueror and adversarial source, not a Kassite king.

## Major cities

- **Babylon:** political and religious center whose Kassite layers remain poorly exposed.
- **Nippur:** major temple and provincial archive center with dense administrative evidence.
- **Dur-Kurigalzu:** purpose-built royal, palace, and temple center.
- **Ur, Uruk, Sippar, and Isin:** old cities with continuing institutions under changing conditions.
- **Sealand and southern Babylonia:** central to early consolidation.
- **Susa:** later findspot for some monuments removed during Elamite campaigns;
  findspot and original custody remain separate.

## Government

Kings governed through royal households, provincial officials, temples, city
institutions, land assignments, military obligations, and administrative
networks. Kassite elites adopted much Babylonian political and scribal practice
without erasing local continuity. Nippur's archive is unusually abundant and
cannot be treated as a universal model for every province or reign.

## Religion

Marduk, Enlil, and other Mesopotamian cults continued while Kassite divine names
and symbols entered royal practice. Temples held texts and monumental land
records whose divine imagery and curses protected claims. Glaüshouse preserves
custody history and meaning but does not translate sacred sanction into modern authority.

## Economy

Irrigation agriculture, land grants, temple and palace estates, grain, livestock,
textiles, craft, rations, labor obligations, dependent workers, military service,
and long-distance exchange appear in the records. Administrative detail exposes
both maintenance systems and coercive social hierarchy.

## Trade

Babylonia exchanged horses, textiles, grain, metals, precious materials, royal
gifts, and envoys with Egypt, Hatti, Mitanni, Assyria, Elam, and Dilmun. Amarna
letters emphasize royal gift exchange; they do not describe the whole economy.
Tribute, palace shipment, private trade, and military seizure remain distinct.

## Science

Sumerian and Akkadian scribal education, lexical lists, literary copying,
divination, administration, metrology, and learned archaism continued. Later
traditions credit Kassite-era scholarship with collecting and stabilizing texts,
but each proposed act of canonization requires its own evidence and date.

## Technology

Clay tablets and seals, stone monuments, irrigation, storage, textile and craft
production, horse and chariot systems, glazed architectural elements, and the
planned center at Dur-Kurigalzu supported institutions. Technical continuity is
regional and cumulative, not a bounded Kassite invention package.

## Architecture

Dur-Kurigalzu preserves palace and temple complexes, including a monumental
ziggurat core. Older Babylonian cities continued to be rebuilt. The Atlas uses
long institutional maintenance, archive context, and material phases rather
than copying ziggurats, temple plans, or divine-symbol monuments.

## Military

Kassite consolidation, Sealand conflict, border wars with Assyria, Babylon's
capture by Tukulti-Ninurta I, and later Elamite invasion shaped the period.
Captivity, deportation, forced labor, and victory claims are cautions. They do
not become healing, continuity, or Integrity Gifts.

## Art

Kudurru/naru monuments, cylinder seals, glazed elements, sculpture, and
archaizing inscriptions join land record, divine symbol, royal identity, and
temple custody. The conventional term “boundary stone” is misleading because
these monuments were generally kept in temples rather than set out in fields.

## Language

Akkadian and Sumerian remained the principal written languages. Kassite names,
some vocabulary, and divine names survive, but the Kassite language is sparsely
known. Cultural and political identity must not be reduced to a language label.

## Major texts

- Nippur administrative records concerning land, grain, livestock, textiles,
  rations, personnel, and offices.
- Kassite royal inscriptions and building records.
- Kudurru/naru monuments recording land grants or transactions.
- Amarna correspondence of Babylonian and Egyptian rulers.
- Literary, lexical, religious, and scholarly manuscripts with witness dates attached.
- Assyrian and Elamite accounts of conflict, retained as adversarial sources.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not the invention of preservation or property
records. It is the conjunction of a long-lived political order, durable
temple-custodied transaction monuments, dense provincial records, and sustained
scribal transmission. The archive supports longitudinal comparison while also
showing how custody can preserve unequal claims.

## Historical relationships

Kassite Babylonia participated in great-king diplomacy with Egypt, Hatti,
Mitanni, and Assyria and maintained relations with Elam and Dilmun. The parallel
[Middle Assyria dossier](MIDDLE_ASSYRIA.md) records invasion and temporary
occupation from the Assyrian side. Kassite rule survives after 1200 and ends on
a different timetable from Hattusa.

## House interpretation

Glaüshouse asks how a living system preserves or restores its integrity. Kassite
Babylonia shows preservation through adoption, copying, institutional custody,
and long administration, while also warning that continuity can preserve
privilege and coercion. Authority remains in the [Glaüshouse Constitution](../../../../../GLAUSHOUSE_CONSTITUTION_V2.md).

### Archaeological evidence

Use Nippur, Dur-Kurigalzu, Babylon, Ur, Uruk, Sippar, Isin, southern sites, and
Susa findspots by phase. Nippur's exceptional archive must remain identified as
a provincial concentration rather than a complete map of Babylonia.
