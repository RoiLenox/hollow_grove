# Babylon — Historical Dossier

Civilization: First Dynasty of Babylon within the Old Babylonian world<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point IV, Stonebend<br>
Date relevance: c. 1894–1595 BCE under the Middle Chronology; strongest Babylonian hegemony c. 1763–1750 BCE<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs chronology-system notation; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes royal inscriptions and year names, law
collections, letters, court and contract tablets, administrative archives,
school exercises, lexical lists, mathematical tablets, seals, buildings, and
objects. Evidence comes from many cities and cannot all be labeled evidence for
the city or government of Babylon.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs the First Dynasty, Hammurabi's
alliances and conquests, later contraction, scribal education, law, economy, and
the conventional 1595 BCE end from sources with unequal survival. Exact absolute
dates depend on High, Middle, Low, or Ultra-Low chronological systems.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Stonebend receives public legal reasoning, attributable
records, measures, and worked procedure—not royal sovereignty, unequal ancient
penalties, coerced labor, or a new source of law, Claim, Title, or Yield.

## Historical overview

After Ur III, dynasties at Isin and Larsa competed for southern Mesopotamian
cities while Babylon's First Dynasty gradually built a central Mesopotamian
territory. The dynasty begins around 1894 BCE in the Middle Chronology, but the
city was not the dominant power for most of its first two centuries. Rim-Sin of
Larsa conquered Isin around 1793 BCE; Hammurabi acceded at Babylon soon after and
initially operated among other powerful states, including Larsa, Eshnunna, Mari,
Assur, Yamhad, and Elam.

Through diplomacy, alliance, and campaigns, Hammurabi dismantled former partners
and rivals and gained control over much of Mesopotamia late in his reign. His
success made Babylon a durable political and cultural center, but the enlarged
territory was brief. Samsu-iluna faced revolts and lost much of the south.
Successors retained a reduced kingdom until a Hittite force sacked Babylon near
1595 BCE in the Middle Chronology.

The Point title “Old Babylonian World” is therefore stronger than “Babylonian
Empire.” It preserves the network of cities, archives, languages, courts,
schools, temples, households, canals, and rival kingdoms in which Babylon rose.

## Chronology

- **c. 2004–1763 BCE — Isin-Larsa background:** post-Ur III dynasties compete;
  much of this begins before Point IV and overlaps Babylon's early dynasty.
- **c. 1894 BCE — conventional beginning of Babylon's First Dynasty:** Sumu-abum
  and early rulers establish a still-limited kingdom.
- **c. 1822–1763 BCE — Rim-Sin of Larsa:** Larsa dominates much of the south
  before Hammurabi's conquest.
- **c. 1792–1750 BCE — Hammurabi:** alliances and late campaigns turn Babylon
  into the principal regional power; the law stele belongs near the reign's end.
- **c. 1749–1712 BCE — Samsu-iluna:** revolts, political contraction, and loss of
  southern territory show that Hammurabi's territorial order did not persist intact.
- **Later seventeenth century BCE:** Abi-eshuh, Ammi-ditana, Ammi-saduqa, and
  Samsu-ditana rule a smaller kingdom while scribal traditions continue.
- **c. 1595 BCE — Hittite sack in the Middle Chronology:** conventional end of
  the First Dynasty and Old Babylonian period; Kassite rule emerges later.

The Atlas retains Middle-Chronology dates to match the supplied coordinate and
must name that convention whenever a precise year is displayed.

## Major figures

- **Sumu-abum and Sumu-la-El:** early rulers associated with formation and growth.
- **Hammurabi:** king, diplomat, conqueror, builder, and commissioner of the
  famous law collection; not the inventor of law or the first lawgiver.
- **Samsu-iluna:** successor whose reign documents revolt, warfare, building,
  and the contraction of Babylonian control.
- **Abi-eshuh, Ammi-ditana, Ammi-saduqa, and Samsu-ditana:** later dynastic rulers.
- **Rim-Sin of Larsa:** essential contemporary and rival whose kingdom Hammurabi
  conquered; he is not a Babylonian king.
- **Zimri-Lim of Mari:** contemporary ruler whose palace archives expose
  diplomacy and regional politics before Hammurabi's conquest of Mari.

## Major cities

- **Babylon:** dynastic capital; early levels are difficult to excavate beneath
  later occupation and groundwater.
- **Sippar and Kish:** major northern Babylonian religious, judicial, economic,
  and scribal centers.
- **Borsippa:** important neighboring cult and urban center.
- **Nippur:** long-lived religious and scribal center under changing rulers.
- **Ur, Larsa, and Isin:** southern centers with their own dynasties and archives.
- **Eshnunna and Mari:** powerful contemporary states connected through diplomacy,
  trade, and war.
- **Susa:** findspot of the Hammurabi stele after its ancient removal from
  Babylonia; findspot and original display setting are not the same thing.

## Government

The king worked through palace, temple, city, provincial, military, judicial,
scribal, and household institutions. Officials managed land, canals, labor,
taxes, supplies, justice, correspondence, and military obligations. Local cities
retained archives and practices under changing rulers; Hammurabi's conquest did
not erase their institutional histories or produce one uniform bureaucracy.

The law stele presents the king as divinely authorized guardian of justice. Its
judgments are invaluable evidence but do not function like a modern exhaustive
statute book, and surviving court practice cannot be reduced to the stele alone.

## Religion

City gods, temples, offerings, divination, personal devotion, and royal building
were part of public and household life. The law stele depicts Hammurabi before
Shamash, god associated with justice. Marduk's prominence grew with Babylon but
later first-millennium theology must not be projected wholesale into this Point.

## Economy

Agriculture, irrigation, landholding, livestock, textiles, craft, trade, loans,
silver accounting, household property, temple and palace estates, dependent
labor, slavery, taxation, and military provisioning appear in the records. The
same archive can reveal precise obligations and deep social inequality. A design
use must preserve both facts.

## Trade

Tablets document sales, leases, inheritance, loans, interest, partnerships,
transport, grain, wool, textiles, livestock, houses, fields, and silver. River
and overland systems connected southern and northern Mesopotamia to Syria,
Anatolia, the Gulf, and Iran. Commercial exchange, palace requisition, tribute,
tax, diplomatic gift, and seizure are not interchangeable categories.

## Science

Old Babylonian school and scholarly tablets preserve sexagesimal place-value
calculation, multiplication and reciprocal tables, coefficients, metrology,
geometry, area and volume problems, commercial arithmetic, lexical lists, and
omen classification. These are procedural and educational traditions; they do
not prove one universal school system or a modern theory of mathematics.

## Technology

Clay tablets and envelopes, cylinder seals, styluses, measuring systems, canals,
boats, mud-brick construction, storage, textile production, metalwork, pottery,
and road or river messaging supported urban institutions. Material and textual
systems worked together: a contract needed witnesses and seals; a delivery
needed measures, custody, transport, and a receiving archive.

## Architecture

Temples, palaces, houses, streets, walls, gates, canals, quays, workshops, and
storehouses are documented across the Old Babylonian world. Because early
Babylon lies beneath later construction, the Atlas does not reconstruct a
complete Hammurabi-era city plan or turn a later Babylonian form into Point IV
architecture.

## Military

Alliance, siege, field campaign, rebellion, deportation, conscription, fortified
cities, and control of canals or supply appear throughout the period. Hammurabi's
territorial success was late and brief. Warfare and conquest are historical
context and caution, not Stonebend Gifts.

## Art

The law stele joins relief and inscription. Cylinder seals, terracotta plaques,
sculpture, jewelry, and manuscript layout circulated through court, temple,
school, workshop, and household settings. Repeated images must be sourced by
object, date, and place rather than used as generic “Babylonian” decoration.

## Language

Akkadian in its Old Babylonian forms was widely written, while Sumerian remained
central in schooling, scholarship, liturgy, and literary copying. Amorite names
and identities are visible, but the surviving archive is not a complete record
of every spoken language or mobile community.

## Major texts

- The stele conventionally called the **Code of Hammurabi**, better described in
  this Atlas as a monumental royal law collection or jurisprudential monument.
- Contracts, deeds, loans, inheritances, marriage agreements, court records,
  witness lists, sealed envelopes, and administrative tablets from many cities.
- Royal inscriptions and year names of First-Dynasty rulers.
- Mari letters and administrative texts, kept under their own archive and polity.
- Sumerian and Akkadian literary manuscripts, including Old Babylonian Gilgamesh
  material, with witness and proposed composition dates separate.
- Mathematical tables and problem texts through DCCMT and CDLI.
- Lexical lists, omen texts, prayers, hymns, and school exercises.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not “the invention of law” or “the invention of
mathematics.” It is the unusually rich conjunction of public royal
jurisprudence, everyday legal and commercial records, and reproducible scribal
problem practice. These sources let modern designers compare proclaimed rule,
documented transaction, taught procedure, and actual social limit.

## Historical relationships

The Point continues the post-Ur III world but does not make Babylon the direct
successor to every Ur III institution. Babylon interacted with Larsa, Isin,
Eshnunna, Mari, Assur, Yamhad, Elam, and Hittite Anatolia through shifting
alliances, trade, and war. The parallel [Old Assyria dossier](OLD_ASSYRIA.md)
preserves a distinct northern merchant and city-state archive.

## House interpretation

Stonebend asks what gives a civilization durable form. The Old Babylonian record
shows that durable institutions require public reasoning, attributable acts,
shared measures, reproducible procedure, and correction. It also shows why a
record or monument cannot legitimize hierarchy or create present authority.
Authority remains in the [Stonebend Constitution](../../../../../STONEBEND_CONSTITUTION_V2.md).

### Recommended House library placement

Place in the Stonebend Age I historical wing under jurisprudence, contracts,
public records, metrology, scribal education, mathematics, urban institutions,
and institutional inequality. Mari and other non-Babylonian archives retain
their polity and archive identities.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*The Republic*, *Metaphysics*, *Meditations*, *The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions*, and *Process and Reality* remain unchanged. They may frame later
questions about law, form, method, and revision; they do not turn Hammurabi's
royal justice into Hollow Grove law or make Babylonian procedure modern science.

## Research notes

- Record the chronological system beside every precise dynastic date.
- Distinguish Babylon, Babylonia, First-Dynasty territory, and the wider Old
  Babylonian world.
- Separate royal law collection, court practice, private contract, and school text.
- Keep social rank, slavery, coercion, and unequal penalties visible.
- Do not assign every Old Babylonian text or invention to the city of Babylon.
