# Old Assyria — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Old Assyrian city-state and merchant network<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point IV, Flynt<br>
Date relevance: especially c. 1900–1750/1700 BCE within the conventional c. 2000–1600 BCE period<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs date refinement and evidence-density caution; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes roughly 24,000 cuneiform tablets, most from
private household archives at Kültepe/Kanesh, along with seals, envelopes,
houses, streets, storage rooms, goods, burials, and a smaller body of royal
inscriptions from Assur. The evidence is exceptionally detailed but concentrated
in time and place.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs family firms, trade institutions,
credit, city government, Anatolian jurisdictions, gendered work, route geography,
political interruption, and chronology. The conventional Old Assyrian period
reaches c. 1600 BCE, but the best evidence clusters in the twentieth through
eighteenth centuries and is especially dense around c. 1895–1865.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Flynt receives route accounting, long-distance operational
correspondence, sealed risk and dispute records, and adaptive handoffs—not
merchant sovereignty, colonial entitlement, family command, or Capacity created by a tablet.

## Historical overview

Assur was a small independent city-state on the Tigris, positioned between larger
kingdoms and trade routes. Its ruler commonly used a title translated as
vice-regent or governor of the god Ashur rather than the ordinary Akkadian word
for king. A city assembly, City Hall, and annual `limmu` official shared important
institutional roles whose exact balance changed over time.

Merchants from Assur organized trade into central Anatolia. At Kanesh, modern
Kültepe, they lived in a lower-town merchant quarter called a `kārum` alongside
Anatolian residents. They used local houses and material culture while bringing
Old Assyrian cuneiform, clay envelopes, and cylinder-seal practices. Tin and
textiles moved toward Anatolia and silver or gold returned; letters, loans,
partnerships, taxes, lawsuits, caravan accounts, and household disputes kept the
distributed system legible.

This was not a simple state colony. Assyrian merchants operated inside Anatolian
polities, paid local taxes, negotiated institutions and jurisdiction, married and
worked with local people, and adopted material practices. The modern phrase
“trading colonies” remains common in scholarship, but Atlas design uses `merchant
community`, `trading settlement`, or `kārum` with explanation to avoid importing
modern territorial assumptions.

## Chronology

- **c. 2025–1950 BCE — Puzur-Ashur I and early independent dynasty:** background
  to Point IV and emergence after Ur III hegemony.
- **c. 1974–1935 BCE — Erishum I:** royal inscriptions, building, trade reforms,
  and prominence of the limmu office; partly before Point IV.
- **c. 1950–1836 BCE — Kültepe Level II in common dating:** richest merchant
  archives and intense Assur–Anatolia trade.
- **c. 1895–1865 BCE:** especially dense written documentation within Level II.
- **c. 1836/1835 BCE:** destructive fire at Kanesh preserves houses and archives;
  cause is often understood as attack but must retain archaeological caution.
- **Level Ib, after c. 1835 to eighteenth century BCE:** settlement and trade
  resume; archives are less abundant.
- **1808/1807 BCE — Shamshi-Adad I takes Assur:** native dynastic and city
  arrangements are disrupted within a larger northern Mesopotamian kingdom.
- **After Shamshi-Adad's death:** native rule returns and trade continues into the
  late eighteenth century, but records become much thinner.
- **late eighteenth century to c. 1600 BCE:** conventional period label continues
  while textual and artistic evidence is sparse.

The Atlas records “Full period label / Uneven evidence,” not a false claim of
equally documented activity through 1600 BCE.

## Major figures

- **Puzur-Ashur I and Erishum I:** early rulers connected to independence,
  building, city institutions, and the flourishing trade system.
- **Ikunum, Sargon I, Puzur-Ashur II, and Naram-Sin of Assur:** dynastic rulers
  documented through eponyms, inscriptions, and merchant-era chronology.
- **Shamshi-Adad I:** Amorite ruler who captured Assur and incorporated it into a
  larger kingdom; not simply another native Old Assyrian merchant king.
- **Imdi-ilum:** merchant named in a surviving caravan account, useful as one
  documented operator rather than a representative of every firm.
- **Women, wives, daughters, agents, and textile producers named in letters:**
  historically recoverable participants whose individual archives and roles must
  be preserved rather than collapsed into anonymous “merchant families.”

## Major cities

- **Assur / Ashur:** city-state, cult center, political home, and transit point;
  early second-millennium levels remain archaeologically difficult.
- **Kanesh / Kültepe:** Anatolian kingdom and principal merchant-archive center;
  the lower town included the kārum and mixed residential neighborhoods.
- **Hattusa, Alişar, Acemhöyük, and other Anatolian centers:** parts of the wider
  network under different local political conditions.
- **Northern Mesopotamian and Taurus route stages:** crossings and settlements
  whose geography is reconstructed from texts and archaeology, not one preserved road map.

## Government

At Assur, ruler, assembly, City Hall, limmu official, judges, and merchant
institutions worked together. The ruler's “vice-regent of Ashur” title emphasized
service to the city-god. City and commercial institutions could issue decisions
and represent Assur abroad.

In Anatolia, merchants operated under local kings, city institutions, taxes,
customs, treaties, and courts. Kārum authorities coordinated merchants and
disputes but did not erase Anatolian sovereignty. Jurisdiction could vary by
person, matter, and place—an especially important Flynt handoff lesson.

## Religion

The god Ashur shared the city's name and stood at the center of political and
religious identity. Merchants carried Mesopotamian devotional and sealing
traditions while living amid Anatolian gods, rituals, and imagery. Letters,
oaths, seals, and occasional incantations show practice but do not form a complete theology.

## Economy

Private capital, family firms, partnerships, credit, loans, silver balances,
textile production, caravan costs, agents, taxes, customs, storage, and dispute
resolution sustained the network. Women in Assur produced and dispatched
textiles and managed business and household matters through correspondence.
Neither “private” nor “family” means free of legal constraint, labor inequality, or conflict.

## Trade

Tin and textiles traveled from or through Assur to Anatolia, where merchants
received silver and gold and participated in copper exchange. A caravan required
donkeys, drivers, guides, clothing, equipment, fodder, tolls, customs, lodging,
security, and weeks of travel. Accounts record investment and error as well as profit.

Routes connected broader supply systems reaching beyond Assur. The provenance of
tin and textiles and the reach of indirect networks must not be simplified into
direct Assyrian control from source to destination.

## Science

There is no surviving technical treatise that defines an Old Assyrian science.
Practical knowledge appears in writing, arithmetic, weights, interest, cost,
route timing, contract formulae, witness procedure, and risk calculation.
Small discrepancies and erasures in accounts are evidence of human work and correction.

## Technology

Old Assyrian cuneiform used a relatively streamlined sign repertoire and frequent
syllabic spellings. Tablets, clay envelopes, cylinder seals, stamp seals,
weights, textiles, metal, donkey caravans, storage rooms, paved streets, drainage,
and local timber-supported mud-brick houses made the network operational.

## Architecture

At Kültepe, Assyrian and Anatolian residents lived in closely built local-style
houses with mud-brick walls on stone foundations and timber supports. Houses
included living, storage, and archive rooms. Streets were paved and could include
pedestrian edges and drains. The built environment was Anatolian, not a replica of Assur.

## Military

Political instability, attack, confiscation, route closure, local war, and the
rise of Shamshi-Adad affected merchants. The archives document pressure and
continuity more clearly than a coherent military system. Flynt inherits risk,
route, stores, communication, and handoff practice—not a merchant-warrior identity.

## Art

Cylinder and stamp seals carried identities and witnessed acts. Early impressions
use Mesopotamian presentation scenes; later “Assyro-Cappadocian” styles combine
Mesopotamian genres with Anatolian motifs and arrangements. The mixed visual
record demonstrates exchange and local adoption, not a pure House style.

## Language

Old Assyrian is a dialect of Akkadian written in cuneiform. Letters and contracts
also preserve Anatolian names, terms, and cross-cultural interaction. The archive
reflects literate commercial households and scribes but not every language spoken at Kanesh.

## Major texts

- Private letters between merchants, wives, relatives, partners, and agents.
- Caravan accounts recording cargo, costs, guides, animals, fodder, fees, and balances.
- Loans, partnerships, commercial notes, quittances, and investment records.
- Court depositions, lawsuits, verdicts, witness records, and sealed envelopes.
- Marriage, inheritance, household, and personal documents.
- Limmu lists and royal inscriptions used for Assur chronology and government.
- A small number of literary and magical texts within merchant archives.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed contribution is not “the invention of trade,” credit, accounting,
or business. It is an extraordinarily dense private archive showing how long
routes depended on household correspondence, shared risk, explicit costs,
sealed evidence, local jurisdiction, and recoverable handoffs.

## Historical relationships

Assur connected southern supply networks to independent Anatolian kingdoms while
remaining politically smaller than many contemporary states. Its merchants and
Anatolian residents worked, litigated, married, and made material culture together.
The parallel [Babylon dossier](BABYLON.md) includes political relations with
Assur and Shamshi-Adad but does not absorb the Old Assyrian archive into Babylon.

## House interpretation

Flynt asks what a system can do under pressure. Old Assyrian archives answer at
the handoff level: capacity depends on loads, routes, stores, money, messages,
local law, trust, repair, delay, and proof of receipt. They also show that a
far-reaching network can be operationally strong without possessing every place
it enters. Authority remains in the [Flynt Constitution](../../../../../FLYNT_CONSTITUTION_V2.md),
whose constitutional House Law is the **Law of Capacity**.

### Archaeological evidence

Prioritize Kültepe Level II and Ib houses, archive rooms, streets, drains,
storage, seals, goods, and the upper mound, alongside Assur's limited contemporary
evidence and other Anatolian trading settlements. A text and an object from the
same site are not automatically from the same household or phase.

## Research notes

- Attach every tablet to archive household, level, date, genre, and editor where known.
- Distinguish conventional Old Assyrian period from the narrower evidence-rich trade phase.
- Explain `kārum`; do not imply modern colonial sovereignty.
- Expand women's letters, textile production, and household management without
  treating one archive as universal.
- Separate Assur institutions, Anatolian local government, and merchant-quarter authority.
- Keep the late eighteenth-century to c. 1600 evidence gap visible.
