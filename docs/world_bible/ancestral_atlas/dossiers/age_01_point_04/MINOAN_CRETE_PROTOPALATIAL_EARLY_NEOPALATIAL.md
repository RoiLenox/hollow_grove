# Minoan Crete, Protopalatial to Early Neopalatial — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Protopalatial and early Neopalatial Crete, conventionally called Minoan<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point IV, Glaüshouse<br>
Date relevance: c. 1900–1600 BCE<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs date, terminology, and institutional-model refinement; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** is principally archaeological: court-centered building
phases, storage rooms, workshops, streets, drains, water features, sealings,
Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A documents, pottery, plaster, tools, botanical
and animal remains, and imported materials. Neither script provides a deciphered
historical narrative.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs political scale, administration,
redistribution, ritual, social hierarchy, trade, destruction, and rebuilding.
The terms `Minoan`, `palace`, `Protopalatial`, and `Neopalatial` are modern
analytical tools; older priest-king and centralized palace-economy models are not
treated as automatic consensus.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Glaüshouse receives contextual records, integrated stores
and workshops, and maintainable light, water, and circulation—not a palace
hierarchy, guessed translation, universal goddess, labyrinth, or clinical Clearance.

## Historical overview

Around the opening of Point IV, monumental court-centered compounds appeared or
developed at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, and other centers. Their central courts,
storage, workshops, circulation, ritual settings, and administrative materials
made them important institutional nodes. Archaeologists conventionally call this
the Protopalatial or First Palace period.

The word “palace” does not prove that each complex housed a king, governed a
bounded territory, or operated the same redistributive system. Sturt Manning
notes that early interpretations often projected later Linear B palace economies
back onto earlier Crete. Site, phase, archive, room, and object context must
therefore remain attached to every claim.

Around 1700 BCE, many first-palace systems were destroyed or substantially
rebuilt. The event was neither necessarily simultaneous across the island nor
securely caused by one earthquake, invasion, or revolution. Early Neopalatial
development followed, with Linear A administration, expanded building systems,
specialized craft, and Mediterranean exchange. This dossier stops at 1600 BCE;
later Late Minoan IA–IB achievements and the c. 1450 destruction horizon belong elsewhere.

## Chronology

- **c. 1900 BCE:** conventional transition from late Prepalatial communities to
  Protopalatial court-centered complexes; exact site dates vary.
- **Middle Minoan IB–II, broadly early second millennium BCE:** first-palace
  building, Cretan Hieroglyphic and early Linear A use, Kamares pottery,
  sealings, storage, craft, and interregional exchange.
- **c. 1700 BCE:** conventional first-palace destruction and rebuilding boundary;
  timing, causes, and island-wide uniformity remain debated.
- **c. 1700–1600 BCE:** early Neopalatial phase, principally Late Minoan IA in
  one high chronological alignment, with Linear A and renewed institutional complexes.
- **After 1600 BCE:** later Neopalatial development continues beyond this Point
  and must not be imported wholesale into the dossier.

Ceramic relative chronology and absolute dates differ among scholars. The Atlas
marks the assignment Full / Transforming and names the phase used by each source.

## Major figures

No named ruler, official, author, builder, navigator, physician, or religious
specialist is securely recoverable. Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A preserve
sign systems and administrative patterns without a deciphered language.

**Minos** belongs to later Greek legend, not a recovered Bronze Age king list.
**Sir Arthur Evans** is central to modern excavation, chronology, restoration,
and the influential construction of “Minoan civilization”; his interpretations
and concrete reconstructions are themselves subjects of source criticism.

## Major cities

- **Knossos:** largest and longest-studied court-centered complex, heavily shaped
  in modern perception by Evans's excavation and restoration.
- **Phaistos:** major southern complex with distinct architectural and ceramic sequences.
- **Malia:** central court, storage, workshops, town, and strong evidence for
  continuity across the first-palace threshold.
- **Petras and Monastiraki:** important for Protopalatial administration and regional comparison.
- **Palaikastro / Roussolakkos:** eastern settlement whose urban features develop
  at the late Protopalatial and early Neopalatial transition.
- **Kommos, Archanes, and other settlements:** evidence for ports, craft, ritual,
  settlement hierarchy, and regional diversity.

## Government

Court-centered complexes coordinated some combination of storage, craft,
gathering, ritual, recordkeeping, and exchange. Sealings and scripts indicate
administrative activity, but undeciphered records do not reveal office titles,
constitutional structure, or complete territorial reach. Knossos cannot stand
for every Cretan institution, and later Linear B archives cannot silently fill
the gap.

## Religion

Court complexes, peak sanctuaries, caves, springs, deposits, figurines, vessels,
seals, and images attest ritual practice. The meanings of horns, bulls, female
figures, axes, plants, and processions remain context-dependent. A universal
mother goddess, priest-king, or Minotaur cult is not established.

## Economy

Agriculture, herding, fishing, storage, pottery, textiles, metalwork, stone
vessels, seal production, building, and exchange supported households and
institutions. Large storage rooms and containers are real; whether they represent
central redistribution, elite display, emergency reserve, tax, offering, or
mixed functions must be tested by site and context.

## Trade

Crete participated in Aegean and eastern Mediterranean exchange involving the
Cyclades, mainland Greece, Cyprus, Egypt, and the Levant. Timber, food, cloth,
oil, pottery, stone vessels, and crafted goods were exchanged for metals, ivory,
fine stones, and other materials. A foreign object does not by itself establish
a named route, state treaty, colony, or Cretan control of the sea.

## Science

There are no deciphered scientific treatises. Practical knowledge is preserved
in building geometry, drainage, water supply, lighting, ventilation, storage,
agriculture, ceramic firing, metallurgy, stonework, seal carving, textile
production, navigation, and administrative classification.

## Technology

Ashlar and rubble masonry, timber reinforcement, plaster, paved courts, stairs,
corridors, light wells, drains, water conduits, storage jars, sealings, clay
documents, pottery wheels, looms, metal tools, stone vessels, and ships supported
complex settlements. Not every feature occurs at every site or belongs to the same phase.

## Architecture

Court-centered compounds integrated large gathering spaces with magazines,
workshops, ritual areas, stairs, corridors, light wells, drains, and repeated
building phases. The spatial inheritance is integrated maintenance: people,
water, air, light, stores, records, repair, and access must move through the same
living system. Hollow Grove does not reproduce Knossos or a “labyrinth district.”

## Military

Weapons, watch positions, strategic settlements, and conflict evidence exist,
but the lack of massive fortification at some sites cannot prove a peaceful
thalassocracy. Named fleets, commanders, campaigns, and island-wide security
institutions are not recoverable for the Point.

## Art

Kamares pottery, seals, jewelry, stone vessels, metalwork, plaster, and early
fresco traditions show specialized material practice and mobile visual forms.
Later marine-style pottery and famous reconstructed fresco scenes must retain
their later phases and restoration histories.

## Language

The spoken languages are not recovered. Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A are
related to administrative change but neither offers a deciphered Point IV
language corpus. Linear A sign values cannot be converted into a secure language
or translated doctrine. Linear B is later and records Greek under different political conditions.

## Major texts

No readable narrative, law collection, medical text, or named literature survives.
The primary record set includes:

- Cretan Hieroglyphic tablets, bars, labels, and sealings;
- Linear A tablets, nodules, sealings, and vessel inscriptions;
- storage and workshop inventories inferred cautiously from sign and context patterns;
- building plans, phase records, drains, light and circulation systems;
- pottery sequences, residues, tools, seals, and provenienced imports.

Every catalogue must show `undeciphered` prominently and separate sign reading,
commodity interpretation, and modern reconstruction.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed contribution is the integration of record, storage, workshop,
water, light, circulation, and repair inside complex institutional environments.
The Atlas does not claim that Crete invented plumbing, palaces, writing, trade,
fresco, or environmental design.

## Historical relationships

This dossier continues the [late Prepalatial Point III dossier](../age_01_point_03/MINOAN_CRETE.md)
without overwriting it. It shows institutional transformation across c. 1900 and
c. 1700. Contact with Egypt and Near Eastern states is reviewed object by object;
Point co-placement with Babylon, Egypt, and Old Assyria is not proof of direct relations.

## House interpretation

Glaüshouse asks how a living system preserves or restores its integrity. This
slice contributes the practical insight that institutional integrity is spatial:
records, materials, people, air, water, light, and maintenance must remain in
relationship. Its undeciphered archives also teach epistemic integrity—preserving
evidence without pretending to understand more than it supports. Authority
remains in the [Glaüshouse Constitution](../../../../../GLAUSHOUSE_CONSTITUTION_V2.md).

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

There are no translated narrative primary texts. The historical wing catalogs
excavation contexts, building phases, Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A documents,
sealings, pottery, tools, water and drainage features, workshop remains, botanical
and animal evidence, and imported materials.

The Cambridge excerpt from Ester Salgarella's *Writing in Bronze Age Crete*
provides an explicit phase-and-script table and a warning that much clay evidence
survived accidentally through destructive fire.

### Archaeological evidence

Prioritize documented Protopalatial and early Neopalatial contexts at Knossos,
Phaistos, Malia, Petras, Monastiraki, Palaikastro, Kommos, Archanes, and related
sites. Record restoration and excavation history, especially where Evans's
reconstruction affects what a visitor sees.

### Major modern scholarship

- Sturt W. Manning, “Formation of the Palaces,” in *The Cambridge Companion to
  the Aegean Bronze Age* (2008).
- Ilse Schoep, *The Administration of Neopalatial Crete* and related work on
  Middle Minoan administration and institutional diversity.
- Ester Salgarella, *Aegean Linear Script(s): Rethinking the Relationship between
  Linear A and Linear B* (2020) and *Writing in Bronze Age Crete*.
- John C. McEnroe, *Architecture of Minoan Crete* (2010).
- [Metropolitan Museum, Minoan Crete](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/minoan-crete),
  used with the centralization cautions above.
- [Ashmolean, Creating the First Europeans](https://www.ashmolean.org/creating-the-first-europeans),
  for critical history of Evans's influential model.

### Recommended House library placement

Place in the Glaüshouse Age I historical wing under conservation archaeology,
undeciphered records, institutional maintenance, storage, workshops, water,
light, ventilation, access, material systems, and historiography. Later Linear B
records and c. 1450 events remain on later shelves.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*On the Origin of Species*, *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, *The Tree of
Knowledge*, and the living-systems collection remain unchanged. They may support
comparison about integration and maintenance; they cannot translate Linear A,
prove an autopoietic palace, or diagnose an ancient society.

## Research notes

- Tag every feature `Protopalatial`, `early Neopalatial`, or later, with site.
- Do not assign all c. 1700 destructions one date or cause without evidence.
- Keep redistribution, hierarchy, and territorial control as tested models.
- Preserve `Minoan` as a modern label and Minos as legend.
- Expose Evans-era restoration and interpretation when using Knossos imagery.
- Expand rural, household, and non-palatial evidence in the next review.
