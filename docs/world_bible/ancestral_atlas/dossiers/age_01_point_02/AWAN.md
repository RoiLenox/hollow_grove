# Awan — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Awan<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point II, Flynt<br>
Date relevance: c. 2350–2150 BCE within a broader late-third-millennium horizon<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs historical, location, and date audit; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** comes mostly from Mesopotamian royal inscriptions,
later copies, a later king list from Susa, a difficult treaty, and archaeology
from Susiana and western Iran that cannot always be attributed specifically to
Awan. Awan's location is unknown.

**Scholarly interpretation** treats Awan variously as a place, dynastic seat,
state, or retrospective label within early Elamite history. “Awan dynasty” is
useful source language, not proof of one continuous territory, constitution,
or archaeologically bounded culture.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Flynt receives source-critical coalition, treaty, and
contested-command problems—not a guessed map, invented Awan technology,
imperial warfare, or constitutional authority.

## Historical overview

Awan appears in Mesopotamian traditions as a western Iranian political center
and adversary. A later Old Babylonian tablet from Susa lists twelve “kings of
Awan,” while several names also occur in traditions of conflict with Sargon
and Rimush. In Naram-Sin's period, a treaty written in Elamite with cuneiform
signs was found at Susa and may have involved an Awan ruler, perhaps Hita.

The evidence is asymmetrical. Akkadian kings describe defeat and subjugation;
those claims establish contact and conflict but not the opponent's internal
view or durable Akkadian control. Puzur-Inshushinak later ruled Susa and used
the title king of Awan, but current chronologies often place him around
c. 2120–2100 BCE—at or after the supplied Point's c. 2150 endpoint.

## Chronology

- **Mid-third millennium BCE:** Awan appears in king-list and conflict
  traditions; the historical value and dates of individual entries vary.
- **Sargon and Rimush, late twenty-fourth to early twenty-third century BCE:**
  rulers associated with Awan/Elam appear in Akkadian campaign traditions.
- **Naram-Sin, twenty-third century BCE:** probable treaty relationship between
  the Akkadian sphere at Susa and an Elamite or Awan ruler.
- **Puzur-Inshushinak, often c. 2120–2100 BCE:** ruler of Susa and king of Awan,
  important to the transition beyond Point II rather than evidence for its
  whole span.
- **Šimaški ascendancy:** follows Awan in the supplied Point III lineage and in
  the retrospective Susa list, though the exact political transition is not a
  simple dynastic handoff.

## Major figures

- **Luhhishan:** name associated with the Awan list and Sargonic conflict;
  spelling, order, and historical reconstruction vary.
- **Hishep-ratep / Hisheprashini:** another ruler associated with the Awan list
  and Akkadian campaigns; variant readings and source order require explicit
  citation.
- **Hita:** possible Awan ruler connected by scholarship to the Naram-Sin-era
  treaty; the identification is uncertain.
- **Puzur-Inshushinak / Kutik-Inshushinak:** last named Awan king in later
  tradition and ruler at Susa, but at or beyond the Point's closing boundary.

No single figure securely represents c. 2350–2150 BCE from an Awan-authored
continuous archive.

## Major cities

- **Awan:** exact location unknown; proposed placements in western Iran or the
  central-western Zagros remain hypotheses.
- **Susa:** source of the treaty, later king list, and Puzur-Inshushinak
  monuments. Susa is not thereby proved to be Awan itself.
- **Anshan and other western Iranian centers:** part of wider Elamite history,
  but their relationship to Awan varied and must not be assumed.
- **Marhashi/Barahshi:** a neighboring or allied eastern power in Akkadian
  texts, not a synonym for Awan.

The absence of a located capital prevents a secure Awan city plan, route map,
or architectural corpus.

## Government

Sources imply rulers capable of warfare, coalition, and treaty. The later Susa
list presents a dynasty, while Akkadian inscriptions describe rulers of Elam
and defeated places. Offices, succession rules, taxation, local government,
and relationships among Awan, Susa, Anshan, and other polities remain obscure.

## Religion

The Naram-Sin-era treaty invokes divine witnesses, and Susa had documented
cults including Inshushinak and Narundi. The political and religious relation
of those cults to Awan is not fully resolved. Puzur-Inshushinak's later
dedications are important transition evidence, not a complete Awan theology.

## Economy

Western Iran provided or transmitted stone, metals, timber, livestock, and
agricultural products valued in Mesopotamia. Campaign and exchange evidence
shows material interest but does not disclose an Awan treasury, tax system, or
household economy. Regional archaeological data must retain its exact site
rather than being relabeled “Awan.”

## Trade

Routes connected Susiana, the Zagros, the Iranian plateau, Mesopotamia, and the
Gulf. Because Awan is unlocated, its precise route control cannot be mapped.
The reviewed Gift therefore concerns coalition and handoff under frontier
pressure, not ownership of a specific trade corridor.

## Science

The treaty demonstrates cross-language diplomatic writing: Elamite expressed
with cuneiform signs. Its interpretation is difficult, and it cannot establish
a general Awan school system, scientific corpus, or universal administrative
practice.

## Technology

No technology can be assigned securely to Awan as an isolated archaeological
culture. Regional sealing, metalwork, stonework, transport, storage, weapons,
and construction are relevant only with site, phase, and attribution attached.
Puzur-Inshushinak's Linear Elamite monuments belong to the closing transition,
not the full Point.

## Architecture

There is no securely identified Awan palace, capital, settlement plan, or
architectural style. Susa's buildings are essential evidence for Susiana, but
must not be renamed Awan architecture. Flynt's manifestation therefore derives
from operational relationships and explicit uncertainty rather than historical
form.

## Military

Military conflict is the most visible relationship in Akkadian sources. They
describe defeated rulers, coalitions, captured forces, and campaigns in Elam
and western Iran. These are adversarial royal claims. They establish pressure
and organized resistance but do not reveal neutral Awan tactics, equipment
systems, force size, or rules of command.

## Art

No bounded Awan visual style is secure. The later inscribed statues and votive
objects of Puzur-Inshushinak at Susa join Akkadian, Elamite, and local forms and
scripts near the period boundary. They should be studied as transition objects,
not copied as the look of Awan.

## Language

The spoken language or languages of Awan are unknown. The treaty is an early
Elamite text in cuneiform and is difficult to interpret. Names preserved in
Mesopotamian writing do not supply a full linguistic corpus. Linear Elamite
under Puzur-Inshushinak is later boundary evidence.

## Major texts

- Old Akkadian royal inscriptions of Sargon, Rimush, and Naram-Sin, including
  later copies, used as adversarial sources.
- The Naram-Sin-era Elamite treaty found at Susa, with uncertain contracting
  ruler and difficult interpretation.
- The Old Babylonian Awan and Šimaški king list from Susa, a retrospective and
  non-exhaustive dynastic record.
- Puzur-Inshushinak inscriptions and bilingual objects from Susa, treated as
  closing-transition evidence.
- Later Sumerian King List manuscripts, used for political memory rather than
  literal regnal chronology.

## Major discoveries or innovations

No first invention is assigned. The strongest evidence-based contribution is
a frontier problem set: multiple regional powers coordinate under pressure,
imperial claims fail to describe actual capacity, and cross-language treaty
protocol becomes necessary. The uncertainty is itself an implementation
constraint.

## Historical relationships

Awan is paired with [Akkad](AKKAD.md) because conflict and a probable treaty
connect the two historical systems. Susa moved through changing relations with
Akkadian and western Iranian authorities. Puzur-Inshushinak's late rise and
the later Šimaški tradition connect this dossier forward to Point III, but the
transition is neither fully dated nor institutionally documented.

### Archaeological evidence

Settlement, fortification, burial, seal, ceramic, metal, stone, environmental,
and route evidence from Susiana and the Zagros belongs in the regional
collection. Because Awan is unlocated, evidence remains catalogued by excavated
site rather than retrofitted to a hypothetical capital.

## Research notes

- Awan's location is unknown; no map pin should appear as certain.
- The Awan king list is later, schematic, and non-exhaustive.
- Names and their order vary across sources and modern transliteration.
- Most conflict evidence is Akkadian royal self-presentation.
- The Naram-Sin treaty's ruler identification and interpretation remain
  difficult.
- Puzur-Inshushinak likely lies at or after c. 2150 and is transition evidence.
- Awan, Elam, Susa, Anshan, and Marhashi are not interchangeable labels.
