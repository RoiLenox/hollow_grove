# Mature Indus Civilization — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Mature Indus / Mature Harappan Civilization<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point II, Glaüshouse<br>
Date relevance: c. 2350–2150 BCE within a broader c. 2600–1900 BCE phase<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** is archaeological: settlements, streets, buildings,
wells, drains, reservoirs, tools, workshops, burials, plant and animal remains,
seals, short inscriptions, weights, and traded materials. The script remains
undeciphered.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs urban coordination, economy,
politics, and belief without named offices or readable narrative texts. Shared
material standards do not by themselves prove a single centralized state.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Glaüshouse receives water-integrity, measurement, and
distributed-maintenance principles—not a claim that drains equal modern public
health, that every city had identical systems, or that one Harappan city can
stand for the whole civilization.

## Historical overview

During the Mature Harappan phase, a very large region contained major cities,
smaller towns, villages, craft sites, ports or coastal settlements, and rural
production. Seals, weights, ceramics, brick proportions, ornaments, and craft
traditions show extensive coordination and exchange. Urban form was not
uniform: Mohenjo-daro's wells and street drains, Dholavira's reservoirs and
stone construction, and Harappa's changing neighborhoods reflect distinct
materials, water conditions, and histories.

The supplied Point sits well inside the mature urban phase. It can therefore
review water and drainage evidence that Point I deliberately kept beyond the
Early Indus boundary.

## Chronology

- **c. 2600 BCE:** transition into Mature Harappan urban forms and widely
  shared material conventions; dates vary by site and model.
- **c. 2350–2150 BCE:** the Atlas Point, within the major urban and
  interregional exchange system.
- **After c. 2000–1900 BCE:** regional transformation and deurbanization in
  many areas; this later transition does not belong to Point II.

Site phases must be used alongside the broad chronology. A feature dated at
one city cannot automatically define the same century at another.

## Major figures

No named ruler, engineer, physician, merchant, or author is securely
recoverable. Proposed readings of the Indus script have not achieved scholarly
consensus. The absence of names is a limit of the evidence, not proof that the
civilization had no officials, specialists, or personal identities.

## Major cities

- **Harappa:** long stratified sequence, large urban center, workshops,
  neighborhoods, walls, drains, seals, weights, and cemeteries.
- **Mohenjo-daro:** extensive baked-brick city with wells, bathing floors,
  streets, drains, and monumental or special buildings.
- **Dholavira:** stone-built regional center with reservoirs, drainage,
  fortifications, differentiated urban sectors, craft, and a long occupation.
- **Rakhigarhi and Ganweriwala:** major eastern centers, with excavation and
  preservation histories differing from better published sites.
- **Lothal and coastal Gujarat centers:** craft and exchange nodes; the famous
  “dockyard” interpretation at Lothal remains debated and should be labeled.

Hundreds of smaller sites were essential to agriculture, pastoralism, craft,
raw-material movement, and regional variation.

## Government

Large cities and wide standards required coordination, but recognizable royal
palaces, royal tombs, victory inscriptions, and readable administrative texts
are absent. Models range from city-based elites and merchant or landholding
institutions to councils, ritual authorities, and broader heterarchical
systems. The Atlas records coordinated outcomes while leaving the governing
mechanism unresolved.

## Religion

Seals, figurines, baths, burials, special buildings, animal and plant motifs,
and fire-related installations are relevant evidence. Their meanings are
debated. Named gods, a “priest-king,” direct Vedic continuity, and universal
ritual interpretations are not accepted as established facts in this dossier.

## Economy

Farming, herding, fishing, gathering, textile work, ceramics, bead making,
shell craft, metallurgy, stone working, building, transport, and exchange
supported urban and rural communities. Workshops varied in scale and location.
Standard weights and seals suggest monitored transactions, but the exact legal
or institutional framework is unknown.

## Trade

Raw materials and finished goods moved across the Greater Indus region and to
Oman/Magan and Mesopotamia. Carnelian, shell, metals, stone, timber, ceramics,
seals, and weights participate in this evidence. Mesopotamian texts mention
Meluhha, and Indus-related objects occur west of South Asia, but the identity,
route, and agency of each object require provenance and compositional study.

## Science

Weights, measured construction, craft recipes, water engineering, spatial
planning, and sign use reveal practical knowledge. The undeciphered script
prevents recovery of technical treatises or named theories. Claims about a
single unit system, geometry, medicine, or astronomy require object-specific
and metrological evidence.

## Technology

Baked and mud brick, wells, drains, settling features, reservoirs, bathing
floors, carts, boats, copper and bronze work, bead drilling, shell working,
faience, seals, cubical weights, and regionally adapted building practices are
well represented. Maintenance access and repeated repair are as important to
Glaüshouse as initial construction.

## Architecture

Street grids, lanes, courtyard houses, walls, gates, wells, drains, raised
areas, workshops, and special buildings vary across cities. Dholavira's stone,
reservoirs, and internally differentiated plan are not a universal Harappan
template. “Citadel,” “granary,” “college,” and similar excavation labels may
encode early interpretations rather than certain ancient functions.

## Military

Fortifications, gates, walls, weapons, and trauma evidence require study, but
there is no readable campaign history or securely reconstructed army. The
older stereotype of an entirely peaceful civilization is as unsupported as an
invented militarized state. Warfare does not drive the Point II Glaüshouse
inheritance.

## Art

Steatite seals, molded and modeled figures, painted ceramics, jewelry, beads,
shell objects, metal sculpture, and patterned design show skilled production
and regional connections. The conventional “priest-king,” “dancing girl,” and
“unicorn” labels are modern catalog terms, not recovered ancient names.

## Language

The language or languages of the Indus Civilization are unknown. The script is
found mainly in short sequences on seals, tablets, pottery, and other objects.
No decipherment has gained consensus, so the Atlas will not invent titles,
translations, literature, or direct doctrinal continuity.

## Major texts

There is no deciphered textual corpus. The primary record set comprises:

- inscribed seals, tablets, tags, pottery marks, and the Dholavira signboard;
- standardized weights and measures recorded with exact context;
- excavation plans, stratigraphy, field registers, conservation records, and
  archaeometric studies;
- Mesopotamian references to Meluhha, shelved as external evidence rather than
  Indus self-description.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed contribution is a family of urban maintenance systems: water
access, bathing and wastewater routing, drainage cleaning, reservoirs in some
regions, calibrated weights, and distributed craft standards. It is not the
myth of one perfectly gridded sanitary civilization.

## Historical relationships

Shared forms linked cities, towns, villages, workshops, farmers, pastoralists,
and coastal routes across a wide region. Contacts with Magan and Mesopotamia
connect this dossier indirectly to [Akkad](AKKAD.md). That relationship is
evidenced through texts and objects but cannot identify a single Indus state,
king, or bilateral treaty.

[`manifestation.glaushouse.water_integrity_commons`](../../ANCESTRAL_MANIFESTATIONS_REGISTRY.md#manifestation.glaushouse.water_integrity_commons).

The Water Integrity Commons is a distributed modern network of sampling,
access, drainage, settling, cleaning, repair, and public learning points. Each
district adapts to its own water and material conditions. It copies neither
Mohenjo-daro nor Dholavira and creates no clinical Clearance.

## House interpretation

Glaüshouse asks how a living system preserves or restores integrity. Mature
Indus urbanism contributes a strong answer: health-supporting infrastructure
is distributed through households, neighborhoods, crafts, materials, and
maintenance access. The [Glaüshouse Constitution](../../../../../GLAUSHOUSE_CONSTITUTION_V2.md),
Law of Integrity, consent, and existing care authority remain the only canon
constraints and permissions.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

- Published excavation reports and field records for Harappa, Mohenjo-daro,
  Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Lothal, and smaller sites, with phase and trench data.
- Inscribed objects, seals, weights, ceramics, tools, drains, wells, and
  reservoirs catalogued with exact provenience.
- [Harappa Archaeological Research Project, Mature Harappan drain
  documentation](https://www.harappa.com/blog/mohenjo-daro-street-drains),
  read alongside its cited excavation publications.
- Mesopotamian Meluhha texts, treated as external records with their own
  language, date, and political viewpoint.

### Archaeological evidence

Settlement plans, stratigraphy, hydrology, paleoenvironment, botanical and
faunal remains, human remains, craft debris, residue and compositional analysis,
transport evidence, and conservation records must remain integrated. A
spectacular city feature cannot stand for the full settlement system.

## Research notes

- Mature Harappan phase dates vary by author and site.
- The script remains undeciphered; no proposed name or doctrine is canonical.
- Social and political organization remain debated.
- Drainage does not automatically equal modern sanitation or public health.
- Lothal's “dockyard” and many inherited building names need interpretation
  labels.
- Water systems must be cited city by city; Dholavira is not Mohenjo-daro.
- Decline lies mainly beyond this Point and should not be reduced to one cause.
