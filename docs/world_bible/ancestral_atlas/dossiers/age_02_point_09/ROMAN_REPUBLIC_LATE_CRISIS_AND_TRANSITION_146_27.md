# Historical Dossier — Roman Republic, Late Crisis and Transition

Status: Working; reviewed dossier. Atlas assignment remains Working. Evidence, interpretation, and Hollow Grove adaptation remain distinct.

## Evidence boundary
Roman and later Greek narratives are elite, partisan, and often retrospective. Inscriptions, coins, law, settlements, roads, and archaeology provide independent checks. Civil-war violence, slavery, provincial extraction, and exclusion are cautions, not Gifts.

## Historical overview
After 146 BCE Roman expansion intensified while republican institutions struggled with unequal citizenship, land and labor change, provincial command, elite competition, and civil conflict. Octavian's 27 BCE settlement transformed the system without erasing every republican office or legal form.

## Chronology
146–133: provincial consolidation and reform conflict; 133–91: Gracchan reform and Italian citizenship crisis; 91–82: Social War and Sulla; 82–49: Pompey, Caesar, and faction; 49–30: civil wars and eastern intervention; 30–27: Octavian's settlement.

## Major figures
Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, Marius, Sulla, Pompey, Caesar, Cicero, Cleopatra VII, Mark Antony, and Octavian; also soldiers, provincials, enslaved people, women, allies, and displaced communities.

## Major cities
Rome, Capua, Corinth, Alexandria, Carthage, Massalia, provincial capitals, colonies, ports, and civil-war battle sites.

## Government
Magistracies, Senate, assemblies, tribunes, courts, provincial commands, tax farming, colonies, and patronage persisted while emergency powers and personal armies altered their operation.

## Religion
Mos maiorum, civic cult, triumph, household religion, philosophical schools, ruler honors, and provincial practices coexisted; public religious performance is not proof of legitimacy.

## Economy
Land concentration, slave labor, tax farming, indemnity, provincial extraction, grain supply, credit, coinage, and military contracts reshaped wealth and political conflict.

## Trade
Grain, metals, wine, oil, slaves, luxury goods, coin, credit, and information moved through Mediterranean ports and inland roads.

## Science
Surveying, law, rhetoric, medicine, geography, astronomy, and engineering circulated through Greek and Roman institutions.

## Technology
Roads, bridges, aqueducts, ports, concrete, siege works, ships, camps, archives, and surveying instruments linked civic and provincial administration.

## Architecture
Forums, temples, roads, bridges, colonies, villas, warehouses, and military camps expressed changing public and private authority.

## Military
The Jugurthine and Social Wars, Sulla's march on Rome, Mithridatic campaigns, Caesar's civil war, and Actium expose mobilization, command, atrocity, displacement, and demobilization.

## Art
Portraits, coins, triumphs, funerary monuments, temples, literature, and imported Greek forms negotiated ancestry, grief, and political legitimacy.

## Language
Latin and Greek dominated different institutions and audiences; local languages and inscriptions remained active across provinces and allied communities.

## Major texts
Cicero, Caesar, Sallust, Polybius, Livy, Plutarch, Appian, and inscriptions; dates, authorship, and narrative distance must remain visible.

## Major discoveries or innovations
No single invention defines the Point. Durable contributions include road and surveying practice, legal-administrative documentation, concrete construction, and complex logistics.

## Historical relationships
Rome engaged Greek cities, Pontus, Ptolemaic Egypt, Parthia, Judaea, Gaul, Spain, Numidia, North Africa, and Italian allies through diplomacy, patronage, annexation, and war.

- land, citizenship, and provincial obligation provenance
- civil-war command and fiscal accountability records
