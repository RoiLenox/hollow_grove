# Historical Dossier — Pontus, Mithridatic Frontier and Roman Wars

Status: Working; Atlas assignment remains Working; reviewed dossier. Frontier scale, source bias, and chronology remain under audit.

## Evidence boundary
Coins, inscriptions, city archaeology, regional material culture, and Greek and Roman narratives provide uneven evidence. Roman accounts are adversarial and later Greek accounts are selective.

## Historical overview
Pontus became a major Black Sea and Anatolian power under Mithridates VI, confronting Rome and local dynasts through alliance, naval operations, diplomacy, recruitment, and war.

## Chronology
146–120: dynastic consolidation and Black Sea expansion; 120–89: Mithridates VI and regional alliances; 89–63: three Mithridatic Wars; 63–27: Roman settlement and client-king reordering.

## Major figures
Mithridates V and VI, Nicomedes IV, Tigranes II, Roman commanders, Greek civic leaders, Black Sea communities, cavalry, naval crews, and displaced civilians.

## Major cities
Sinope, Amaseia, the Pontic coast, Crimea, Colchis, Cappadocia, Bithynia, Armenia, Greece, Asia Minor, and Black Sea ports.

## Government
Dynastic court, local cities, client rulers, military commanders, ports, tax systems, and allied communities supported uneven and contested control.

## Religion
Iranian, Anatolian, Greek, Black Sea, and royal cult traditions interacted; royal claims and Roman portrayals are political performances.

## Economy
Grain, timber, metals, horses, slaves, coinage, ports, estates, and military supply linked coast, mountains, and allied markets.

## Trade
Black Sea grain, timber, metals, wine, horses, ships, and information moved through ports and mountain routes.

## Science
Geography, multilingual diplomacy, shipbuilding, metallurgy, fortification, intelligence, and medical or toxicological lore supported frontier adaptation.

## Technology
Ports, ships, roads, fortifications, cavalry equipment, arsenals, coinage, mountain routes, and depots connected difficult terrain.

## Architecture
Harbor works, city walls, mountain strongholds, royal centers, roads, and civic buildings expressed contested regional authority.

## Military
The Mithridatic Wars, Roman campaigns, piracy, siege, massacres, displacement, and treaty settlement demonstrate capacity under severe civilian cost.

## Art
Royal coinage, portraiture, civic forms, local ritual objects, fortifications, and mixed court imagery expressed contested legitimacy.

## Language
Greek, local Anatolian and Caucasian languages, Iranian traditions, and Roman administrative language met in different institutions.

## Major texts
Appian, Plutarch, Strabo, Roman military accounts, diplomatic evidence, coins, and inscriptions; narrative distance remains explicit.

## Major discoveries or innovations
No single Pontic invention is asserted. The design inheritance is adaptive frontier logistics, naval coordination, and multilingual route intelligence.

## Historical relationships
Pontus engaged Rome, Bithynia, Armenia, Cappadocia, Greece, Colchis, Crimea, Parthia, and Black Sea communities through alliance, marriage, trade, resistance, and war.
