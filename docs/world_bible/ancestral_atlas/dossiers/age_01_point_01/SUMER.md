# Sumer — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Sumer<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point I, Stonebend<br>
Date relevance: c. 3200–2500 BCE<br>
Status: Reviewed benchmark; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** below comes from settlements, buildings, waterways,
tablets, sealings, ceramics, tools, and other excavated materials. The earliest
tablets are administrative and their language is not always securely
identifiable.

**Scholarly interpretation** supplies period names, institutional models, and
reconstructions of urban government. Those models remain contested and should
not be compressed into a single universal “temple-state.”

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. It does not claim that Sumer became Stonebend or that
Stonebend institutions descend through a literal historical chain.

## Historical overview

The Atlas range crosses the late Uruk, Jemdet Nasr, and early Early Dynastic
periods. Southern Mesopotamia contained a changing field of large cities,
smaller settlements, institutional households, cultivated land, marshes, and
river or canal routes. Uruk had already reached exceptional scale by the late
fourth millennium. During the early third millennium, several city-centered
polities became more distinct, with their own cults, rulers, institutions, and
material histories.

Administrative writing, sealing, measured allocation, storage, and organized
labor are central evidence. They document institutions; they do not give a
complete account of ordinary life or political legitimacy.

## Chronology

- **Late Uruk / Uruk IV, c. 3350–3200 BCE:** large urban center at Uruk;
  proto-cuneiform accounting, monumental precincts, seals, and extensive craft
  production.
- **Uruk III / Jemdet Nasr horizon, c. 3200–2900 BCE:** expanded administrative
  notation and regional variation amid changing settlement networks.
- **Early Dynastic I–II, c. 2900–2600 BCE:** city-centered polities and more
  localized material traditions; absolute dating remains debated.
- **Early Dynastic IIIa, beginning around c. 2600 BCE:** the end of the Point
  touches a better documented scribal and political world, but much of that
  evidence postdates 2500 BCE and must not be generalized backward.

## Major figures

No individual securely represents the entire Point. Names in later king lists
combine memory, ideology, and legend. Enmebaragesi and Mesannepada stand near
the later boundary of the range and should be used only with exact dating and
source qualification. Gilgamesh belongs first to later literary tradition, not
to a contemporary biography suitable for this dossier.

## Major cities

- **Uruk:** the strongest late-fourth-millennium evidence for urban scale,
  monumental precincts, and administrative writing.
- **Ur:** an increasingly important southern city with later third-millennium
  evidence that must be phased carefully.
- **Eridu:** a long-lived settlement and cult center within the southern
  alluvium.
- **Nippur:** a major cult and scribal center whose best-known archives often
  belong to later periods.
- **Kish:** an influential northern Babylonian city and later title of
  political prestige.

These names identify changing settlements, not a stable league or a single
state covering the full Atlas range.

## Government

Evidence points to city-level rulers, officials, large households, storage,
labor organization, and institutions associated with cult and emerging palace
authority. Terms translated as ruler or king vary by time and city. The balance
among temple, palace, assembly, kin group, and household cannot be reconstructed
uniformly from administrative tablets alone.

## Religion

Cult buildings, offerings, votives, seal imagery, and city-specific divine
associations are well attested. Later Sumerian hymns and myths preserve major
traditions but cannot be treated as verbatim evidence for every belief in
3200–2500 BCE. The dossier therefore records ritual institutions without
inventing a complete theology.

## Economy

Cereal agriculture, herding, fishing, textile production, pottery, metal and
stone work, storage, rationing, and labor allocation supported urban life.
Institutional tablets make some large organizations unusually visible while
leaving many household and informal exchanges less legible.

## Trade

Southern alluvium lacked many kinds of building timber, stone, and metal.
Waterborne and overland exchange connected cities to northern Mesopotamia,
Susiana and the Iranian plateau, the Gulf, and more distant material sources.
The route and political meaning of any individual object require separate
provenance; “trade” must not be used as a claim of direct state-to-state contact.

## Science

The strongest knowledge evidence is practical: numerical signs, commodity
categories, measures, totals, allocation, and lexical ordering. These practices
support a history of calculation and classification. They do not justify
attributing later Mesopotamian astronomy or mathematics wholesale to this
earliest Point.

## Technology

Relevant systems include clay-tablet accounting, cylinder seals, mud-brick
construction, storage, canals and field channels, pottery production, textile
work, metalworking, boats, and wheeled transport. Technologies developed
unevenly and often depended on collective maintenance rather than one moment of
invention.

## Architecture

Mud-brick monumental precincts, platforms, halls, courtyards, workshops,
storage, housing, and settlement routes are archaeologically visible. Later
formal ziggurats must not be projected backward as the standard architecture of
the entire Point.

## Military

Inter-city conflict becomes increasingly visible in Early Dynastic imagery and
later texts, but the evidence is uneven and often later than the start of this
range. Warfare is not a reviewed Stonebend Gift here.

## Art

Cylinder seals, carved vessels, modeled and carved figures, architectural
ornament, and patterned ceramics linked image, ritual, labor, office, and
exchange. Their motifs need contextual reading; they are not a generic visual
asset library for Hollow Grove.

## Language

Sumerian is a language isolate documented in cuneiform. The earliest
proto-cuneiform system was used primarily for administration, and assigning a
spoken language to every archaic sign sequence remains difficult. Later
cuneiform and Sumerian literature cannot erase that uncertainty.

## Major texts

- Proto-cuneiform accounts from Uruk and related sites.
- Receipts, allocations, ration and labor records, commodity accounts, and
  lexical lists.
- Early Dynastic administrative and school texts near the later end of the
  range.

Later epics, royal hymns, law collections, and laments belong in later Point
collections unless a dossier explicitly establishes their date relevance.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed formulation is deliberately plural: city-scale organization,
administrative writing, sealing, measured allocation, and managed waterscapes
developed together. The dossier does not assign a simplistic “first ever” claim
where chronology or independent development is debated.

## Historical relationships

Material and administrative connections linked southern Mesopotamia with
Susiana, the Iranian plateau, northern Mesopotamia, and Gulf exchange. Sumer and
the Proto-Elamite horizon were adjacent participants in changing networks, not
two modern nation-states with a single documented bilateral relationship.

## House interpretation

Stonebend asks what gives a civilization durable form. This dossier answers
with a bounded triad: a work must be recorded, connected to maintained civic
dependencies, and placed inside legible institutions. Sumer does not authorize
Stonebend law; the [Stonebend Constitution](../../../../../STONEBEND_CONSTITUTION_V2.md)
and established offices remain the authority.

### Primary historical records

- [CDLI Uruk IV administrative tablet P003801](https://cdli.earth/P003801),
  c. 3350–3200 BCE.
- [CDLI proto-cuneiform texts from diverse collections](https://cdli.earth/publications/3865),
  including receipts and grain accounts.

  ORACC, used with provenance and date metadata.

### Archaeological evidence

Settlement plans, monumental precincts, canal and landscape research,
buildings, storage, workshops, ceramics, seals, and environmental reconstruction
belong together; text-only history would overrepresent institutions that wrote.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*The Republic*, *Metaphysics*, *Meditations*, *The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions*, and *Process and Reality* remain unchanged. They give Stonebend
later philosophical languages for order, causation, duty, knowledge change,
and persistence. They do not explain what Sumerians “really meant” and must not
replace the archaeological record.

## Research notes

- Absolute dates and the ED I/II division vary across scholarly chronologies.
- The earliest writing should not automatically be called fully Sumerian.
- “Temple economy” and “priest-king” are useful historiographic terms only when
  their evidence and limits are stated.
- Canal governance is well grounded as a system need; the exact office and
  legal organization of every channel are not known.
- Named figures and later literary works remain outside the benchmark Gifts.
