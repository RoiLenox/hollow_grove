# Early Indus Civilization — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Early Indus Civilization / Early Harappan Regionalization Era<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point I, Glaüshouse<br>
Date relevance: c. 3300–2600 BCE, with the Point continuing to 2500 BCE<br>
Status: Reviewed benchmark; Atlas assignment remains Working and Needs date refinement at the c. 2600 BCE Mature Harappan boundary

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** is archaeological: settlement layers, buildings,
streets, wells and drains, ceramics, plant and animal remains, craft debris,
weights, seals, sealings, signs, tools, ornaments, and traded materials. The
Indus script remains undeciphered, so there is no translated political or
religious narrative.

**Scholarly interpretation** identifies a Regionalization Era and describes
Ravi/Hakra, Kot Diji, and other regional phases. The names organize related but
diverse communities; they do not imply one uniform early state.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** uses settlement integrity, water stewardship, and
emerging standards. It must keep early evidence separate from the more
spectacular Mature Harappan systems after c. 2600 BCE.

## Historical overview

During the later fourth and early third millennia BCE, communities across the
Greater Indus region expanded settlements, intensified crafts and exchange,
developed local and interregional material styles, and adopted signs, seals,
weights, and forms of spatial coordination that preceded Mature Harappan
urbanism. Harappa’s Ravi and Kot Diji levels provide a particularly well-phased
sequence. Dholavira preserves a long occupation beginning around 3000 BCE and
is important for studying continuity and later elaboration in a water-scarce
environment.

The Atlas Point c. 3200–2500 BCE crosses the conventional transition into the
Mature Harappan or Integration Era around c. 2600 BCE. This dossier does not
rewrite that supplied range; it records the phase boundary and prevents later
urban achievements from being silently assigned to the early phase.

## Chronology

- **Regionalization Era / Ravi-Hakra phase at Harappa, c. 3300–2800 BCE:** early
  settlement, household production, craft activity, local signs, and growing
  exchange.
- **Kot Diji / Early Harappan phase at Harappa, c. 2800–2600 BCE:** expanded
  settlement, more complex craft, seal use, early script development, and
  emerging weight standards.
- **Mature Harappan / Integration Era, c. 2600–1900 BCE:** large urban centers,
  broad standardization, extensive script and seal use, and major water and
  drainage systems; only c. 2600–2500 overlaps Point I.

Regional phase dates vary by site. A label from Harappa should not be forced
onto every settlement without local stratigraphy.

## Major figures

No named ruler, official, author, engineer, or physician can be securely
recovered. The undeciphered script and lack of later king lists make invented
biography especially inappropriate. Museum or educational content should
foreground communities, craftspeople, builders, farmers, traders, and
maintainers without assigning unsupported personal names.

## Major cities

For the early phase, “major settlements and sites” is safer than assuming every
location was already a Mature Harappan city.

- **Harappa:** Ravi and Kot Diji layers document settlement growth, craft,
  writing-related signs, seals, and early weights.
- **Kot Diji:** fortified early settlement and the type-site for a major Early
  Harappan material phase.
- **Kalibangan:** early settlement and regional evidence in Rajasthan.
- **Rehman Dheri:** planned and fortified settlement important to studies of
  early urbanization.
- **Dholavira:** occupation from around 3000 BCE with a long sequence later
  renowned for urban and water systems.

Other sites must be added with precise phase and excavation references.

## Government

Settlement hierarchy, building programs, craft organization, exchange, seals,
and emerging standards imply coordination. They do not disclose the name or
form of government. Models involving chiefs, councils, merchants, ritual
leaders, corporate groups, or states remain interpretations rather than
translated constitutional records.

## Religion

Burials, figurines, animal and geometric motifs, special objects, and spatial
practices provide evidence of meaning and ritual. Named gods, “priest-kings,”
yogic interpretations, and direct continuities with later South Asian religions
are not securely established for the early phase and are excluded from Gifts.

## Economy

Mixed farming and herding supported settlements. Pottery, bead, shell, stone,
metal, textile-related, and other craft activities were organized at household
and specialist scales. Storage, weights, seals, and raw-material movement show
increasing coordination while leaving many exchange mechanisms uncertain.

## Trade

Marine shell, stones, metals, and other materials moved across river plains,
coasts, and highland routes. Interregional links with Baluchistan, Central Asia,
the Iranian plateau, Oman, and Mesopotamia developed over time. Stronger
evidence for named Mesopotamian “Meluhha” belongs mainly to the Mature period
and should not be assigned wholesale to the early centuries.

## Science

Practical knowledge appears through measures, craft sequences, pyrotechnology,
spatial orientation, material sourcing, agriculture, and water use. An early
cubical limestone weight at Harappa falls into a later Harappan weight category,
but wider standardization is a developing process, not proof of one early
central bureau.

## Technology

Mud-brick and some stone construction, pottery, hearths and kilns, copper use,
stone blades, bead making, shell working, seals, sealings, signs, weights,
storage, wells, drains, and water capture occur with regional and chronological
variation. Sophisticated integrated drainage and reservoir systems are most
securely documented in Mature phases or long-lived sites and are not renamed
“early public sanitation” without phase evidence.

## Architecture

Early settlement organization includes oriented buildings, streets or paths,
walls, storage, production areas, and fortifications at some sites. Dholavira’s
later reservoirs, Harappa’s Mature urban form, Mohenjo-daro’s Great Bath, and
Mature standardized brick systems are comparative later evidence, not templates
for this dossier.

## Military

Walls and fortified sectors indicate protection, boundary, or status but do not
reveal a detailed military institution. Weapons and conflict evidence are
insufficient to make warfare a Point I Glaüshouse inheritance.

## Art

Painted ceramics, beads, ornaments, figurines, seals, incised signs, and crafted
stone, shell, faience, and metal expressed regional identity, status, exchange,
and skill. Motifs remain culturally specific and should not be copied as a
generic “Indus” pattern library.

## Language

Spoken languages are unknown. Signs occur from early phases and develop toward
the Indus script, but the script remains undeciphered and its linguistic basis
is unresolved. Hollow Grove must never fabricate translations or doctrinal
texts.

## Major texts

There are no readable historical texts. The primary record class includes:

- early signs on pottery and other objects;
- seals and seal impressions;
- later short Indus inscriptions studied only with phase context;
- archaeological field records and object catalogues.

The absence of translated texts is positive metadata, not a placeholder to fill
with speculation.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed contribution is a long developmental process: organized
settlements, increasingly complex craft production, exchange networks, signs,
seals, and emerging measures laid foundations for later urban integration.
Water adaptation varied by landscape and became especially important at
long-lived arid sites.

## Historical relationships

Regional traditions increasingly interacted and converged before c. 2600 BCE.
The result was not cultural uniformity; mature shared practices overlaid local
histories. Contacts with western regions should be described through specific
materials and contexts rather than a single civilizational pipeline.

Stratified architecture, streets, walls, water features, ceramics, botanical
and animal remains, craft debris, raw-material sourcing, weights, seals,
sealings, signs, and burials. Site phase and context must accompany every image
or design claim.

## Research notes

- The supplied Point crosses the c. 2600 BCE Early/Mature boundary and needs
  date refinement without rewriting its current assignment.
- Dholavira preserves a long sequence; its most sophisticated waterworks should
  not be assigned automatically to its earliest occupation.
- The script is undeciphered. All translations or named offices would be
  unsupported.
- “Public sanitation” is retained for Mature Indus research, not this early
  benchmark Gift set.
- Regional diversity should remain visible beneath later integration.
