# Minoan Crete — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Late Prepalatial Crete, conventionally called Minoan<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point III, Glaüshouse<br>
Date relevance: c. 2100–1900 BCE, principally EM III–MM IA<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs date and terminology refinement; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** is archaeological: settlement phases, houses, storage,
burials, pottery, seals and sealings, stone vessels, metalwork, tools, plant and
animal remains, and imported materials. The relevant slice has no readable
narrative or administrative corpus.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs social differentiation, craft networks,
exchange, ritual, and the formation of court-centered complexes. The terms
`Minoan`, `Prepalatial`, and `palace` are modern analytical labels. Their apparent
clarity must not conceal regional variation or debate.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Glaüshouse receives preservation of craft knowledge,
material custody, and settlement memory—not a palace economy, imagined rulers,
labyrinth mythology, reconstructed cult, or replica Knossos.

## Historical overview

The supplied c. 2100–1900 BCE coordinate falls largely within late Prepalatial
Early Minoan III–Middle Minoan IA. Communities across Crete occupied different
landscapes and maintained regionally varied traditions in pottery, stonework,
metal, seals, burial, storage, and settlement organization while exchanging
materials and forms within Crete and beyond it.

At Malia, the École française d'Athènes identifies c. 2200–1900 BCE as a poorly
known final Prepalatial phase. Earlier structures and occupation lay beneath or
were incorporated into later construction. The first-palace or Protopalatial
period begins around 1900 BCE. Comparable dating varies by site and ceramic
sequence, but the principle is firm: the mature palace networks usually invoked
by “Minoan Crete” are a boundary or Point IV subject, not a full description of
Point III.

The dossier therefore centers continuity through institutional transformation.
Craft practice, custody, material movement, and inhabited places did not appear
from nothing when court-centered complexes emerged. Nor can archaeology show
that one palace, ruler, religion, or political system already governed all Crete.

## Chronology

- **Early Minoan II, before c. 2200 BCE:** important background in settlement,
  exchange, craft, and burial traditions; mostly outside the Point.
- **Early Minoan III, approximately c. 2200–2050/2000 BCE:** regionally uneven
  settlement and material change after earlier widespread exchange.
- **Middle Minoan IA, approximately c. 2050/2000–1925/1900 BCE:** late
  Prepalatial development, growing centers, craft and storage change.
- **Middle Minoan IB and the first-palace threshold, around c. 1900 BCE:**
  Protopalatial court-centered complexes emerge at sites including Knossos,
  Phaistos, and Malia; this belongs at the Point boundary and continues in Point IV.

Ceramic phases are relative chronologies aligned imperfectly with absolute dates.
The Atlas marks the assignment Boundary / Transforming.

## Major figures

No named ruler, official, craft worker, author, physician, or religious figure
is securely recoverable for this slice. King Minos is legendary, and Sir Arthur
Evans used that name in the modern construction of “Minoan” chronology. Minos
is not inserted into the historical dossier as a ruler.

## Major cities

- **Knossos:** long-lived settlement and later major court-centered complex;
  early levels are difficult and later rebuilding dominates visibility.
- **Phaistos:** southern center with prepalatial contexts and a later first palace.
- **Malia:** especially useful for the c. 2200–1900 / c. 1900 boundary.
- **Mochlos:** coastal settlement, craft, burial, and exchange evidence.
- **Vasiliki and Myrtos:** important earlier settlement contexts whose occupation
  histories provide regional comparison.
- **Archanes, Petras, and other centers:** relevant to regional networks and
  institutional formation, with exact phases reviewed site by site.

## Government

Households, growing settlements, storage, seals, craft specialists, burial
communities, and differentiated buildings imply coordination and inequality.
They do not reveal a constitution, named office, territorial border, single
island ruler, or uniform redistributive system. First-palace administration is
not backdated from later Cretan Hieroglyphic or Linear A records.

## Religion

Burials, figurines, special deposits, seals, caves, peaks, and later ritual
continuities inform scholarship. Named gods, priestly offices, doctrine, and
the meaning of particular symbols remain unavailable. Later bull, goddess,
labyrinth, and fresco traditions cannot serve as a key to every prepalatial
object.

## Economy

Agriculture, herding, fishing, storage, household production, pottery,
stoneworking, metalwork, textiles, sealing, and exchange sustained settlements.
The scale and institutional ownership of surplus vary by site. “Redistribution”
is a hypothesis to test against context, especially before the first palaces.

## Trade

Communities exchanged obsidian, metals, stone, ceramics, craft forms, and other
materials within Crete and across the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean. Contact
with Egypt and the Near East is phase- and object-specific. A foreign parallel
or imported object does not prove direct diplomatic relations between named
courts.

## Science

No scientific treatise survives. Practical knowledge is visible in material
selection, firing, stoneworking, alloying, construction, storage, agriculture,
navigation, and repeated craft sequences. These are recoverable as situated
knowledge rather than named theories.

## Technology

Pottery production, stone vessels, copper-alloy work, seal engraving, textile
tools, boats, storage containers, masonry, plaster, and food-processing equipment
supported communities. Wheel-made pottery and formal palace archives are mainly
later boundary developments and must be tagged by phase.

## Architecture

Houses, courts, storage, workshops, streets, burial structures, and larger
buildings differ across sites. At Malia, late Prepalatial walls were incorporated,
leveled, or built over as the first palace formed. The reviewed design principle
is preservation of building phases and displaced functions—not copying a central
court or Evans's reconstructed Knossos.

## Military

Weapons, defensible locations, and conflict may be present, but the evidence does
not recover named armies, campaigns, commanders, or an island-wide military
system. Claims that palace sites lacked defenses or were uniformly peaceful rely
mostly on later periods and contested interpretation.

## Art

Pottery, stone vessels, seals, jewelry, metalwork, figurines, and burial goods
show strong material knowledge and regional style. Famous later frescoes, marine
style ceramics, bull-leaping images, and palace sculpture are not Point III
assets.

## Language

The spoken languages of the communities are not recoverable. Cretan Hieroglyphic
and Linear A appear in later administrative development and remain undeciphered
as languages. They are not evidence for a named Point III people or readable
doctrine.

## Major texts

No readable Point III text corpus is available. Relevant records are material:

- seals, sealings, and their contexts;
- ceramic sequences and workshop evidence;
- settlement and building phases;
- burial assemblages;
- storage containers and residues;
- imported and provenienced materials.

Later Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A records belong in a separate Point IV
collection with script status and date attached.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed contribution is a long, regional process in which craft knowledge,
custody, exchange, and inhabited settlement survived and shaped the emergence of
new institutional centers. The Atlas does not claim one invention, sudden palace
revolution, or uniform “Minoan genius.”

## Historical relationships

Late Prepalatial Crete participated in Aegean and wider eastern Mediterranean
exchange. The dossier's most important internal relationship is temporal: it
connects diverse prepalatial communities to the court-centered complexes that
begin around the endpoint and continue under the supplied Minoan Crete label in
Point IV. That later dossier must not simply copy this one.

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

There are no readable primary texts for the reviewed slice. Catalogue “records”
are excavation contexts, stratigraphic descriptions, sealings, pottery groups,
building phases, burial assemblages, and provenienced objects. The [British
Museum Early Minoan III goblet](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1906-1112-69)
is one collection object, not a representative civilization in itself.

### Archaeological evidence

The collection prioritizes late Prepalatial layers at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia,
Mochlos, Vasiliki, Myrtos, Archanes, Petras, and regional surveys. The [Malia
historical sequence](https://www.efa.gr/presentation-historique-3/) provides a
clear published boundary between c. 2200–1900 Prepalatial evidence and the first
palace after c. 1900.

### Major modern scholarship

- John C. McEnroe, [*Architecture of Minoan Crete*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7560/721937),
  especially the under-studied EM III–MM IA transition.
- Maria Relaki, [“Rethinking administration and seal use in third millennium
  Crete”](https://www.aegeussociety.org/en/new_article/rethinking-administration-and-seal-use-in-third-millennium-crete/),
  for the warning against reading later palace administration back into early sealings.
- Ilse Schoep, Peter Tomkins, and Jan Driessen, eds., work on social and
  institutional change before and during palace formation.
- Donald Preziosi and Louise Hitchcock, and the [Cambridge Companion chapter on
  palace formation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-the-aegean-bronze-age/formation-of-the-palaces/31EDAE121C08653BFF9CA45FC1AB14A0),
  used as interpretive frameworks rather than settled constitutions.
- [Metropolitan Museum, “Minoan Crete”](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/minoan-crete),
  used cautiously because its palace synthesis centers the c. 1900 boundary and later periods.

### Recommended House library placement

Place in the Glaüshouse Age I historical wing under conservation archaeology,
craft knowledge, materials, custody, settlement maintenance, and institutional
change. Later scripts, palace archives, frescoes, and water features receive a
separate Point IV shelf.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*On the Origin of Species*, *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, *The Tree of
Knowledge*, and the living-systems collection remain unchanged. They can support
comparison about continuity and organization, but they do not establish an
ancient ecology theory, decode symbols, or prove autopoietic palace systems.

## Research notes

- Catalogue every design source by site, context, and ceramic phase.
- Test `seal-mediated custody` against secure EM III–MM IA contexts.
- Keep first-palace and later Neopalatial architecture outside this Point unless
  explicitly labeled boundary context.
- Avoid `Minoan` as a self-name and Minos as a historical ruler.
- Expand subsistence, household, and rural evidence so court formation does not
  dominate the entire memory of Crete.
