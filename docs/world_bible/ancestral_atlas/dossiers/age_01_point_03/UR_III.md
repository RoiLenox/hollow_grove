# Ur III — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Third Dynasty of Ur / Ur III<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point III, Stonebend<br>
Date relevance: c. 2112–2004 BCE within the supplied c. 2100–1900 BCE Point<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs date refinement; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes tens of thousands of administrative and legal
tablets, year names, seals, building deposits, excavated structures, and royal
inscriptions. Archive, provenance, document genre, and date formula matter:
records from one office cannot automatically describe the whole state.

**Scholarly interpretation** reconstructs provinces, taxation, redistribution,
labor, royal ideology, and collapse from an unusually abundant but institutionally
selective record. The scope of the `bala` system, the attribution of the Laws of
Ur-Nammu, and the causes of collapse remain debated.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Stonebend receives accountable measures, dated obligations,
and traceable custody—not kingship, taxation, coerced labor, divine status, or a
second source of Claim, Title, or Yield.

## Historical overview

Ur-Nammu founded the dynasty after the post-Akkadian struggles involving
Utu-hegal and the rulers of Lagash. He and his successors ruled from Ur while
working through older cities, provinces, temple and palace households, governors,
scribes, messengers, military officials, and dependent rulers. Shulgi's long
reign is associated with major administrative development, royal travel and
communication, building, warfare, and an expanded royal ideology.

The dynasty's archives make classification and coordination exceptionally
visible. They also record extraction, dependent labor, captives, livestock,
work quotas, deliveries, and institutional inequality. Administrative detail is
therefore both a design inheritance and a warning: legibility can support
maintenance or coercion depending on the authority and obligations around it.

Under Ibbi-Sin, provinces and supply relationships fractured. Ishbi-Erra
established power at Isin, food shortage appears in the royal correspondence,
and a coalition associated with Elam, Susiana, and Kindattu took Ur around 2004
BCE. Environmental stress, political secession, conflict, and supply failure
must remain a multi-causal field rather than a single collapse story.

## Chronology

- **Ur-Nammu, conventional Middle Chronology c. 2112–2095 BCE:** dynastic
  foundation, construction at Ur and other cities, and early law collection.
- **Shulgi, c. 2094–2047 BCE:** long reign, administrative intensification,
  campaigns, building, royal hymns, and eventual royal divinization.
- **Amar-Suen, c. 2046–2038 BCE:** continuation of the dynastic system and
  eastern campaigns.
- **Shu-Suen, c. 2037–2029 BCE:** frontier conflict, defensive construction,
  and continuing archives.
- **Ibbi-Sin, c. 2028–2004 BCE:** provincial loss, supply crisis, Ishbi-Erra's
  breakaway, and the fall of Ur.
- **After c. 2004 BCE:** Isin and other successor states continue many scribal
  and institutional practices, but they are not Ur III and are not silently
  folded into the remaining century of Point III.

Absolute dates vary with Mesopotamian chronological systems. The Atlas uses the
Middle Chronology convention visible in Douglas Frayne's RIME volume and marks
the assignment Partial / Terminal.

## Major figures

- **Ur-Nammu:** dynasty founder and monumental builder. The law collection
  bearing his name may have been compiled or promulgated under him or Shulgi;
  attribution is not treated as settled.
- **Shulgi:** ruler of the dynasty's longest reign, associated with administrative
  expansion, roads and messengers, hymns, building, and divinized kingship.
- **Amar-Suen and Shu-Suen:** successors documented through year names,
  inscriptions, seals, and administrative archives.
- **Ibbi-Sin:** last king of Ur III, known through contemporary records and later
  literary memory of Ur's fall.
- **Ishbi-Erra:** formerly connected to Ibbi-Sin and founder of an independent
  dynasty at Isin; he belongs to the terminal transition, not the Ur III royal line.
- **Kindattu:** Šimaškian ruler associated with the coalition that captured Ur;
  see the parallel [Šimaški dossier](SIMASKI.md).

## Major cities

- **Ur:** royal capital and major cult and building center.
- **Uruk:** older urban and cult center connected to the dynasty's formation.
- **Nippur:** center of Enlil's cult and a major source of legitimacy and texts.
- **Girsu/Lagash and Umma:** provincial centers with large administrative archives.
- **Puzrish-Dagan/Drehem:** collection and distribution center best known through
  livestock records; it must not stand for every Ur III institution.
- **Susa:** eastern center under changing Ur III and local control.
- **Eshnunna and Isin:** important to the late fragmentation and successor order.

## Government

The king and royal family stood above a layered system of provinces, governors,
local officials, temple and palace households, scribes, messengers, military
personnel, estates, and labor groups. Surviving archives show recurring measures,
accounts, seals, dates, and reporting paths, but not a single procedural manual
followed identically everywhere.

Provincial obligations and redistribution are often discussed through the
`bala` system. Scholarship disputes its exact scope and whether one model can
cover all taxes, deliveries, and offices. The Atlas therefore uses the concrete
Gift “provincial obligation and redistribution records,” not “perfect centralized
bureaucracy.”

## Religion

City cults and temple institutions remained central. Ur-Nammu built for Nanna,
Inanna, and other deities; royal inscriptions name divine patrons; foundation
deposits joined building to ritual. Shulgi and later kings used divine
determinatives, an exceptional royal ideology that should not be projected
backward to all Mesopotamian rulers or outward to all residents' belief.

## Economy

Institutional agriculture, herding, textiles, craft production, landholding,
labor, rations, stores, taxation, deliveries, and military provisioning dominate
the archives. Tablets permit precise study of particular people and offices, but
their institutional viewpoint underrepresents informal, household, and
unrecorded activity. Rations are not assumed to be wages in every context.

## Trade

River, Gulf, and overland systems moved grain, wool, textiles, livestock, wood,
metals, stone, and luxury materials. Records connect the state to Dilmun, Magan,
Syria, northern Mesopotamia, Susiana, and the Iranian plateau. Trade, tax,
tribute, diplomatic gift, and coerced transfer are separate categories even when
the same material appears in each.

## Science

Scribal work included metrology, numerical notation, accounting, surveying,
legal formulae, lexical training, calendars, and year-name chronology. These are
practical knowledge systems. Later Old Babylonian mathematical and astronomical
corpora are not assigned wholesale to Ur III.

## Technology

Clay tablets, cylinder seals, envelopes, mud brick, baked brick in selected
uses, canals, riverboats, road and messenger networks, storage, textile
production, metalwork, stonework, plows, and managed herds supported institutions.
The relevant inheritance is the joined system of tool, record, route, store,
maintenance, and accountable handoff.

## Architecture

Ziggurats, temples, courtyards, houses, walls, gates, canals, storehouses, and
administrative buildings were constructed or restored across several cities.
Foundation figures and inscribed bricks make building sponsorship visible. Hollow
Grove does not reproduce a ziggurat district; it translates foundation record,
service dependency, measured work, and repair layer into modern Stonebend form.

## Military

Royal year names and inscriptions record eastern and northern campaigns,
defeated cities, captives, frontier walls, garrisons, and rebellion. These are
often victory claims or administrative records of coerced people, not neutral
campaign histories. Warfare, captive labor, and imperial extraction are not
Stonebend Gifts.

## Art

Foundation figures, royal statuary, cylinder seals, inscribed bricks, relief,
and hymnic images joined ruler, office, construction, cult, and memory. The Met's
Ur-Nammu foundation figure is useful evidence for a building deposit and its
inscription, not a costume or statue template for Stonebend.

## Language

Sumerian dominates much administration and royal representation, while Akkadian
was also used. Language, genre, place, and archive varied. Later Old Babylonian
copies preserve royal hymns, correspondence, and literary works, so the date of
the proposed composition and the date of the surviving witness must both be
recorded.

## Major texts

- Contemporary administrative and legal tablets from Umma, Girsu, Puzrish-Dagan,
  Ur, Nippur, and other archives.
- Royal inscriptions of Ur-Nammu through Ibbi-Sin, edited in the Royal
  Inscriptions of Mesopotamia and ORACC corpora.
- Year-name lists and dated documents.
- The Laws of Ur-Nammu, with attribution and manuscript history flagged.
- Royal hymns and praise poetry, mostly preserved in later copies.
- The literary correspondence of Ibbi-Sin and Ishbi-Erra, preserved in later
  educational manuscripts and shelved apart from contemporary letters.
- City laments about Ur and Sumer, treated as later literary memory rather than
  eyewitness transcripts.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The strongest reviewed contribution is the density and comparability of dated
institutional records across several archives. That record permits analysis of
obligations, deliveries, labor, custody, and failure at unusual resolution. The
Atlas does not claim that Ur III invented bureaucracy, law, taxation, roads, or
accounting.

## Historical relationships

Ur III inherited cities and scribal practices from the post-Akkadian order,
including the [Akkad dossier](../age_01_point_02/AKKAD.md). It campaigned,
negotiated, married, and traded across northern and eastern frontiers. Its final
crisis connects directly to [Šimaški](SIMASKI.md), but the fall of Ur cannot be
explained by one enemy or one event.

## Modern Hollow Grove manifestation

Reviewed flagship:
[`manifestation.stonebend.civic_obligation_ledger`](../../ANCESTRAL_MANIFESTATIONS_REGISTRY.md#manifestation.stonebend.civic_obligation_ledger).

The Civic Obligation Ledger records measured maintenance duties, delivery and
inspection dates, responsible offices, exceptions, and completion evidence for
public works. It cannot tax, command labor, or create Claim, Title, or Yield.

## House interpretation

Stonebend asks what gives a civilization durable form. Ur III shows both the
capacity and danger of administrative legibility. Durable civic form requires
measures, dates, custody, maintenance, and bounded responsibility; it also
requires constitutional limits so a complete record does not become a complete
claim over persons. Authority remains in the [Stonebend Constitution](../../../../../STONEBEND_CONSTITUTION_V2.md).

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

- [Douglas Frayne, *Ur III Period (2112–2004 BC)*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.3138/j.ctt13x1qkz),
  a modern edition of royal inscriptions with witness and chronology apparatus.
- [ORACC ETCSRI, Ibbi-Sin royal inscription Q004992](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/etcsri/Q004992),
  shelved with edition metadata rather than as a transparent political report.
- Administrative and legal tablets through CDLI and published Ur III archive
  corpora, always with provenience and genre attached.
- [ETCSL royal correspondence catalogue](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/catalogue/catalogue3.htm),
  shelved as later manuscript transmission and literary memory.

### Archaeological evidence

The collection includes excavated buildings, canals, foundation deposits,
inscribed bricks, seals, tablets, workshops, burials, botanical and animal
remains, and settlement change. The [Met Ur-Nammu foundation figure](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/329067)
anchors a documented object case in building ritual and inscription.

### Major modern scholarship

- Douglas Frayne, *Ur III Period (2112–2004 BC)*, RIME 3/2 (1997).
- Piotr Steinkeller and Michael Hudson, eds., *Labor in the Ancient World*,
  including source-critical Ur III labor studies.
- Tonia Sharlach, *Provincial Taxation and the Ur III State* (Brill, 2004),
  with the limits and debate around provincial systems retained.
- McGuire Gibson and Robert Biggs, eds., *The Organization of Power: Aspects of
  Bureaucracy in the Ancient Near East*, including [Ur III administration](https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc46.pdf).

### Recommended House library placement

Place in the Stonebend Age I historical wing under administrative records,
metrology, public works, legal documents, labor history, and institutional
failure. Royal hymns and later laments receive separate genre and witness labels.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*The Republic*, *Metaphysics*, *Meditations*, *The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions*, and *Process and Reality* remain the permanent foundational shelf.
Ur III evidence gives them an earlier comparative archive; those books do not
validate kingship, decode Sumerian institutions, or turn ancient administration
into Hollow Grove law.

## Research notes

- Keep Middle, High, and Low Chronology distinctions visible when exact dates matter.
- Audit each claimed `bala` example by archive rather than using one state-wide diagram.
- Separate contemporary documents, later copies, literary letters, and laments.
- Preserve the coercive and captive-labor context of relevant records.
- Review the Laws of Ur-Nammu attribution before naming a figure as lawgiver.
