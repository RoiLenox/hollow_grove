# Šimaški — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Šimaški / Shimashki political and dynastic horizon<br>
Atlas assignment: Age I, Point III, Flynt<br>
Date relevance: especially c. 2004–1900 BCE, with earlier Ur III relations<br>
Status: Reviewed dossier; Needs historical, location, and chronology audit; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes Ur III administrative records, year names and
royal inscriptions; later Susian building inscriptions; a retrospective Old
Babylonian tablet listing twelve Awan and twelve Šimaški kings; later literary
copies about Ur's fall; and difficult archaeological sequences at Susa and on
the Iranian plateau.

**Scholarly interpretation** tries to distinguish Šimaški as a place, a set of
highland polities, a dynasty, an alliance, and a later ruling tradition at Susa.
Its location, borders, language, material culture, succession, and relation to
Anshan and the early Sukkalmah/Epartid order remain disputed.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Flynt receives a source-critical continuity problem about
coalition command and disputed succession—not conquest, trophy culture, invented
frontier doctrine, pseudo-Elamite writing, or new command authority.

## Historical overview

Šimaški appears in Mesopotamian sources during the Third Dynasty of Ur as an
eastern place and political formation with several named rulers. Ur III kings
alternated war, diplomacy, marriage relations, and claims of control involving
Šimaški and other Iranian plateau polities. The evidence does not establish one
stable territorial kingdom across the whole c. 2100–1900 BCE Point.

Kindattu, named as the sixth Šimaškian king in the later Susa list, is associated
with Elamite and Susian forces that captured Ur around 2004 BCE and carried
Ibbi-Sin away. The fall belonged to a wider Ur III crisis involving provincial
secession, supply failure, conflict, and changing regional power. It is not
treated as the achievement of one opponent or a positive Gift.

After Ur's fall, Šimaškian rulers are associated with Susa and with construction
or restoration for Inshushinak. The later king list preserves a dynastic memory
but gives no regnal years or parentage and was written centuries after several
listed rulers. Late Šimaškian names overlap early rulers classed by modern
scholarship as Sukkalmahs or Epartids, so a clean single-date handoff is not
supported.

## Chronology

- **Late twenty-second / early twenty-first century BCE:** rulers including
  Girnamme, Tazitta, and Yabrat appear in uneven relations with Ur III; sequence
  and identifications require specialist review.
- **Shulgi through Shu-Suen:** repeated campaigns, diplomacy, marriages, and
  administrative references connect Ur with eastern polities, including Šimaški.
- **c. 2004 BCE:** Kindattu is associated with the coalition that captured Ur.
- **Early second millennium BCE:** Idaddu I and other Šimaškian rulers are linked
  to Susa, building, and relations with Mesopotamian successor states.
- **Toward c. 1900 BCE:** late Šimaškian and early Sukkalmah/Epartid rulership
  overlaps in the sources; the transition is not a simple replacement event.
- **c. 1800–1600 BCE:** the surviving Louvre king-list tablet was written at
  Susa, long after the early rulers it orders.

The Atlas marks the assignment Partial / Transition and strongest after c. 2004.
Some publications use alternate absolute chronologies; dates must not be mixed
without labeling the system used.

## Major figures

- **Girnamme and Tazitta:** early listed or attested rulers associated with the
  Ur III–Šimaški relationship; their exact position remains debated.
- **Yabrat:** known through records concerning relations with the Ur III state;
  modern scholarship has revised his place in Šimaškian history.
- **Kindattu:** ruler associated with the capture of Ur around 2004 BCE and later
  remembered in Susa's dynastic and temple traditions.
- **Idaddu I:** Šimaškian ruler associated with Susa and restoration or building;
  exact genealogical reconstruction requires caution.
- **Tan-Ruhurater and later listed rulers:** evidence for dynastic and diplomatic
  continuity, including overlap with the emerging successor order.
- **Ibbi-Sin and Ishbi-Erra:** Ur III and Isin figures essential to the terminal
  context but not Šimaškian rulers.

## Major cities

- **Šimaški:** name of the relevant country or center, not securely located.
- **Susa:** principal source of the king list and later inscriptions; political
  importance does not prove that Susa was identical with Šimaški.
- **Anshan:** plateau center important to Elamite history and possible regional
  relationships; it is not used as an automatic synonym for Šimaški.
- **Ur:** site of the c. 2004 rupture described in Mesopotamian and later memory.
- **Eshnunna and Isin:** successor-state contexts for diplomacy and chronology.

## Government

The sources attest rulers, dynastic ordering, coalitions, marriage relations,
diplomacy, campaigns, and later construction at Susa. They do not recover a
constitution, stable capital, provincial map, uniform bureaucracy, or certain
rules of succession. `Dynasty` describes the later list and scholarly grouping;
it does not prove direct parent-to-child succession among all twelve names.

## Religion

Later evidence connects Kindattu and Idaddu I to work for Inshushinak at Susa.
Deities and cults of Susa, Anshan, and wider Elamite traditions provide context,
but a comprehensive distinct Šimaškian theology is not reconstructable. Temple
restoration claims are later royal evidence, not eyewitness reports of every act.

## Economy

Direct Šimaškian economic archives are scarce or hard to isolate. Highland and
lowland agriculture, herding, metals, stone, timber, craft, tribute, diplomatic
movement, and exchange form the regional context. Mesopotamian demands for
eastern materials do not identify who controlled every mine, route, or transfer.

## Trade

Susiana, the Zagros, plateau routes, Mesopotamia, and the Gulf formed overlapping
movement systems. Šimaškian participation is likely and sometimes textually
visible, but exact route control and commercial institutions remain Working.
The reviewed Gift is about political and custody handoffs, not an invented trade
network map.

## Science

No Šimaškian scientific corpus is recoverable. Cuneiform records show practices
of naming, dynastic ordering, diplomacy, building inscription, and administration
in Mesopotamian and Susian contexts. They do not justify assigning a general
school, mathematics, astronomy, or technical doctrine.

## Technology

Regional evidence includes transport, sealing, mud-brick and stone construction,
metalwork, weaponry, fortification, and management of stores. Because a bounded
Šimaškian archaeological assemblage is difficult to identify, every technical
claim must retain site and context rather than becoming a culture-wide Gift.

## Architecture

Later inscriptions record construction or restoration at Susa. Excavation there
is fundamental but older recording and disturbed contexts complicate historical
reconstruction. No known “Šimaškian architecture” is authorized as a Flynt
style. The modern station uses legible routes, stores, handoffs, and unknown
zones, not a reconstructed fortress or temple.

## Military

Ur III year names and later traditions document campaigns involving Šimaški and
associate Kindattu with the fall of Ur. These sources are royal, adversarial, or
retrospective. They do not reveal neutral order of battle, stable ranks, a
distinct doctrine, or civilian experience. Conquest and captive-taking are not
Gifts.

## Art

Glyptic, inscribed objects, and building evidence at Susa span Ur III,
Šimaškian, and successor contexts. Style and political identity are not identical.
No pseudo-Elamite script, seal motif, or guessed highland costume is transferred
into Hollow Grove.

## Language

The surviving king-list tablet is in Akkadian cuneiform. Other relevant records
use Sumerian, Akkadian, and Elamite contexts of different dates. The language or
self-name of all communities grouped as Šimaškian cannot be recovered from the
dynastic label.

## Major texts

- Ur III administrative references, year names, and royal inscriptions naming
  Šimaški, its rulers, campaigns, or diplomatic relations.
- The [Awan–Šimaški dynastic list, Louvre SB 17729](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010188640),
  an Akkadian tablet from Susa dated c. 1800–1600 BCE.
- Later Susian inscriptions crediting earlier rulers with temple work.
- The literary correspondence of Ibbi-Sin and Ishbi-Erra, preserved in later copies.
- Mesopotamian city laments and later traditions about the fall of Ur, shelved as
  literary memory rather than contemporary Šimaškian testimony.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed contribution is not a claim that Šimaški invented coalition warfare
or successor government. It is a historically difficult case where external
records, later dynastic memory, disputed geography, and overlapping rule must be
held together without inventing a clean map. That source discipline is itself
essential to Flynt's pressure testing.

## Historical relationships

Šimaški follows the poorly located [Awan dossier](../age_01_point_02/AWAN.md)
within a changing Elamite and western Iranian field. It interacted with [Ur III](UR_III.md)
through diplomacy, conflict, and the final rupture; then with Isin, Eshnunna, and
Susa-based successor authorities. Neither Awan, Elam, Anshan, Susa, nor Šimaški
is treated as an interchangeable label.

capacity during fracture depends on honest uncertainty, bounded command, custody,

## Library integration

### Primary historical records

- [Louvre SB 17729](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010188640),
  with its later date, Akkadian language, provenance, and missing regnal data.
- Ur III inscriptions and archive references, indexed through the Royal
  Inscriptions and cuneiform corpora.
- Later Susian building inscriptions, with the ruler claimed, commissioning
  context, object date, and historical event kept separate.
- Later literary correspondence and laments, segregated from contemporary records.

### Archaeological evidence

The collection includes Susa stratigraphy, buildings, tablets, sealings, glyptic,
burials, ceramics, metal and stone objects, plus plateau surveys and excavations.
Susa's early excavation record and the difficulty of isolating a Šimaškian
assemblage are catalogue warnings, not blanks filled by speculation.

### Major modern scholarship

- D. T. Potts, [“The Dynasty of Shimashki”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/archaeology-of-elam/dynasty-of-shimashki/8520D6FBF4472DBFE93567B651C8C1F4),
  in *The Archaeology of Elam*.
- Piotr Steinkeller, “On the Dynasty of Šimaški: Twenty Years (or so) After,”
  for revised philological reconstruction.
- Walther Sallaberger and Ingo Schrakamp, eds., third-millennium chronology and
  political history, with source traditions identified.
- Matthew Stolper and Elizabeth Carter, *Elam: Surveys of Political History and
  Archaeology*, and the bibliography attached to the Louvre tablet.
- [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Elam i. History”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/elam-i/),
  used as a scholarly synthesis whose chronology and date require comparison
  with newer work.

Place in the Flynt Age I historical wing under contested command, route and
custody handoffs, collapse studies, source criticism, and succession under
uncertainty. Do not shelve it as a tactical manual or triumphant national epic.

*The Book of Five Rings*, *Achilles in Vietnam*, *The Path of Aloneness /

## Research notes

- Reconcile Middle and alternate chronologies before using exact regnal dates.
- Track each ruler by attestation, source language, object date, and proposed sequence.
- Never plot Šimaški as a precise territory without a visible hypothesis label.
- Separate the c. 2004 event, later king list, later temple claims, and literary memory.
- Review the transition from late Šimaški to early Sukkalmah/Epartid rulers before Point IV.
