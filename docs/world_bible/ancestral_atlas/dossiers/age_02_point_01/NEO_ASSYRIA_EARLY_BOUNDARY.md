# Neo-Assyria — Early Boundary Historical Dossier

Civilization: Neo-Assyria, admitted only at its conventional opening boundary  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point I, Flynt  
Date relevance: c. 911–900 BCE within Adad-nirari II's reign, 911–891 BCE  
Status: Reviewed dossier; only an eleven-year boundary slice is admitted; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes Assyrian royal inscriptions, king and eponym
lists, building records, archaeological phases, and comparison with neighboring
records. Adad-nirari II's royal inscriptions sequence campaigns and claims but
remain selective, ideological, and saturated with violence.

**Scholarly interpretation** conventionally begins Neo-Assyria in 911 BCE with
Adad-nirari II, although recovery under Ashur-dan II began earlier and some
periodizations include him in an early Neo-Assyrian phase from 934. The Point
admits only eleven years, not the mature ninth- to seventh-century empire.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Flynt receives dated sequence, route-aware source review,
and infrastructure-reactivation analysis—not reconquest, hostage-taking,
tribute, deportation, punitive terror, divine command, later palace imagery, or
Capacity merely because an operation was recorded as successful.

## Historical overview

Assyria survived the transformations after 1200 BCE but lost much territorial
reach during the eleventh and tenth centuries. The state, city of Assur,
kingship, temples, scribal practices, annual eponym system, and military
institutions continued. Ashur-dan II, who ruled from 934 to 912 BCE, pursued
recovery, settlement, agricultural, and military measures and sits on the
Middle-to-Neo-Assyrian transition.

Adad-nirari II acceded in 911 BCE and is conventionally treated as the first
Neo-Assyrian king. The c. 911–900 slice includes the opening phase of campaigns
toward upper Mesopotamian, Anatolian, highland, and Babylonian frontiers. His
reign continued to 891, so even his program is truncated by the Point boundary.

The familiar empire of Kalhu's Northwest Palace, monumental wall reliefs,
large-scale provincial expansion, elaborate relay communications, and famous
siege imagery belongs principally to Ashurnasirpal II and later rulers after
883 BCE. Those materials may enter later Points but cannot serve as Point I
evidence or visual shorthand.

## Chronology

- **c. 1050–935 BCE:** Assyrian political continuity persists amid contraction
  and uneven evidence; this remains Middle or transitional Assyria.
- **934–912 BCE:** Ashur-dan II precedes the conventional boundary and is
  associated with recovery and restoration.
- **911 BCE:** Adad-nirari II accedes; conventional opening of Neo-Assyria.
- **910 BCE:** the Assyrian eponym sequence names the king for the year.
- **910–900 BCE:** annual eponyms and royal inscriptions preserve a dated
  operational sequence within the Point.
- **891 BCE:** Adad-nirari II's reign ends, outside the reviewed range.
- **890–884 and 883–859 BCE:** Tukulti-Ninurta II and Ashurnasirpal II belong to Point II.

## Major figures

- **Ashur-dan II:** late Middle/early Neo-Assyrian transition ruler, 934–912 BCE.
- **Adad-nirari II:** conventional first Neo-Assyrian king, 911–891 BCE, and the
  only such king in the reviewed slice.
- **Named limmu officials:** annual eponyms preserve a chronological sequence;
  the title does not imply present command authority.
- **Tukulti-Ninurta II and Ashurnasirpal II:** excluded from Point I except as later transition context.
- Populations described as enemies, captives, settlers, or tributaries in royal
  texts require independent dignity and source-bias labels.

## Major cities

- **Assur:** religious, dynastic, archival, and institutional center.
- **Nineveh and Arbela:** continuing Assyrian centers under changing royal attention.
- **Tigris heartland:** agricultural, settlement, and route base of recovery.
- **Khabur and Tur Abdin approaches:** campaign and contested settlement regions.
- **Nairi and highland routes:** royal campaign geography, not a neutral provincial map.
- **Babylonian frontier:** area of conflict whose clearer outcomes extend after 900.
- **Kalhu/Nimrud:** not yet Ashurnasirpal II's imperial capital in this Point.

## Government

The king worked with Assur's temple and city institutions, royal household,
scribes, annual limmu eponyms, military leaders, officials, stores, settlements,
and local governors or subordinate rulers. These institutions carried Middle
Assyrian precedents forward. The mature standardized provincial system must not
be assumed for the eleven-year slice.

## Religion

The god Ashur remained the principal divine reference of king and state, joined
by Ishtar, Ninurta, Shamash, Adad, and other deities. Royal inscriptions frame
campaigning through divine appointment and cosmic kingship. Flynt preserves
that language as ideology and adversarial evidence, never as command law.

## Economy

Agriculture, settlement, temple and palace estates, craft, livestock, storage,
labor, tribute, and military supply supported continuing institutions and
recovery. Royal accounts of repopulation or restored productivity may conceal
forced movement, extraction, and unequal status.

## Trade

Routes linked the Assyrian heartland to upper Mesopotamia, Anatolian approaches,
Babylonia, the Zagros, and Syrian networks. Texts mention animals, grain, metals,
timber, horses, tribute, and supplies, but conquest, requisition, tribute, and
commercial exchange must remain distinct.

## Science

Annual dating, king lists, scribal copying, route knowledge, building records,
mustering, accounting, divination, and chronological compilation supported
institutional memory. An exact sequence improves auditability but does not make
a royal report neutral or an order valid.

## Technology

Fortifications, roads and river crossings, stored provisions, agricultural
restoration, canals, chariots, developing cavalry, metal weapons, siege practice,
tablets, seals, workshops, and repair supported renewed reach. Iconic siege
engines and mature imperial communications require later evidence.

## Architecture

Temples, walls, gates, palaces, houses, stores, canals, and restored settlements
carried older Assyrian forms into a new expansion phase. Flynt translates
maintenance evidence, sequence, and stopping conditions, not palace reliefs,
lamassu, or a replica military capital.

## Military

Adad-nirari II's inscriptions describe repeated campaigns, forced crossings,
destruction, tribute, hostages, captives, and claimed victories. Their ordering
can support operational source criticism, but the practices themselves are
evidence of coercion and civilian danger. Reconquest is not a Gift and royal
annals are not after-action truth.

## Art

Stelae, inscribed building works, seals, temple objects, and architecture belong
to the boundary phase. The elaborate narrative relief cycles of the Northwest
Palace at Kalhu and later capitals are excluded. No Assyrian imperial motif is
introduced into Flynt visual canon.

## Language

Assyrian Akkadian in cuneiform remained the principal language of royal and
administrative record, alongside scholarly Babylonian traditions and growing
contact with Aramaic-speaking communities. Later bilingual administration and
imperial Aramaic use are not Point I Gifts.

## Major texts

- Royal inscriptions of **Ashur-dan II** as transition context.
- Royal inscriptions of **Adad-nirari II**, including route and campaign sequences.
- The **Assyrian King List**, with manuscript and reconstruction history attached.
- The **Assyrian Eponym List**, including named years 910–900 BCE.
- Building and restoration inscriptions catalogued by object and findspot.
- Neighboring Babylonian, Syrian, and archaeological records used to test royal claims.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not the invention of annual dating, logistics,
siegecraft, cavalry, or imperial communications. Its design value is the narrow
and inspectable combination of a conventional regime boundary, a continuous
annual sequence, route-ordered royal claims, and evidence of reactivating a
system after contraction—all under explicit adversarial and ethical review.

## Historical relationships

Assyria confronted Aramaean-labeled groups, highland polities, and Babylonia
while rebuilding reach from its heartland. Major encounters with Phoenician
cities and Israel become clearer later in the ninth century. Co-placement in
Point I does not imply that all four assignments engaged directly during 911–900.

### Archaeological evidence

Use Assur, Nineveh, Arbela, the Tigris heartland, Khabur approaches, Nairi
routes, and the Babylonian frontier by exact date. Do not use Ashurnasirpal II's
Kalhu palace, later reliefs, Sargonid capitals, or seventh-century administrative
systems as evidence for c. 911–900.
