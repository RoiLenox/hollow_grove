# Phoenicia — Historical Dossier

Civilization: Phoenician coastal cities, retained under the supplied umbrella label  
Atlas assignment: Age II, Point I, Sandmanor  
Date relevance: coastal continuity throughout c. 1200–900 BCE; shared first-millennium identity and inscriptions clearest especially eleventh–tenth centuries BCE  
Status: Reviewed dossier; umbrella label and inscription dates need continuing refinement; Atlas assignment remains Working

## Evidence boundary

**Historical evidence** includes settlement and harbor archaeology, burials,
workshops, objects, short royal and dedicatory inscriptions, imported and
exported materials, and later Egyptian, Assyrian, biblical, Greek, and Latin
accounts. The surviving contemporary written record is small and uneven.

**Scholarly interpretation** uses `Phoenician` for related first-millennium
coastal communities, languages, scripts, and material networks. The term is
Greek, the source communities' self-identifications were commonly civic, and
Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Arwad, and neighboring centers were not one territorial
country. Dates assigned to important inscriptions, especially Ahiram's, remain
debated.

**Hollow Grove adaptation** begins only under Permanent Gifts and Modern Hollow
Grove manifestation. Sandmanor receives independent port-city relationship,
alphabetic transmission, and accountable craft synthesis—not a unified
Phoenician nation, maritime entitlement, merchant sovereignty, colonial
romance, purple-costume identity, or new Court or recognition authority.

## Historical overview

The northern Levantine coast included ancient urban centers with histories
stretching deep into the Bronze Age. Around 1200 BCE, political and exchange
systems changed markedly, but several coastal cities display stronger
continuity than many palace centers elsewhere. Egyptian control contracted;
local kings and institutions operated within renewed coastal and island networks.

During the eleventh and tenth centuries, inscriptions and material patterns
become more recognizably Phoenician in modern classification. Byblos retained a
particularly visible royal inscriptional tradition, while Tyre, Sidon, Arwad,
Sarepta, and other sites followed distinct paths. A common Northwest Semitic
language continuum, related scripts, craft exchange, and maritime connections
did not erase civic rivalry or local cult.

Later Phoenician networks transformed Mediterranean writing, craft, settlement,
and exchange, but most western colonization and the foundation of Carthage lie
after this Point. The dossier therefore concentrates on coastal continuity,
local identity, early alphabetic inscription, and regional synthesis rather
than projecting eighth- or seventh-century reach backward.

## Chronology

- **Around 1200 BCE:** Egyptian imperial power in the Levant contracts and
  regional exchange changes; coastal cities follow different continuity paths.
- **Twelfth–eleventh centuries BCE:** local workshops, ports, settlements, and
  exchange reconnect the coast with Cyprus, Egypt, inland Syria, and the Aegean.
- **Eleventh–tenth centuries BCE:** recognizable Phoenician inscriptional forms
  and city-king records emerge, though several key dates remain debated.
- **Traditionally c. 969–936 BCE:** Hiram I of Tyre belongs to later literary
  traditions about Tyre and Jerusalem; contemporary documentation is limited.
- **Near and after 900 BCE:** Assyrian records, wider Mediterranean settlement,
  and more abundant inscriptions increase; these belong mainly to Point II and later.
- **Traditional 814 BCE:** Carthage's foundation date lies outside this dossier.

## Major figures

- **Ahiram of Byblos:** named on a royal sarcophagus inscription whose date has
  been placed differently within the early first millennium.
- **Ithobaal, son of Ahiram:** commissioner named in the inscription; not to be
  confused automatically with later rulers of similar names.
- **Hiram I of Tyre:** important in biblical and later historical tradition;
  his chronology and works require source-layer labeling.
- **Local rulers, scribes, craftspeople, sailors, merchants, and patrons:** the
  city-level actors through whom material and inscriptional evidence was produced.
- No figure represents a united Phoenician state.

## Major cities

- **Byblos:** long-lived port and royal-inscription center associated with early Phoenician script.
- **Sidon:** major coastal city with its own civic and cultic history.
- **Tyre:** island/coastal center whose larger documented expansion is mainly later.
- **Arwad:** island city on the northern coast.
- **Sarepta:** settlement and workshop evidence important for everyday production.
- **Beirut and Dor:** connected coastal centers with their own archaeological sequences.
- **Cypriot ports:** exchange partners, not Phoenician replicas or automatic colonies.

## Government

Individual cities were ruled by local kings and operated through royal
households, temples, families, merchants, craftspeople, harbors, and local
institutions. The relationships among cities included competition, alliance,
kinship, exchange, and later submission to larger powers. Common script or craft
does not establish a confederation, and classical descriptions cannot be
projected unchanged into the early Point.

## Religion

Local deities and cults included Baalat Gebal at Byblos and, in later or
city-specific evidence, Melqart, Astarte, Eshmun, Baal, and others. Royal
dedications and funerary curses show relationships among identity, place,
lineage, patronage, and divinity. No uniform Phoenician pantheon, ritual, or
theology is imported into Sandmanor.

## Economy

Agriculture in a narrow coastal hinterland, fishing, forestry, harbor activity,
textiles, purple dye, pottery, woodworking, metalwork, ivory carving, glass and
faience, stonework, household production, and exchange supported cities. Royal,
temple, household, workshop, commercial, gift, and tributary movement remain
separate categories.

## Trade

Cedar and other timber, wine, oil, textiles, purple dye, pottery, metals,
ivories, glass, luxury goods, food, and people moved among Levantine cities,
Cyprus, Egypt, Anatolia, inland Syria, and the Aegean. Later western routes are
historically important but not assumed throughout c. 1200–900.

## Science

Navigation, seasonal and coastal knowledge, ship and harbor practice, weights,
material selection, dye production, woodworking, metalworking, and alphabetic
literacy preserve practical expertise. The record does not support one
Phoenician academy or scientific doctrine.

## Technology

Ships and rigging, harbor installations where preserved, timber construction,
stone and mud-brick architecture, pottery kilns, metal and ivory tools, textile
production, murex processing, seals, weights, and incised alphabetic text
supported urban and maritime life. Ship types and harbor reconstructions must be
tied to dated evidence rather than treated as timeless symbols.

## Architecture

Dense coastal settlements, island locations, walls, temples, houses, workshops,
stores, tombs, quays, and harbors responded to constrained land and maritime
access. Sandmanor translates multiple approaches, thresholds, relationship maps,
and visible maker histories, not replica Byblian temples or Tyrian palaces.

## Military

Walls, ships, local competition, changing Egyptian involvement, and later
pressure from inland powers form the strategic context. The Point does not
support a story of continuous naval supremacy. Colonization, tribute, warfare,
enslavement, and imperial accommodation remain cautions rather than Gifts.

## Art

Wood, ivory, metal, textile, pottery, stone, seals, jewelry, and funerary objects
combine local Levantine practice with changing Egyptian, Cypriot, Syrian, and
Aegean forms. Every modern use records maker or workshop where possible,
material, patron, route, date, and scholarly attribution rather than presenting
“Phoenician style” as anonymous hybrid decoration.

## Language

Phoenician belongs to the Canaanite branch of Northwest Semitic and was written
with a consonantal alphabet descended from earlier regional alphabetic
traditions. The reviewed culture did not invent alphabetic writing from nothing.
Early inscriptions document adaptation and transmission; Greek adoption and
development belong chiefly at or after the next boundary.

## Major texts

- The **Ahiram sarcophagus inscription** from Byblos, with its debated date explicit.
- Other early Byblian royal inscriptions, each catalogued by object and chronology.
- The Egyptian **Tale of Wenamun**, a literary composition and manuscript
  witness used cautiously for relations with Byblos.
- Later Assyrian, biblical, Greek, and Latin references retained as later external sources.
- Excavation publications and object catalogues for settlements, workshops,
  burials, harbors, and traded materials.

## Major discoveries or innovations

The reviewed inheritance is not “the first alphabet,” the invention of trade,
or Mediterranean globalization. Its design value lies in independent civic
identities sustaining relationship through a compact inherited writing system,
port networks, and crafts that preserve traceable adaptation among several
traditions.

## Historical relationships

Phoenician-labeled cities interacted with Egypt, Cyprus, Aegean communities,
inland Syrian and Hittite-successor centers, early Israelite and Aramaean
polities, and Assyria. Each relationship changed by city and date. Co-placement
does not imply a shared alliance or equal participation in one network.

## House interpretation

Sandmanor asks how people recognize, relate to, and transform one another. The
coastal evidence shows relationship without necessary unification: cities keep
their names while scripts, crafts, patrons, objects, and travelers cross
boundaries. Current authority remains in the [Sandmanor Constitution](../../../../../SANDMANOR_CONSTITUTION_V2.md).

### Primary historical records

- [DEAPS digital edition of the Ahiram inscription, KAI 1](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11441).
- [UNESCO, Byblos site and alphabet history](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/295/).

  paleography, and competing date attached.
- Excavation and collection records for Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Arwad, Sarepta,
  coastal workshops, burials, imports, and exports.

### Archaeological evidence

Use Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Arwad, Sarepta, Beirut, Dor, and related Cypriot or
Levantine contexts by city and phase. Do not treat a later western settlement,
Carthaginian object, or eighth-century Assyrian depiction as generic Point I evidence.

### Relationship to foundational House classics

*Poetics*, *Man and His Symbols*, *The Archetypes and the Collective
Unconscious*, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, and *The Presentation of Self

identity, symbol, narrative, and social role but cannot decode an inscription,
invent a Phoenician nation, or erase historical makers.
