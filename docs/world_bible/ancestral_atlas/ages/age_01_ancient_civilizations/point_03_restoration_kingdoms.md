## Historical summary

This staggered transition joins the Ur III state and its c. 2004 BCE fall, the emergence of Middle Kingdom Egypt after reunification, late Prepalatial Crete at the threshold of its first court-centered complexes, and Šimaškian power before and after the Ur rupture. The preserved title describes a comparative transition, not four simultaneous or structurally equivalent restoration kingdoms.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Restoration Kingdoms** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [I:2 — First Empires](../../ages/age_01_ancient_civilizations/point_02_first_empires.md) through four different lineage changes: post-Akkadian competition precedes Ur III; Old Kingdom fragmentation precedes Egyptian reunification; Mature Indus and late Prepalatial Crete are parallel House assignments rather than a causal succession; and the Awan–Šimaški relationship remains poorly resolved.

## Transition into next Point

Ur III has already ended when [I:4 — Old Babylonian World](../../ages/age_01_ancient_civilizations/point_04_old_babylonian_world.md) begins. Middle Kingdom Egypt and Minoan Crete continue under the same supplied labels but enter later internal phases, while the Flynt lineage shifts to Old Assyria; the Point boundary is therefore a change of comparative coordinate, not one shared collapse or migration.

## Stonebend — Ur III

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_03.stonebend.ur_iii`<br>
**Primary culture:** Ur III<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial / Terminal<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 2112–2004 BCE. Ur III occupies the early half of the Point and ends with the fall of Ur; post-2004 successor states are not silently folded into this assignment.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for standardized administrative accounting, provincial obligation and redistribution records, dated legal and labor documentation; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Ur-Nammu, Shulgi, Amar-Suen, Shu-Suen, and Ibbi-Sin are the five dynastic kings; Ishbi-Erra and Kindattu belong to the terminal transition and must not be collapsed into Ur III government.<br>
**Major locations:** Ur, Uruk, Nippur, Girsu/Lagash, Umma, Puzrish-Dagan/Drehem, and Susa under changing political relations.<br>
**Major works or records:** Administrative and legal tablets, year names, seal inscriptions, building deposits, royal inscriptions, and later literary copies whose transmission history remains explicit.<br>
**Government and institutions:** The crown worked through provinces, governors, temple and palace households, scribes, messengers, military officials, dependent labor, taxes, deliveries, and rotating obligations; practices varied by archive and region.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** City cults, royal devotion, temple construction, hymns, and the deification of Shulgi and successors shaped public ideology without establishing one uniform lived religion.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Accounting, metrology, scribal education, lexical traditions, legal documentation, and date formulas supported institutional comparison and memory.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay records, seals, canals, river transport, roads and messengers, mud brick, monumental construction, workshops, herding, and large stores made administration material.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Foundation figures, inscribed bricks, seals, statues, and royal hymnic representation joined building, office, ritual, and memory.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Campaigns, garrisons, frontier walls, captive labor, rebellion, and the final Elamite-Susian coalition are well attested but do not become Stonebend Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Institutional movement connected southern cities with the Gulf, northern Mesopotamia, Syria, Susiana, and the Iranian plateau; tax, tribute, trade, and coerced transfer must remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Ur III succeeded the post-Akkadian order, repeatedly campaigned and negotiated eastward, lost provinces under Ibbi-Sin, and ended when Ur fell around 2004 BCE amid a multi-causal political and supply crisis.<br>

## Sandmanor — Middle Kingdom Egypt

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_03.sandmanor.middle_kingdom_egypt`<br>
**Primary culture:** Middle Kingdom Egypt<br>
**Temporal fit:** Emergent / Partial<br>
**Date relevance:** especially c. 2030–1900 BCE. Middle Kingdom Egypt begins after Mentuhotep II's reunification within the supplied range and continues beyond it.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for reunification narrative and renewed kingship, regional artistic synthesis, literature of loyalty exile and return; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Mentuhotep II, Amenemhat I, Senwosret I, and historically attested nonroyal writers or owners such as Heqanakht are central; named literary speakers are not automatically historical authors.<br>
**Major locations:** Thebes and Deir el-Bahri, Lisht, the unlocated residence Itjtawy, Abydos, Elephantine, and early Nubian and eastern-desert routes.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal and private inscriptions, stelae, tomb and temple contexts, Heqanakht letters and accounts, administrative papyri, and literature preserved in manuscripts of different dates.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Reunified kings worked through central offices, regional officials, estates, temples, scribes, and households while relationships between court and provinces changed across Dynasties 11 and 12.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Kingship, ma'at, funerary access, Osirian traditions, local cults, and the memory of disorder were reworked after the First Intermediate Period; elite evidence is not a complete popular theology.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Hieratic writing, accounts, estate correspondence, surveying, calendars, and early Middle Egyptian literary practice are visible; later Lahun scientific papyri remain outside this Point.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Mud-brick and stone construction, river transport, irrigation agriculture, quarrying, woodworking, metalwork, jewelry, and administrative sealing supported renewed institutions.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Theban forms and northern Old Kingdom traditions were combined and transformed in sculpture, relief, architecture, stelae, jewelry, and painted objects.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Reunification involved conflict, and early Middle Kingdom rulers campaigned in Nubia and maintained frontiers; royal claims require source criticism and do not define the Sandmanor inheritance.<br>
**Resources and trade:** The Nile, deserts, Nubia, Sinai, Byblos, and the Levant connected Egypt to gold, stone, copper, timber, luxury materials, and diplomatic exchange.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The Point follows late Old Kingdom fragmentation and regional rule, centers the cultural work of reunification, and leads toward the mature Twelfth Dynasty that continues in Point IV.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Minoan Crete

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_03.glaushouse.minoan_crete`<br>
**Primary culture:** Minoan Crete<br>
**Temporal fit:** Boundary / Transforming<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 2100–1900 BCE. The relevant Cretan evidence is late Prepalatial EM III–MM IA; first-palace institutions belong at the c. 1900 boundary and continue into Point IV.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for regional craft knowledge networks, seal-mediated property and custody marking, settlement continuity at the palace threshold; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** No named ruler, author, or official is securely recoverable for this slice. 'Minoan' is a modern archaeological label derived from legendary Minos, not a self-name preserved in readable records.<br>
**Major locations:** Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, Mochlos, Vasiliki, Myrtos, Archanes, and other regionally distinct settlements and burial landscapes.<br>
**Major works or records:** Settlement sequences, houses, storage, seals and sealings, pottery, stone vessels, metalwork, burials, plant and animal remains, and building phases; readable narrative texts are absent.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Growing centers, households, craft groups, storage, exchange, and communal or elite institutions are visible, but a uniform island state or palace administration cannot be assigned before the threshold evidence.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Burials, figurines, seals, caves, peaks, deposits, and later continuities suggest ritual practice, but named deities and the intentions of images remain uncertain.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Craft recipes, material selection, seal use, storage, construction, navigation, and regional style networks are recoverable materially; Linear A and Cretan Hieroglyphic administration belongs mainly after the Point boundary.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Pottery, stoneworking, metalwork, seal engraving, textile production, boats, storage, and changing settlement construction supported communities connected across Crete and the Aegean.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Regionally varied pottery, seals, stone vessels, jewelry, figurines, and burial goods show skilled material traditions before the later fresco and palace canon.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Weapons and defensible positions exist, but rulers, armies, campaigns, and any island-wide security system remain unrecoverable for this Point.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Cretan communities exchanged materials and craft forms within the island and across the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean, but later palatial redistribution must not be backdated wholesale.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The reviewed phase preserves regional diversity and exchange while settlements reorganized toward the first-palace threshold; this is continuity through transformation, not a sudden replacement by palace society.<br>

## Flynt — Šimaški

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_03.flynt.simaski`<br>
**Primary culture:** Šimaški<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial / Transition<br>
**Date relevance:** especially c. 2004–1900 BCE. Šimaški is visible before 2004 in Ur III relations, but its strongest Point relevance follows the fall of Ur; geography, succession, and political structure remain disputed.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for coalition command during Ur III collapse, Susa-plateau political handoffs, dynastic continuity across overlapping successor rule; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Girnamme, Tazitta, Yabrat, Kindattu, Idaddu I, and later listed rulers are relevant in uneven evidence; sequence, identity, and contemporaneity remain debated.<br>
**Major locations:** Šimaški itself is not securely located. Susa is the principal textual center; Anshan and highland-lowland routes provide regional context without proving one fixed territorial map.<br>
**Major works or records:** Ur III administrative and royal records, the later Awan-Šimaški king list from Susa, building inscriptions, Mesopotamian laments and royal correspondence preserved in later copies, and difficult Susian archaeology.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Named rulers, coalitions, dynastic lists, marriage or diplomatic relations, and later rule at Susa are attested, but offices, borders, succession rules, and the relation among Šimaški, Anshan, Susa, and Elam remain unresolved.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Later records associate Šimaškian rulers with restoration of sanctuaries at Susa, especially Inshushinak; a distinct comprehensive Šimaškian religion cannot be reconstructed.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Cuneiform diplomatic, administrative, dynastic, and building records survive through Mesopotamian and Susian contexts; they do not reveal a general Šimaškian curriculum.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Regional transport, metal and stone movement, fortification, sealing, and building are relevant, but no bounded Šimaškian technological package is securely isolated.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Inscribed building objects and glyptic evidence at Susa belong to shifting Ur III and Šimaški contexts; no uniform House style is inferred.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Kindattu and allied Elamite and Susian forces are tied to the fall of Ur, but hostile and later sources do not supply a neutral doctrine or prove that one campaign caused the entire collapse.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Highland-lowland movement connected Susiana, the Zagros and plateau, Mesopotamia, and Gulf routes; exact Šimaškian control of corridors and commodities remains uncertain.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Šimaškian rulers moved between hostility, diplomacy, and accommodation with Ur III and successor states; overlapping late Šimaški and early Sukkalmah/Epartid rulers complicate a simple dynastic handoff.<br>
