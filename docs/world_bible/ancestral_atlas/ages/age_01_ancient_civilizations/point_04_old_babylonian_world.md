## Historical summary

This sequential coordinate joins the First Dynasty of Babylon and the wider Old Babylonian city-state world, later Middle Kingdom Egypt and its terminal transition, Protopalatial through early Neopalatial Crete, and the Old Assyrian commercial network whose evidence is concentrated in the twentieth through eighteenth centuries BCE. The four assignments overlap broadly but do not occupy the c. 1900–1600 BCE span with equal political continuity or evidence density.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Old Babylonian World** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [I:3 — Restoration Kingdoms](../../ages/age_01_ancient_civilizations/point_03_restoration_kingdoms.md) after Ur III has ended. Middle Kingdom Egypt continues into a later internal phase; late Prepalatial Crete crosses into its first court-centered systems; Babylon emerges within the Isin-Larsa successor world; and Old Assyrian merchant evidence supplies a new Flynt lineage rather than a continuation of Šimaški.

## Transition into next Point

The Point closes unevenly before [I:5 — Great Bronze Age Powers](../../ages/age_01_ancient_civilizations/point_05_great_bronze_age_powers.md): later Middle Kingdom political unity has already fragmented; the richest Old Assyrian merchant record has faded; Babylon's First Dynasty ends near 1595 BCE; and Cretan Neopalatial systems continue beyond the boundary. Point V therefore begins a new great-power coordinate, not one simultaneous succession event.

## Stonebend — Babylon

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_04.stonebend.babylon`<br>
**Primary culture:** Babylon<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Transforming<br>
**Date relevance:** First Dynasty of Babylon c. 1894–1595 BCE; regional hegemony principally c. 1763–1750 BCE. The dynasty substantially spans the Point, but Babylon is one competing power in its early centuries and becomes dominant under Hammurabi; the Hittite sack near 1595 closes the conventional Middle-Chronology period.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for publicly inscribed jurisprudence, sealed contract and case records, place-value mathematical problem practice; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Sumu-abum and Sumu-la-El belong to the dynasty's formation; Hammurabi, Samsu-iluna, Abi-eshuh, Ammi-ditana, Ammi-saduqa, and Samsu-ditana mark its rise, contraction, and end. Rim-Sin of Larsa and Zimri-Lim of Mari are essential contemporaries, not Babylonian rulers.<br>
**Major locations:** Babylon, Sippar, Kish, Borsippa, Nippur, Ur, Larsa, Eshnunna, Mari, and Susa as the findspot of the Hammurabi law stele; territorial control changed sharply across the period.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal inscriptions and year names, the Hammurabi law collection, court and contract tablets, letters and administrative archives, school tablets, lexical lists, mathematical problem texts, omen collections, and literary manuscripts with exact provenience and genre attached.<br>
**Government and institutions:** The king worked through palace, temple, city, provincial, judicial, military, scribal, and household institutions. Local law and archives persisted unevenly; Hammurabi's conquests did not create one timeless or uniform Babylonian administration.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Royal justice was presented as divinely authorized, especially through Shamash on the law stele; city cults and personal devotion remained diverse. The law collection is royal representation and jurisprudence, not a modern constitution.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Scribal schools copied Sumerian and Akkadian literature, lexical lists, law collections, omen series, multiplication tables, coefficient lists, and mathematical problems. The surviving corpus documents procedures and curricula rather than a single abstract scientific method.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay tablets and envelopes, cylinder seals, metrology, canals, boats, mud-brick construction, storage, textile production, metal and stone craft, and road or river messaging supported urban institutions.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Royal stelae, cylinder seals, terracotta plaques, sculpture, jewelry, and manuscript layout joined image, divinity, office, teaching, and household use.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Alliance, siege, conquest, rebellion, deportation, and water control shaped the period. Royal victory accounts and law monuments are not neutral operational records and do not become Stonebend authority.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Contracts and archives document land, houses, labor, loans, partnerships, grain, silver, textiles, livestock, and long-distance materials; private, temple, palace, and tributary transfers must remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The Point follows the Isin-Larsa successor order, includes intensive diplomacy and war among Babylon, Larsa, Mari, Eshnunna, Assur, and Elam, and closes conventionally with the Hittite sack and later Kassite political order.<br>

## Sandmanor — Middle Kingdom Egypt

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_04.sandmanor.middle_kingdom_egypt`<br>
**Primary culture:** Middle Kingdom Egypt<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial / Terminal<br>
**Date relevance:** later Middle Kingdom, especially c. 1900–1650/1640 BCE. Late Dynasty 12 and Dynasty 13 belong here, but the supplied endpoint extends into chronologies classed as the Second Intermediate Period; the exact boundary remains debated.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for private memorials at shared sacred sites, extended-family and associate representation, literature of social voice and moral argument; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Senwosret II, Senwosret III, Amenemhat III, Amenemhat IV, Sobekneferu, and selected Dynasty 13 rulers belong to the slice. Named literary speakers and later manuscript owners are not automatically historical authors.<br>
**Major locations:** Itjtawy remains unlocated; Lahun, Lisht, Dahshur, Hawara, Abydos, Elephantine, Thebes, Semna, Buhen, Sinai, and the eastern Delta provide royal, private, settlement, frontier, and documentary evidence.<br>
**Major works or records:** Lahun business, legal, medical, veterinary, mathematical, ritual, hymn, festival, and letter papyri; private stelae; royal inscriptions; statues; temple and pyramid contexts; seals; administrative documents; and literary manuscripts of different dates.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Late Dynasty 12 saw stronger central royal institutions and altered provincial office; Dynasty 13 retained many institutions through rapid and non-hereditary royal succession. The exact process and date of political fragmentation remain debated.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Royal and divine cults, Osirian pilgrimage and commemoration at Abydos, funerary practice, ma'at, family memory, and personal participation are visible chiefly through elite and institutional evidence.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** The Lahun papyri preserve practical mathematics, healing, veterinary knowledge, accounts, deeds, schedules, letters, and ritual texts. They demonstrate varied scribal practice, not a single comprehensive scientific library.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** River transport, planned settlements, mud-brick and stone building, quarrying, fortification, mining expeditions, sealing, papyrus administration, metalwork, jewelry, and agricultural coordination supported institutions.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Royal portraiture changed markedly under Senwosret III and Amenemhat III; private stelae increasingly represented extended kin and associates; fine jewelry, sculpture, relief, and literature joined public and intimate identity.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Nubian campaigns and fortress networks and activity in Sinai and the Levant are documented through Egyptian sources whose ideological and colonial framing must remain visible.<br>
**Resources and trade:** The Nile and routes through Nubia, Sinai, the Red Sea, Byblos, and the Levant moved gold, copper, stone, timber, luxury goods, people, and information under commercial, diplomatic, extractive, and military conditions.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The slice continues Point III's reunified state, transforms during late Dynasty 12 and 13, and ends before the Point's 1600 BCE boundary in most chronologies; early Second Intermediate material and political division must not be mislabeled as uninterrupted Middle Kingdom rule.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Minoan Crete

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_04.glaushouse.minoan_crete`<br>
**Primary culture:** Minoan Crete<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Transforming<br>
**Date relevance:** Protopalatial through early Neopalatial Crete, c. 1900–1600 BCE. The assignment spans the Point while changing internally around c. 1700 BCE; causes and uniformity of the first-palace destructions and rebuilding remain debated.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-script administrative recordkeeping, court-centered storage and workshop coordination, light water and circulation planning in complex buildings; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** No named ruler, official, author, physician, or builder is securely recoverable because Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A do not provide deciphered language or historical narrative. Minos is legendary and 'Minoan' is a modern label.<br>
**Major locations:** Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, Petras, Monastiraki, Palaikastro, Kommos, Archanes, and peak, cave, settlement, workshop, storage, and burial contexts across Crete.<br>
**Major works or records:** Court-centered building phases, storage rooms and vessels, workshops, streets, drains and water features, sealings, Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A documents, pottery, fresco fragments, tools, botanical and animal remains, and imported materials.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Large court-centered institutions coordinated storage, craft, ritual, and recordkeeping in varying ways, but the titles, territorial scale, hierarchy, and degree of redistribution cannot be read directly from undeciphered documents or later Linear B analogies.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Court complexes, peak and cave sanctuaries, deposits, vessels, seals, and images attest ritual practice. Named deities, priest-kings, a universal goddess, and the intentions of individual images remain unproven.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A supported administrative classification, while building, water, light, storage, craft, navigation, and agricultural knowledge is visible materially. Neither script is translated as readable doctrine.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Ashlar and rubble masonry, timber reinforcement, plaster, courts, drains, water supply, light wells, storage jars, seals, tablets, pottery, textile tools, metalwork, stone vessels, and ships supported complex settlements.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Kamares pottery, seals, stone vessels, metalwork, jewelry, plaster, and early fresco traditions show specialized production and dynamic form; phase and site remain attached to every design source.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Weapons and strategic sites exist, but the absence of massive fortification at some centers does not prove island-wide peace, naval supremacy, or one security system.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Cretan goods and materials moved through Aegean and eastern Mediterranean networks, with evidence of contact involving Egypt, the Levant, Cyprus, and the Cyclades; specific routes and political control remain phase-dependent.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The slice emerges from Point III's late Prepalatial communities, changes around the conventional c. 1700 first-palace destruction/rebuilding horizon, and continues beyond 1600 into later Neopalatial developments excluded from this dossier.<br>

## Flynt — Old Assyria

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_04.flynt.old_assyria`<br>
**Primary culture:** Old Assyria<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full period label / Uneven evidence<br>
**Date relevance:** especially c. 1900–1750/1700 BCE within the conventional c. 2000–1600 BCE Old Assyrian period. The period label reaches the Point endpoint, but the Kültepe merchant archives and strongest historical evidence cluster in the nineteenth and eighteenth centuries, with a major evidence gap thereafter.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for caravan cost and cargo accounting, family-firm correspondence across long routes, sealed loans partnerships and dispute records; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Erishum I, Ikunum, Sargon I, Puzur-Ashur II, Naram-Sin, and later rulers at Assur are relevant; Shamshi-Adad I interrupted the native dynasty after taking Assur. Merchants such as Imdi-ilum and many named women and family members appear in private archives.<br>
**Major locations:** Assur on the Tigris; Kanesh/Kültepe and its kārum; Hattusa, Alişar, Acemhöyük, and other Anatolian trading settlements; routes crossed northern Mesopotamia and the Taurus under changing local jurisdictions.<br>
**Major works or records:** About 24,000 Old Assyrian tablets, chiefly private archives at Kültepe, including letters, caravan accounts, loans, partnerships, contracts, court depositions, quittances, household records, sealings, and a smaller body of royal inscriptions from Assur.<br>
**Government and institutions:** At Assur, the ruler used the title vice-regent or governor of the god Ashur and worked with the city assembly, City Hall, and annual limmu official. Abroad, merchant institutions operated within Anatolian polities through local law, taxes, treaties, and household networks.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** The god Ashur was identified with city and polity; merchants also lived amid Anatolian religious and visual traditions. Private letters and seals show practice more directly than a comprehensive theology.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** A streamlined Old Assyrian cuneiform practice supported letters, accounts, contracts, witnesses, sealed envelopes, dates, credit, route costs, and dispute records over long distance.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay tablets and envelopes, cylinder and stamp seals, textiles, tin and copper exchange, donkey caravans, storage, paved streets, local Anatolian houses, route staging, and weighing supported the network.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Seal impressions combined Mesopotamian and Anatolian motifs over generations; material culture at Kanesh remained strongly local, warning against treating merchant presence as an Assyrian replica city.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Merchants faced political instability, attack, seizure, route closure, and the rise of Shamshi-Adad, but the archive does not supply a single Old Assyrian military doctrine. Commercial risk and continuity, not conquest, drive the Flynt interpretation.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Assyrian merchants moved tin and textiles to Anatolia and received silver and gold, while tracking loans, joint ventures, guides, donkeys, fodder, tolls, customs, losses, and household production through family firms and partnerships.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Assur connected southern supply networks to independent Anatolian kingdoms. Assyrian and Anatolian people lived, worked, married, litigated, and exchanged material traditions together; the word kārum denotes a trading institution or quarter, not a simple modern colony.<br>
