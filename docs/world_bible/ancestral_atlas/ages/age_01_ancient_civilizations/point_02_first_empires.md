## Historical summary

Akkadian expansion, late Old Kingdom institutions, Mature Harappan urban networks, and the poorly located Awan polity overlapped within a connected but uneven third-millennium world. The Point title is preserved; it does not claim that all four assignments were structurally equivalent empires.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **First Empires** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Stonebend — Akkad

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_02.stonebend.akkad`<br>
**Primary culture:** Akkad<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 2350–2150 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for delegated territorial administration, bilingual scribal continuity, long-range institutional coordination; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Sargon, Rimush, Manishtushu, Naram-Sin, and Shar-kali-sharri are central; later literary Sargon traditions must be separated from contemporary or copied royal inscriptions.<br>
**Major locations:** Agade/Akkad (unlocated), Kish, Nippur, Ur, Girsu, Susa, and northern centers under changing degrees of control.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal inscriptions, administrative tablets, seals and sealings, year names, brick stamps, monuments, and later copies of Old Akkadian originals used with transmission history attached.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kings worked through governors, local institutions, scribes, military officials, messengers, estates, and dependent rulers; control was layered and varied rather than uniformly centralized.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Royal devotion and divine patronage remained city-specific; Naram-Sin's deification was a marked political development, not a timeless Akkadian norm.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Akkadian and Sumerian scribal practice, accounting, metrology, lists, and official inscription supported administration across linguistic and regional boundaries.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay records, seals, standardized administrative practice, river and overland transport, metal and stone craft, and building inscriptions supported long-range coordination.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Monumental relief, royal portraiture, and cylinder seals developed powerful new visual forms while retaining and transforming earlier Mesopotamian conventions.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Conquest, garrisons, governors, and repeated revolts are prominent in royal evidence, whose victory claims require source criticism.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Texts and materials connect the empire to Dilmun, Magan, Meluhha, northern Syria, Anatolia, and the Iranian plateau without proving equal direct control everywhere.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Akkadian rulers conquered Mesopotamian cities and campaigned against or negotiated with western Iranian powers; Point co-placement with Awan reflects an attested conflict-and-treaty frontier.<br>

## Sandmanor — Old Kingdom Egypt

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_02.sandmanor.old_kingdom_egypt`<br>
**Primary culture:** Old Kingdom Egypt<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 2350–2150 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for inscribed mortuary remembrance, integrated image-and-text commemoration, provincial service biography; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Unas, Teti, Pepi I, Merenre, Pepi II, Weni, and Harkhuf fall within or near the reviewed span; earlier Giza builders are context, not Point II exemplars.<br>
**Major locations:** Memphis and Saqqara, Abusir, Abydos, Elephantine and Qubbet el-Hawa, plus provincial cemeteries and expedition routes.<br>
**Major works or records:** Pyramid Texts, tomb biographies and reliefs, royal and private inscriptions, sealings, papyri from temple administration, settlement remains, and mortuary complexes.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Royal, temple, estate, and provincial offices coordinated taxation, labor, provisioning, expeditions, and cult; authority became more regionally distributed during Dynasty 6.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Royal mortuary ritual, solar and Osirian traditions, offerings, and the preservation of name and person are documented, but elite mortuary evidence cannot stand for every resident's belief.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Scribal administration, calendrical and accounting practice, architectural surveying, and expedition records are visible; later mathematical and medical papyri must not be backdated.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Stone and mud-brick building, quarrying, copper tools, river transport, carpentry, faience, and organized provisioning supported institutions and monuments.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Relief, statuary, hieroglyphic inscription, offering scenes, and tomb biographies integrated identity, service, ritual, and commemoration.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Weni's biography and frontier expeditions attest organized force, but royal or elite self-presentation is not a neutral campaign archive.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Expeditions and exchange reached Nubia, the Levant, Sinai, and likely Punt, while the Nile joined central and provincial provisioning.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Court and provinces became increasingly interdependent; external expeditions and diplomacy are known mainly through Egyptian institutional records.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Mature Indus Civilization

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_02.glaushouse.mature_indus_civilization`<br>
**Primary culture:** Mature Indus Civilization<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 2350–2150 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for neighborhood water and wastewater management, interregional weights and material standards, distributed urban craft coordination; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** No named ruler or author is securely recoverable. The script remains undeciphered, so political titles, personal names, and doctrines cannot be supplied.<br>
**Major locations:** Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Ganweriwala, Lothal, and many smaller urban, rural, coastal, and craft settlements.<br>
**Major works or records:** Excavated streets, houses, wells, drains, reservoirs, workshops, burials, seals, short inscriptions, weights, ceramics, plant and animal remains, and material provenience studies.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Large-scale coordination is evident, but the balance among city authorities, merchants, landholders, ritual specialists, councils, and wider institutions remains debated.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Seals, figurines, baths, burials, and special buildings provide evidence, but named deities and direct continuity with later religions cannot be established from undeciphered texts.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Standardized weights, craft recipes, planned construction, water control, and short sign sequences document practical classification and coordination without recoverable treatises.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Baked and mud brick, wells, drains, reservoirs, bead drilling, shell and metal craft, carts, boats, seals, weights, and regionally adapted construction supported settlement integrity.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Seals, modeled figures, painted ceramics, jewelry, beads, shell work, and small sculpture joined skilled production to exchange and identity.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Walls and gates exist, but armies, rulers, campaigns, and the scale of organized violence remain poorly documented and do not drive this House interpretation.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Standard weights, seals, raw materials, and provenienced goods show internal exchange and contact with Oman/Magan and Mesopotamia, where Meluhha appears in texts.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Shared conventions linked a wide settlement network without erasing local water systems, materials, layouts, and craft traditions.<br>

## Flynt — Awan

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_02.flynt.awan`<br>
**Primary culture:** Awan<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 2350–2150 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for coalition mobilization across western Iran, treaty protocol across language systems, frontier command under contested sovereignty; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Luhhishan and Hishep-ratep appear in later list and Akkadian campaign traditions; Hita is a possible treaty ruler. Puzur-Inshushinak belongs at or just after the Point boundary and must not represent the whole span.<br>
**Major locations:** Awan itself is unlocated. Susa supplies crucial texts and later monuments but is not proved to be Awan; the central-western Zagros is a working regional hypothesis.<br>
**Major works or records:** Old Akkadian royal inscriptions and later copies, the difficult Naram-Sin treaty from Susa, a later Susa king list, and archaeological evidence from Susiana and western Iran whose Awan attribution remains uncertain.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Sources imply rulers capable of coalition, war, and treaty, but titles, offices, succession, and the relationship among Awan, Elam, Susa, and neighboring polities remain poorly resolved.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** The treaty invokes a divine order and Susa had its own cults, but an Awan-specific theology cannot be reconstructed.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** The treaty demonstrates cross-language cuneiform diplomatic practice; it does not reveal a general Awan scribal curriculum or administration.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Awan-specific technology cannot be isolated securely. Regional metal, stone, sealing, transport, and fortification evidence must retain exact archaeological provenance.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** No securely bounded Awan style is established. Puzur-Inshushinak's inscribed monuments are a late transition, not a visual template for the whole Point.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Awan and allied western Iranian powers appear in repeated Akkadian victory claims; those sources establish conflict but not neutral tactical detail.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Mesopotamian interest in western Iranian wood, stone, and metals is clear, while Awan-specific routes and institutions remain hypothetical because the center is unlocated.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Conflict with Sargon and Rimush and a probable treaty in Naram-Sin's reign place Awan within the Akkadian frontier system; the later rise of Puzur-Inshushinak leads toward the Point III transition.<br>
