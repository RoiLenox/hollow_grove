## Stonebend — Sumer

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_01.stonebend.sumer`<br>
**Primary culture:** Sumer<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 3200–2500 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for administrative recordkeeping, irrigation and canal governance, urban institutional planning; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** No individual can securely represent the entire 3200–2500 BCE span; later king-list traditions must not be read as straightforward contemporary biography.<br>
**Major locations:** Uruk, Ur, Eridu, Nippur, and Kish, with settlement prominence changing across the range.<br>
**Major works or records:** Proto-cuneiform and early cuneiform administrative tablets, sealings, lexical lists, buildings, canals, ceramics, and settlement archaeology.<br>
**Government and institutions:** City-level rulers and large institutional households are visible, but a single uniform 'temple-state' model is not supported for every city and century.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** City cults, offerings, and ritual institutions are archaeologically visible; much surviving Sumerian religious literature is later than this Point.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Numerical notation, metrology, accounting, and lexical ordering are better evidenced than abstract scientific doctrine.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay-tablet accounting, seals, mud-brick construction, canal agriculture, craft specialization, and wheeled transport developed within a changing urban landscape.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Cylinder seals, carved vessels, architectural ornament, and early sculpture joined image, office, labor, and ritual.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Conflict among cities is clearer in later Early Dynastic evidence; it is not the basis of Stonebend's Point I inheritance.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Southern cities depended on regional agriculture and imported stone, timber, and metals through overlapping river, Gulf, and overland networks.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Contact with Susiana and wider Iranian and Gulf networks is evidenced materially; Point co-placement does not prove a single political system.<br>

## Sandmanor — Early Dynastic Egypt

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_01.sandmanor.early_dynastic_egypt`<br>
**Primary culture:** Early Dynastic Egypt<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 3200–2500 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for royal titulary and emblematic identity, ceremonial image-language, court ritual and commemorative practice; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Narmer, Aha, Merneith, Den, and Khasekhemwy are historically relevant, but individual sequences and the identification of Menes remain debated.<br>
**Major locations:** Abydos, Hierakonpolis, the Memphis–Saqqara region, Tarkhan, and other Nile Valley administrative and mortuary sites.<br>
**Major works or records:** Inscribed labels, seal impressions, royal names in serekhs, the Narmer Palette, tomb assemblages, architecture, and later king lists used cautiously.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Territorial kingship operated through officials, storerooms, sealings, taxation or collection, and royal mortuary institutions.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Royal and mortuary ritual, local cults, and emblematic divine associations are visible, while later theological systems cannot simply be projected backward.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Writing, accounting, calendrical organization, and standardized image conventions expanded; detailed scientific and medical texts do not survive from this Point.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Mud-brick monumental building, stone working, copper tools, faience, boat use, labels, and seals supported administration and ceremony.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Formal pose, scale, regalia, titulary, and ordered registers made identity and authority legible through repeated visual conventions.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Images of domination and captives express royal ideology; they are not transparent battle reports and require cautious interpretation.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Materials and objects attest exchange with Nubia, the southern Levant, and other Nile regions, alongside centrally organized provisioning.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** State formation joined Upper and Lower Egyptian regions unevenly; evidence for unification is a process, not one securely documented event.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Early Indus Civilization

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_01.glaushouse.early_indus_civilization`<br>
**Primary culture:** Early Indus Civilization<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 3200–2500 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for planned settlement organization, water management across long-lived settlements, emerging craft and measurement standardization; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** No named historical ruler or author is securely recoverable; the script remains undeciphered and political offices are inferred from material evidence.<br>
**Major locations:** Early levels at Harappa, Kot Diji, Kalibangan, Rehman Dheri, and Dholavira, among a much broader and regionally varied settlement field.<br>
**Major works or records:** Stratified architecture, ceramics, plant and animal remains, craft debris, weights, seals and sealings, early signs, wells, drains, and water-management works.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Settlement hierarchy and coordination are visible, but rulers, state titles, and a single political constitution are unknown.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Burials, figurines, motifs, and special spaces are evidence; named deities and later religious systems cannot be securely assigned.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Emerging weights, craft recipes, spatial planning, and signs show practical classification and coordination without a deciphered textual corpus.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Mud-brick construction, pottery, bead and shell work, copper use, seals, weights, and locally varied water control developed before and across the Mature transition.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Painted ceramics, ornaments, beads, figurines, and seal imagery participated in regional identities and exchange.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Fortifications occur at some sites, but political violence and military organization remain too poorly documented to drive the House interpretation.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Stone, shell, metals, and craft goods moved through interregional networks; the long-distance system intensified over time.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Regional traditions increasingly converged before c. 2600 BCE; the Point extends to 2500 and therefore crosses into Mature Harappan chronology.<br>

## Flynt — Elam

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_01.flynt.elam`<br>
**Primary culture:** Elam<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 3200–2500 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for highland-lowland exchange logistics, tablet-and-seal accounting, distributed craft and storage coordination; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** No securely named Proto-Elamite ruler or author is available; later Awan rulers must not be imported into this earlier evidence.<br>
**Major locations:** Susa, Anshan (Tal-e Malyan), Tepe Sialk, Tepe Yahya, and other plateau administrative centers linked by related material and writing practices.<br>
**Major works or records:** Proto-Elamite administrative tablets, numerical systems, seals and sealings, ceramics, metal and stone objects, storage buildings, workshops, and settlement archaeology.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Large households or institutions administered grain, livestock, labor, and storage, but office names and political structure remain uncertain.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Seal imagery and special buildings survive, but religion cannot be reconstructed securely from the undeciphered administrative corpus.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Numerical systems, commodity accounting, totals, seal control, and document layout demonstrate administrative technique under severe decipherment limits.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay-tablet accounting, sealing, storage, mud-brick public construction, ceramics, and stone and metal craft supported dispersed centers.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Distinctive glyptic imagery, painted ceramics, and animal figures circulated with administrative practices and highland materials.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Walls and later conflict do not establish a detailed Proto-Elamite military system; Flynt receives logistics and adaptive capacity, not invented militarism.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Lowland Susiana and highland centers exchanged agricultural products, labor, stone, metals, and crafted goods across ecological zones.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Susa alternated between strong Mesopotamian and plateau connections; Proto-Elamite writing spread widely, but its language is unknown and not proven to be Elamite.<br>
