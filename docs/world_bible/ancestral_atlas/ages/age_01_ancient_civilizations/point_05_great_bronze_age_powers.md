Boundary rationale: The supplied c. 1550–1200 BCE coordinate is retained as a staggered comparative window. New Kingdom Egypt begins near the opening but continues to c. 1070; Kassite rule emerges through an obscure transition and continues to c. 1155; the Hittite imperial phase enters around c. 1400 and ends near 1200/1180; Middle Assyrian expansion enters with Ashur-uballit I around 1365 and continues beyond the endpoint. ## Historical summary

New Kingdom Egypt, Kassite Babylonia, the later Hittite imperial system, and Middle Assyrian expansion participated in a densely connected Late Bronze Age world of courts, envoys, treaties, archives, tribute, craft, and war. Their strongest common overlap lies principally in the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE. Around and after 1200, Hattusa and several eastern Mediterranean palace systems ended or transformed, while Egypt, Kassite Babylonia, Assyria, and successor Hittite centers followed different timelines.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Great Bronze Age Powers** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [I:4 — Old Babylonian World](../../ages/age_01_ancient_civilizations/point_04_old_babylonian_world.md) through several unequal transitions: the New Kingdom follows Egypt's Second Intermediate division; Kassite rule emerges after Babylon's First Dynasty through an obscure early phase; the later Hittite imperial system develops from an older Anatolian kingdom; and Middle Assyrian expansion follows centuries in which Assur was constrained by Mitanni. No single event begins all four assignments.

## Transition into next Point

The supplied boundary opens [II:1 — Early Iron Age](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_01_early_iron_age.md) as a post-palatial transition, not a clean replacement of four fallen states. Central rule at Hattusa ends, several eastern Mediterranean palace systems transform, and Egyptian Levantine control contracts, while New Kingdom Egypt, Kassite Babylonia, Middle Assyria, Carchemish, and many local networks continue on different trajectories beyond 1200 BCE.

**Atlas interpretation:** Retain the supplied label as a transition marker for the uneven eastern Mediterranean and Near Eastern crises around and after 1200 BCE, not as one simultaneous event or the disappearance of all four assignments.

## Stonebend — New Kingdom Egypt

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_05.stonebend.new_kingdom_egypt`<br>
**Primary culture:** New Kingdom Egypt<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** reviewed slice c. 1550–1200 BCE within the New Kingdom, c. 1550–1070 BCE. Dynasties 18 and 19 substantially span the Point, but the New Kingdom continues for more than a century after the supplied endpoint; Ramesses III and his conflict inscriptions belong chiefly beyond this reviewed slice.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for successive-reign building attribution, specialist work-community records, drafting and correction practice; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Ahmose I, Hatshepsut, Thutmose III, Amenhotep III, Akhenaten, Nefertiti, Tutankhamun, Horemheb, Seti I, Ramesses II, and Seti II are representative. Ramesses III belongs chiefly after the reviewed endpoint and appears only as transition context.<br>
**Major locations:** Thebes, Karnak, Luxor, Deir el-Bahri, the Valley of the Kings, Deir el-Medina, Memphis, Pi-Ramesses, Akhetaten/Amarna, Nubian centers including Gebel Barkal, and changing Levantine administrative sites.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal and private inscriptions, temple and tomb programs, foundation deposits, the Amarna diplomatic archive, Deir el-Medina ostraca and papyri, work and supply accounts, letters, legal records, medical and literary papyri, sketches, plans, and archaeological building phases.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kingship worked through viziers, treasuries, temples, military and provincial officials, scribes, work crews, and imperial administrators. Institutions changed across nearly four centuries; monumental presentation is not a transparent administrative chart.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Amun-Re and many local and state cults, royal mortuary religion, household practice, and changing afterlife texts coexist with the exceptional Aten-centered Amarna program and its later reversal.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Scribal education, surveying, accounting, medicine, calendrics, craft apprenticeship, drafting, correction, and long textual transmission are visible through papyri, ostraca, buildings, and tools without constituting one uniform academy.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Stone and mud-brick construction, quarrying, ramps and transport, chariots, shipbuilding, metalwork, glass and faience, pigments, irrigation, storage, writing materials, and specialist tomb production supported state and household life.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Successive temple additions, rock-cut tombs, statuary, relief, painted burial chambers, luxury craft, informal ostraca, and Amarna experimentation reveal both strong conventions and deliberate change.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Egyptian expansion in Nubia and the Levant relied on campaigns, garrisons, chariots, tribute, and coerced movement. Royal victory scenes are ideological sources and conquest is not a Stonebend Gift.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Nile, Red Sea, Nubian, Mediterranean, and Levantine routes moved grain, gold, copper, timber, horses, chariots, glass, luxury goods, envoys, and tribute; exchange, taxation, diplomacy, and extraction remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Egypt corresponded and competed with Hatti, Mitanni, Kassite Babylonia, Assyria, Cyprus, and Levantine polities. Ramesses II's treaty with Hattusili III belongs to both Egyptian and Hittite archives, but the Stonebend interpretation centers attributed works and work records rather than diplomacy.<br>

## Sandmanor — Hittite Empire

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_05.sandmanor.hittite_empire`<br>
**Primary culture:** Hittite Empire<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial / Terminal<br>
**Date relevance:** Hittite imperial phase especially c. 1400–1200/1180 BCE. The Hittite kingdom predates the Point, but the supplied Empire assignment is strongest under the later Great Kings; Hattusa's end does not erase continuing successor traditions at centers such as Carchemish.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for treaty archives distinguishing parity and subordination, multilingual diplomatic correspondence, preservation of regional ritual traditions; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Tudhaliya I/II, Suppiluliuma I, Arnuwanda II, Mursili II, Muwatalli II, Hattusili III, Queen Puduhepa, Tudhaliya IV, and Suppiluliuma II are central; Egyptian, Mitannian, Assyrian, Ugaritic, and western Anatolian rulers appear as independent counterparts or subordinates according to each document.<br>
**Major locations:** Hattusa, Yazilikaya, Sapinuwa, Sarissa, Tarhuntassa, Carchemish, Ugarit, Aleppo, Kizzuwatna, Amurru, Wilusa, and other Anatolian and Syrian polities under changing relationships.<br>
**Major works or records:** Hittite and Akkadian treaties, diplomatic letters, annals, prayers, edicts, laws, instructions, inventories, land grants, oracle reports, myths, festival and purification rituals, sealings, monuments, and archaeological contexts from Hattusa and regional centers.<br>
**Government and institutions:** The Great King and royal household worked with the Great Queen, princes, high officials, governors, viceroys, councils, temples, and subordinate rulers. Parity treaties, subordination treaties, annexed lands, and indirect rule were not one uniform relationship.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Hittite, Hattian, Hurrian, Luwian, Syrian, and Mesopotamian deities and rituals entered a changing state cult. The archival preservation of many traditions does not prove frictionless tolerance or a single syncretic theology.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Multilingual scribes copied, translated, catalogued, and adapted diplomatic, ritual, legal, historical, divinatory, medical, and literary texts. Surviving tablets reflect palace and temple selection, copying, and deposition.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortifications, gates, roads, grain storage, reservoirs, cuneiform tablets, seals, bronze tools and weapons, chariots, horse training, textiles, and regional craft systems supported the capital and wider state.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Monumental gates, reliefs, royal and divine images, seals, metalwork, pottery, and Yazilikaya's rock sanctuary joined court representation with varied Anatolian and Syrian traditions.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Campaigns, siege, chariot forces, garrisons, levies, deportation, tribute, and vassal military obligations sustained expansion. Treaties often record hierarchy under coercive conditions and are not models of modern consent.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Anatolian metals and agriculture connected with Syrian ports, Cyprus, Mesopotamia, and Egypt through merchants, palace exchange, tribute, gifts, and contested corridors; archival absence does not imply a closed royal economy.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Hatti negotiated, fought, intermarried, and exchanged envoys with Egypt, Mitanni, Assyria, Kassite Babylonia, Ahhiyawa, Cyprus, Ugarit, and many Anatolian polities. The end of Hattusa did not erase successor authority at Carchemish and other centers.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Kassite Babylonia

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_05.glaushouse.kassite_babylonia`<br>
**Primary culture:** Kassite Babylonia<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Extends beyond boundary<br>
**Date relevance:** Kassite rule c. 1595–1155 BCE; consolidated Babylonia especially from c. 1475 BCE. The Point contains the long Kassite order, but its earliest political reconstruction is obscure and the dynasty survives beyond 1200; it does not terminate in the same event as Hattusa.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for temple-custodied land transaction monuments, long-duration scribal and literary preservation, provincial resource and labor archives; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Agum II is associated with early restoration traditions but remains chronologically difficult; Kurigalzu I, Burnaburiash II, Kadashman-Enlil I, Kurigalzu II, Nazi-Maruttash, and Kashtiliashu IV belong to better-attested phases. Tukulti-Ninurta I is an Assyrian conqueror and adversarial source, not a Kassite ruler.<br>
**Major locations:** Babylon, Nippur, Dur-Kurigalzu, Ur, Uruk, Sippar, Isin, the Sealand, Susa as the later findspot of removed monuments, and Dilmun within changing southern connections.<br>
**Major works or records:** Nippur administrative tablets, royal inscriptions, kudurru/naru land-transaction monuments, seals, building remains, the Amarna letters, diplomatic gifts, lexical and literary manuscripts, later learned traditions, and Assyrian or Elamite records read with political bias attached.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kings ruled through royal households, provincial governors, temples, urban institutions, land grants, military obligations, and administrative networks. Nippur's abundant archive cannot stand for every city or century, and Kassite ethnicity did not replace Babylonian civic life wholesale.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Mesopotamian cults and languages continued while Kassite divine names and symbols entered royal practice. Temple custody and divine sanctions protected some durable records, but this does not make land hierarchy or royal grants a healing institution.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Scribal transmission in Sumerian and Akkadian, lexical and literary collecting, monumental archaism, administration, measurement, and diplomacy continued across a long political order; later claims about canonization require careful dating.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Clay archives, seals, monumental stone records, irrigation agriculture, storage, textile and craft production, horse and chariot systems, and the planned royal center at Dur-Kurigalzu supported institutions.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Kudurru imagery, seals, glazed architectural elements, sculpture, and archaizing inscriptions combined Babylonian traditions with Kassite royal identity; 'boundary stone' remains a conventional but misleading modern label for temple-held monuments.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Conflict with Assyria and Elam, control of the Sealand, and temporary Assyrian occupation shaped the dynasty. Captivity, coerced labor, land privilege, and conquest remain historical cautions, not Glaüshouse Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Babylonia exchanged horses, textiles, metals, precious materials, agricultural goods, envoys, and royal gifts with Egypt, Hatti, Mitanni, Assyria, Elam, and Dilmun through both institutional and private channels.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Kassite kings joined the Late Bronze Age great-king correspondence system and survived the crisis around 1200 before Elamite campaigns ended the dynasty near 1155. The Point's terminal label therefore marks pressure and transformation, not Kassite disappearance.<br>

## Flynt — Middle Assyria

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_01.point_05.flynt.middle_assyria`<br>
**Primary culture:** Middle Assyria<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Middle Assyrian expansion c. 1365–1200 BCE within a period continuing to c. 1050 BCE. Ashur-uballit I opens the reviewed imperial slice well after 1550. Assyria remains a major polity beyond 1200, so the terminal collapse label cannot describe its disappearance.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for eponym-dated administrative records, provincial supply and personnel accounting, fortified route and garrison logistics; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Ashur-uballit I, Enlil-nirari, Arik-den-ili, Adad-nirari I, Shalmaneser I, and Tukulti-Ninurta I are central. Mitannian, Hittite, Kassite, and Babylonian rulers appear as rivals or correspondents; later Tiglath-pileser I lies outside the reviewed slice.<br>
**Major locations:** Assur, Nineveh, Arbela, Kar-Tukulti-Ninurta, the Habur and Balikh regions, Hanigalbat, Tell Sheikh Hamad/Dur-Katlimmu, Tell Sabi Abyad, and frontier sites under changing Assyrian control.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal inscriptions, eponym lists and dated administrative tablets, provincial archives, ration and personnel records, letters, legal deeds, Middle Assyrian Laws and palace decrees, seals, buildings, weapons, and records from conquered or neighboring polities.<br>
**Government and institutions:** The king worked through palace officials, annual limmu eponyms, governors, district officers, military commanders, temples, estates, and provincial administrations. Records distinguish ranks and dependent populations while exposing coercive state control.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Ashur remained city, god, and imperial reference; Ishtar, Ninurta, and other cults were important. Royal inscriptions cast conquest as divinely ordered and require comparison with administrative and archaeological evidence.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Eponym dating, scribal copying, route and personnel accounting, legal documentation, divination, and technical records supported long-range administration. A dated tablet is evidence of a record system, not proof of legitimate command.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortified centers, roads and river routes, chariots, bronze weapons and armor, granaries, workshops, canals, clay records, seals, weights, and staged supply systems supported armies and provinces.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Royal stelae, seals, carved objects, temple and palace construction, and the new city of Kar-Tukulti-Ninurta joined divine, dynastic, and military representation; victory imagery remains adversarial evidence.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Campaigning, siege, deportation, forced resettlement, conscription, tribute, massacre claims, and garrisoning drove expansion. These practices are studied as operational and moral failure conditions, never celebrated as Capacity.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Agricultural surplus, textiles, metals, horses, livestock, tribute, and supplies moved through Assur and provincial routes. Military requisition, deportee labor, palace distribution, taxation, and exchange must not be conflated.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Assyria displaced Mitanni/Hanigalbat, bordered Hatti, corresponded with Egypt, fought Kassite Babylonia, and occupied Babylon temporarily. Its institutional and territorial history continues after the Point boundary despite later contraction.<br>
