## Stonebend — Byzantine Empire

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_03.point_01.stonebend.byzantine_empire`<br>
**Primary culture:** Byzantine Empire<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** 476–610 CE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for Roman legal continuity, diplomatic statecraft, fortified urbanism; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Anastasius, Justinian, Theodora, Belisarius, Narses, Maurice, Phocas, emperors, jurists, generals, governors, bishops, merchants, and provincial communities.<br>
**Major locations:** Constantinople, Rome, Ravenna, Carthage, Antioch, Alexandria, the Balkans, Anatolia, Italy, Africa, and eastern frontier cities.<br>
**Major works or records:** Justinianic laws, chronicles, coins, buildings, papyri, inscriptions, church records, archaeology, and diplomatic correspondence.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Emperor, court, army, prefectures, provincial governors, cities, churches, tax offices, and local institutions formed layered eastern Roman administration.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Roman civic identity, Christianity, Judaism, local cults, philosophy, and regional practice coexisted amid imperial legal and fiscal pressure.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Law, rhetoric, surveying, engineering, medicine, theology, translation, and archive practice circulated through cities and institutions.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Walls, harbors, cisterns, roads, bridges, churches, workshops, mints, and military supply systems were maintained or transformed at different local tempos.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Mosaics, architecture, manuscripts, coins, sculpture, and reused monuments negotiated Roman and Christian authority.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Persian and Balkan wars, reconquest, siege, plague, requisition, captivity, and civil conflict shaped the period; restoration is not innocence.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, silk, spices, metals, coin, books, medicines, and information moved through capital, provincial, Black Sea, Red Sea, and Mediterranean routes.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Eastern Romans engaged Sasanian Persia, Goths, Lombards, Slavs, Armenia, Arab groups, Jewish communities, and western successor polities through diplomacy, law, trade, migration, and war.<br>

## Sandmanor — Franks

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>

**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for successor kingship, customary law, church-state alliance; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Clovis, Clotilde, Childebert, Chlothar, Theuderic, Theudebert, Gregory of Tours, bishops, queens, aristocrats, judges, warriors, artisans, and rural communities.<br>
**Major locations:** Tournai, Paris, Reims, Soissons, Orléans, Burgundy, Austrasia, Neustria, Aquitaine, Tours, and episcopal and monastic centers.<br>
**Major works or records:** Gregory of Tours, Frankish law codes, royal letters, saints' lives, coins, burials, churches, inscriptions, and settlement archaeology.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kings, royal households, aristocrats, bishops, cities, local courts, customary practice, and written law formed negotiated and regionally varied authority.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Christian conversion and patronage interacted with local practice, Roman civic memory, customary norms, and diverse community identities.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Law, genealogy, letter writing, liturgy, rhetoric, craft, agriculture, and episcopal recordkeeping circulated through elite and local institutions.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Roads, river routes, churches, workshops, agricultural estates, fortifications, mints, and burial landscapes supported successor communities.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Goldwork, weapons, fibulae, manuscripts, churches, inscriptions, and Roman-reused forms expressed changing elite and local identities.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Conquest, feud, succession conflict, siege, tribute, enslavement, and aristocratic violence shaped kingdoms; kingship is not a modern national model.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, wine, salt, metals, textiles, coin, livestock, slaves, and river and overland information routes connected regions.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Frankish polities engaged Roman cities, Burgundians, Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Bretons, bishops, monasteries, and neighboring peoples through war, marriage, law, patronage, and trade.<br>
