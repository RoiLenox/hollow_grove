## Historical summary

Roman constitutional and military fracture, expanding but diverse Christian networks, Alexandrian textual and care traditions, and the Parthian-to-Sasanian transition reshape the late classical world without moving on one clock.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Crisis and Conversion** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Stonebend — Roman Empire

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>

**Primary culture:** Roman Empire<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** 180–313 CE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for public law and record, municipal administration, infrastructure standards; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Septimius Severus, Caracalla, Julia Domna, Decius, Valerian, Gallienus, Zenobia, Postumus, Aurelian, Diocletian, Galerius, Constantine, Licinius, governors, jurists, soldiers, municipal officers, craftspeople, enslaved people, and provincial communities.<br>
**Major locations:** Rome, Milan, Trier, Nicomedia, Antioch, Alexandria, Palmyra, Carthage, frontier capitals, provincial cities, forts, ports, roads, and water systems.<br>
**Major works or records:** Legal constitutions, papyri, inscriptions, coins, municipal records, archaeology, Cassius Dio, Herodian, Lactantius, Eusebius, imperial panegyrics, and later epitomes with source-position labels.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Emperor, court, army, Senate, governors, cities, tax offices, courts, mints, and councils changed across Severan rule, third-century fracture, Aurelianic reunification, and the Tetrarchy.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Traditional civic cults, imperial rites, local religions, Judaism, Christianity, Manichaeism, philosophy, and household practice coexisted under changing and uneven imperial policy.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Law, medicine, surveying, engineering, rhetoric, philosophy, calendars, and administrative accounting circulated through schools, workshops, households, armies, and offices.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortifications, roads, bridges, ports, water systems, mints, workshops, ships, mills, archives, and military supply systems were maintained or transformed at different local tempos.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Coins, imperial portraits, sarcophagi, mosaics, wall painting, inscriptions, funerary art, and Tetrarchic concord imagery negotiated authority and community.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Civil war, invasion, regional breakaways, siege, forced labor, captivity, and requisition shaped the era; reunification and multi-ruler coordination remain consequence records, not rightful-command models.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Tax, requisition, grain, wine, oil, textiles, papyrus, metals, ceramics, animals, coin, military supplies, and information sustained unequal systems through disruption.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Roman communities engaged Sasanians, Goths and other northern peoples, Palmyra, Armenia, African and eastern provinces, Jewish and Christian communities, and rival Roman regimes through war, law, diplomacy, trade, and migration.<br>

**Resources and trade:** Household support, gifts, alms, burial associations, hospitality, copying, travel, and emerging communal property sustained communities at uncertain scales.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Christian communities engaged Jewish communities, Roman officials, philosophers, households, urban associations, soldiers, rival Christian teachers, and communities across Roman and Sasanian frontiers.<br>

## Flynt — Sasanian Persia

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_11.flynt.sasanian_persia`<br>
**Primary culture:** Sasanian Persia<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial<br>
**Date relevance:** 224–313 CE. Sasanian rule begins within the Point; the Parthian-Sasanian transition occupies 180–224 CE.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for imperial administration, frontier strategy, scholarly and religious institutions; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Vologases VI, Ardavan or Artabanus IV, Ardashir I, Shapur I, Mani, Kartir, Bahram I and II, Narseh, Shapur II, local kings, noble houses, priests, city leaders, soldiers, deported communities, artisans, merchants, and frontier residents.<br>
**Major locations:** Istakhr, Firuzabad, Bishapur, Ctesiphon, Susa, Hatra, Nisibis, Armenia, Mesopotamia, Fars, Khuzestan, the Iranian plateau, and eastern territories.<br>
**Major works or records:** Shapur I's inscription at the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht, Kartir's inscriptions, the Paikuli inscription, reliefs, coins, seals, Mani's writings, Roman and Syriac narratives, archaeology, and later chronicles.<br>
**Government and institutions:** The King of Kings, royal family, provincial kings, noble houses, officials, cities, temples, commanders, and local authorities shared layered power; early Sasanian rule retained substantial Parthian continuities.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Zoroastrian institutions and royal fire cults gained prominence while Judaism, Christianity, Manichaeism, local cults, and other traditions continued under changing court relationships.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Multilingual administration, surveying, irrigation, medicine, astronomy, calendars, military engineering, theology, and translation moved among courts, cities, temples, communities, and captives.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Rock-cut monuments, fortifications, siege practice, cavalry equipment, irrigation, roads, bridges, mints, planned cities, workshops, and caravan systems expressed capacity and dependency.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Investiture and victory reliefs, royal coinage, stucco, silverwork, seals, architecture, textiles, and multilingual inscriptions made kingship visible while local traditions remained active.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Roman-Parthian and Roman-Sasanian wars, cavalry, siege, Armenia, Hatra, Palmyra, Valerian's capture, deportation, and shifting frontier cities reveal capacity with severe civilian consequences.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Agriculture, irrigation, taxation, coinage, silk, horses, metals, textiles, glass, spices, grain, books, captives, artisans, and information moved through regional and long-distance networks.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Parthian and Sasanian rulers engaged Rome, Armenia, Hatra, Palmyra, Mesopotamian cities, Kushan and Central Asian regions, Gulf and Indian Ocean networks, Jewish and Christian communities, and Manichaean missions.<br>
