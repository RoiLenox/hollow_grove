## Historical summary

Highland communities identified with early Israel, long-lived coastal cities later grouped as Phoenician, mobile and settled communities later associated with Aram, and continuing Assyrian institutions followed different paths after 1200 BCE. The supplied labels become clearest at different moments: Israel is attested as a people near the opening but monarchic scale is debated; Phoenician and Aramaean political identities remain local and emergent; and conventionally defined Neo-Assyria enters only in 911 BCE.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Early Iron Age** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [I:5 — Great Bronze Age Powers](../../ages/age_01_ancient_civilizations/point_05_great_bronze_age_powers.md) through uneven continuity rather than a universal reset. Egyptian and Assyrian institutions continue beyond 1200; Hittite-successor centers survive; coastal cities retain older populations and exchange; and new or newly visible highland, mobile, and city-state communities develop within transformed regional systems. The Point therefore records emergence, local recombination, and contraction alongside survival.

## Transition into next Point

At [II:2 — Kings, Prophets, and Assyrian Expansion](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_02_kings_prophets_and_assyrian_expansion.md), the labels become more securely political but still do not synchronize perfectly: the northern Kingdom of Israel and its external attestations become clearer in the ninth century; Phoenician cities expand their documented networks; Aramaean states and inscriptions become more visible; and Neo-Assyrian rulers after 900 develop the expansion whose fall of Samaria closes Point II. Point I's settlement, script, and recovery evidence remains a foundation layer rather than being overwritten.

## Stonebend — Kingdom of Israel

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_01.stonebend.kingdom_of_israel`<br>
**Primary culture:** Kingdom of Israel<br>
**Temporal fit:** People full / Kingdom emergent<br>
**Date relevance:** Israel named c. 1208 BCE; highland settlement through Iron I; disputed monarchic formation especially eleventh–tenth centuries BCE. The supplied Kingdom of Israel label is retained, but the Point distinguishes the externally attested people, archaeologically varied highland communities, disputed early monarchy, and northern kingdom emerging near c. 930. Samaria and the best external evidence for the northern state belong after this Point.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for clustered highland settlement networks, household agrarian storage and water practice, layered preservation of foundation and kingship traditions; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** The Merneptah inscription names Israel but no ruler. Saul, David, Solomon, and the division under Rehoboam and Jeroboam belong to later textual traditions whose historical cores, dates, scale, and political geography remain debated. Omri and Samaria fall after the Point boundary and must not define this dossier.<br>
**Major locations:** The central highlands and varied village sites; Shechem, Shiloh, Bethel, Gibeah, Jerusalem, Khirbet Qeiyafa, Hazor, Megiddo, Gezer, and the Jezreel region under disputed attribution and chronology. Samaria is a later ninth-century capital outside the reviewed slice.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Merneptah victory stela; regional surveys and excavated settlement, pottery, faunal, storage, water, and architectural evidence; short early alphabetic inscriptions whose language and date may be disputed; and the books of Samuel and Kings as later literary witnesses rather than contemporaneous court annals.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Iron I evidence chiefly exposes households, villages, local institutions, and regional networks. The timing, territory, administrative depth, and unity of early monarchy remain disputed; the securely attested northern Kingdom of Israel becomes much clearer in the ninth century.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Early Israelite religion developed within a wider southern Levantine religious environment. Household practice, local cult places, Yahwistic traditions, and later covenantal and royal theology cannot be reduced to one uniform system or projected backward from later monotheism.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Settlement organization, agricultural and storage practice, craft, oral memory, and sparse alphabetic writing are visible unevenly. Later biblical composition and redaction preserve cultural memory but do not provide a transparent tenth-century archive.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Stone and mud-brick houses, storage installations, cisterns and local water systems, terracing where securely dated, pottery, iron and bronze tools, textile production, agricultural processing, fortifications at selected sites, and short inscriptions supported varied communities.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Pottery, architecture, seals, figurines, cult objects, inscriptions, and later literary traditions provide evidence. No single visual package securely identifies every Israelite community, and debated iconoclasm or aniconism is not a Stonebend style rule.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Settlement competition, Philistine and other regional conflict, raiding, fortified sites, and later royal war narratives belong to the evidence. Biblical battle accounts are literary sources, and conquest or territorial claim is not a Gift.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Households and settlements exchanged grain, oil, wine, livestock, ceramics, metals, timber, and craft goods through highland, valley, coastal, Transjordanian, and copper routes; political control of those routes changed and remains debated.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Early Israelite communities formed within Canaanite continuities and interacted with Philistine, Phoenician/coastal, Aramaean, Transjordanian, Egyptian, and Assyrian worlds. Co-placement in the Atlas does not prove a four-party system or validate later national boundaries.<br>

## Sandmanor — Phoenicia

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_01.sandmanor.phoenicia`<br>
**Primary culture:** Phoenicia<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full regional continuity / Emergent label<br>
**Date relevance:** coastal city continuity throughout c. 1200–900 BCE; Phoenician inscriptions and shared first-millennium identity clearest especially eleventh–tenth centuries BCE. Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Arwad, and neighboring centers were independent cities with distinct histories. 'Phoenicia' is a later external umbrella, and early western colonization or a unified maritime confederation is not projected across the whole Point.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for independent port-city identity and exchange, consonantal alphabetic inscription and transmission, cross-cultural craft synthesis; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Ahiram and Ithobaal of Byblos are known from a royal sarcophagus inscription whose date remains debated within the early first millennium. Hiram of Tyre is prominent in later biblical and classical traditions but lacks a comparable contemporary royal archive for the whole reported reign.<br>
**Major locations:** Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Arwad, Sarepta, Beirut, Dor, and connected Cypriot and Levantine ports, each with different continuity, destruction, harbor, craft, and inscriptional histories.<br>
**Major works or records:** Early Phoenician inscriptions from Byblos and other sites, including the Ahiram text with disputed dating; the Egyptian Tale of Wenamun as a literary witness; archaeology of ports, settlements, burials, workshops, pottery, metals, ivories, timber, and murex production; and later Assyrian, biblical, and classical sources kept at their own dates.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Independent city-kings, households, temples, merchants, craft groups, harbors, and local elites governed distinct communities. Relationships among the coastal cities varied; neither common language nor maritime exchange proves a single state.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Local cults included deities such as Baalat Gebal at Byblos, Melqart at Tyre in later evidence, Astarte, Eshmun, and other Levantine traditions. Divine names, civic identity, royal dedication, and funerary curses differed by city and period.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Northwest Semitic alphabetic traditions were adapted into recognizable Phoenician scripts and inscriptions. The Phoenicians did not invent alphabetic writing from nothing; the reviewed inheritance is economical inscription, local textual identity, and later transmission across language communities.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Shipbuilding and navigation, harbor use, woodworking, stone construction, metalwork, ivory carving, textile production, purple dye, pottery, weights, seals, and alphabetic inscription supported coastal life; exact evidence varies by city and century.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Craft combined local Levantine, Egyptian, Cypriot, Aegean, and Syrian forms. This synthesis reflects movement, patronage, adaptation, and workshop practice, not an anonymous decorative mixture available for historical cosplay.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Walls, ships, local competition, changing relations with Egypt, and later pressure from inland kingdoms shaped the coast. Naval dominance, western colonial systems, and mature Assyrian conflict are chiefly later and are not backdated across the Point.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Timber, wine, oil, textiles, purple dye, metals, ivory, glass, pottery, luxury craft, food, and people moved among the Levant, Cyprus, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Aegean. The scale and direction of exchange changed after 1200 and were not controlled by one city.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Coastal cities maintained and renegotiated ties with Egypt, Cyprus, surviving Syrian centers, emerging Israelite and Aramaean polities, and Assyria. Later Mediterranean settlements and Carthage belong after the reviewed Point.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Aram

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_01.glaushouse.aram`<br>
**Primary culture:** Aram<br>
**Temporal fit:** Emergent / Dispersed<br>
**Date relevance:** Aram and Aramaean designations increasingly visible from the late second millennium; city-state formation and Old Aramaic evidence cluster near or after the tenth–ninth-century boundary. Aram is not treated as one state or uniform ethnicity. Assyrian labels, mobile communities, locally distinct Syrian polities, and the later development of written Aramaic remain separate evidence layers.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-center city-state formation, regional cultural exchange with local variation, early Aramaic language and inscriptional continuity; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Tiglath-pileser I is an Assyrian adversarial witness to Ahlamu-Aramaean groups, not an Aramaean ruler. Named local dynasts and the best Old Aramaic royal inscriptions cluster near or after the Point boundary; no single figure securely represents the entire range.<br>
**Major locations:** The Khabur, Balikh, and Middle Euphrates regions; Tell Halaf/Guzana, Tell Fekheriye/Sikani, Bit Adini, Bit Bahiani, Hama, Damascus, Carchemish, and Sam'al as differently dated or culturally mixed contexts rather than one Aram territory.<br>
**Major works or records:** Middle and early Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions using external labels; settlement and material evidence from Syrian and upper Mesopotamian sites; sparse tenth- and ninth-century Old Aramaic inscriptions; and later biblical, Assyrian, and local texts whose dates and political agendas remain attached.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Mobile communities, households, towns, city-states, dynastic houses, and mixed Luwian-Aramaean or Syrian polities developed through local histories. House names such as Bit Bahiani identify particular political formations and do not prove a single ethnic constitution.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Hadad and other regional deities, local cults, dynastic monuments, and inherited Syrian traditions are visible more clearly in later first-millennium evidence. The reviewed Point cannot supply a uniform Aramaean theology.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Spoken Aramaic and related Northwest Semitic practices preceded a sparse early inscriptional record. Old Aramaic writing and its later administrative spread develop chiefly at the Point boundary and afterward, so the later lingua-franca role is future inheritance rather than a Point I condition.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Local agriculture, pastoral mobility, river and overland movement, fortifications, urban rebuilding, pottery, metalwork, stone carving, seals, and alphabetic inscription appear unevenly across sites. None defines an exclusive Aramaean material package.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Monuments and crafts belong to a wider Syrian cultural koine with local, Luwian, Assyrian, Levantine, and older regional elements. Scholars cannot securely isolate one early Aramaean art style.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Assyrian sources describe conflict, pursuit, crossings, tribute, and settlement through imperial rhetoric. They preserve names and geography unevenly but are not neutral ethnography or an Aramaean strategic manual.<br>
**Resources and trade:** River, steppe, agricultural, pastoral, and urban networks moved animals, grain, textiles, metals, timber, craft, and people. The familiar image of Aramaeans as a universal caravan people or merchants belongs to broader and often later evidence.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Aramaean-labeled groups and locally distinct polities interacted with Assyria, Babylonia, Hittite-successor and Luwian centers, Phoenician cities, Israel and Judah, and other Syrian communities. Regional exchange did not erase local political identity.<br>

## Flynt — Neo-Assyria

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_01.flynt.neo_assyria`<br>
**Primary culture:** Neo-Assyria<br>
**Temporal fit:** Boundary / Eleven-year slice<br>
**Date relevance:** c. 911–900 BCE within Adad-nirari II's reign, 911–891 BCE. The conventional Neo-Assyrian boundary enters only at the Point's close. Ashur-dan II's recovery belongs to the late Middle/early Neo-Assyrian transition, while most familiar Neo-Assyrian capitals, reliefs, siege imagery, and imperial systems belong after 900.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for continuous annual dating sequence, route-sequenced operational annals, post-contraction infrastructure reactivation; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Ashur-dan II, 934–912 BCE, is a late Middle or early Neo-Assyrian transition ruler. Adad-nirari II, 911–891 BCE, is the only conventional Neo-Assyrian king in the reviewed slice. Tukulti-Ninurta II and Ashurnasirpal II belong after 900.<br>
**Major locations:** Assur, Nineveh, Arbela, the Tigris heartland, the Khabur and Tur Abdin approaches, Nairi routes, and the Babylonian frontier. Kalhu/Nimrud's role as Ashurnasirpal II's capital belongs after the Point.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal inscriptions of Ashur-dan II and Adad-nirari II, the Assyrian King List, eponym lists including annual names from 910 through 900, campaign sequences, building inscriptions, and archaeological evidence read against fragmentary preservation and royal propaganda.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kingship, Assur's temples and city institutions, annual eponyms, officials, army, governors, stores, and route systems continued from earlier Assyria. The mature provincial empire cannot be projected into the first eleven years.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** The god Ashur and other Assyrian deities framed royal authority and campaign claims. Royal ideology presents expansion as divinely authorized; the Atlas preserves that framing as adversarial evidence, never as constitutional Capacity.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Annual dating, king lists, scribal copying, campaign ordering, route knowledge, musters, tribute accounting, and building records supported institutional memory. Surviving annals are selective royal compositions rather than neutral after-action reports.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortified cities, roads and river crossings, stored provisions, chariots, cavalry in development, metal weapons, siege practices, canals, agricultural restoration, tablets, seals, and workshops supported recovery and renewed campaigning; later iconic siege machinery requires later evidence.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Stelae, inscribed building works, seals, and temple or palace repair belong to the boundary slice. The monumental relief programs of Ashurnasirpal II, Sargon II, and later rulers must not become its visual shorthand.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Adad-nirari II's annals describe repeated campaigns, conquest, destruction, hostages, tribute, and forced movement. Operational sequence can be studied, but royal success claims, coercion, deportation, and territorial recovery are cautions rather than Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Agricultural restoration, tribute, livestock, metals, timber, horses, supplies, and routes linked the Assyrian heartland with upper Mesopotamia, Anatolian approaches, Babylonia, and the Zagros. Royal texts often blur extraction, tribute, and exchange.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Early Neo-Assyrian campaigns confronted Aramaean-labeled groups and highland polities and renewed pressure on Babylonia. The Point ends before the larger ninth-century encounters with Phoenician cities, Israel, and mature Syrian states.<br>
