Status: Working<br>

Point number: 6<br>
Point title: Alexander and the Diadochi<br>
Date range: 336–281 BCE<br>
Point type: Transition<br>
Boundary rationale: The supplied 336–281 BCE range is retained. Alexander's accession opens the coordinate, but his Asian invasion begins in 334, Darius III dies and Achaemenid kingship ends in 330, Alexander dies in 323, Ptolemy receives Egypt in 323 and assumes kingship in 305/304, Seleucus's durable return to Babylonia dates to 312/311 and his kingship to 305/304, Ipsus repartitions power in 301, and Corupedium ends the Lysimachus–Seleucus contest in 281. The Point is therefore a sequence of formation thresholds rather than one stable Diadochi settlement.

## Historical summary

This transition coordinate begins with two still-living monarchies, not four successor states. Darius III and Achaemenid resistance remain political realities until 330; Alexander's Macedonian-led empire then continues to 323; rival regencies, kings, coalitions, and partitions reshape the inheritance; Ptolemaic and Seleucid states emerge only inside those struggles. The 281 endpoint closes the first successor-generation formation arc without implying that war, border change, or institutional negotiation ended.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Alexander and the Diadochi** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [II:5 — Achaemenid World](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_05_achaemenid_world.md) at Alexander III's accession in 336. Achaemenid government and Darius III remain political realities until 330, Macedonia enters a conquest and successor phase, Egypt remains under Achaemenid rule until 332, and Ptolemaic and Seleucid assignments emerge only later within this coordinate.

## Transition into next Point

Corupedium in 281 and the deaths of Lysimachus and Seleucus I close this first successor-generation formation arc before [II:7 — Mature Hellenistic World](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_07_mature_hellenistic_world.md), but they do not produce an instantaneous stable system. Ptolemaic and Seleucid states continue under dynastic succession; Greek cities and leagues retain distinct political lives; and Antigonid rule in Macedonia becomes durable only after further conflict. Point VII is therefore a mature-system comparison, not a claim that successor war ended everywhere in 281.

## Supplied transition note

**Major transition:** Alexander conquers the Achaemenid Empire; his successors divide and stabilize the inheritance.

## Stonebend — Achaemenid

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_06.stonebend.achaemenid`<br>
**Primary culture:** Achaemenid<br>
**Temporal fit:** Political 336–330 / Institutional legacy thereafter<br>
**Date relevance:** Darius III and Achaemenid polity, 336–330 BCE; administrative, local, documentary, and infrastructural continuities through 281. Achaemenid is a polity only during the coordinate's first six years. Alexander and the successors retained, reassigned, or adapted personnel and regional structures unevenly; institutional continuity must be recorded as a changed-sovereignty handoff, not continuous Achaemenid rule or automatic legitimacy.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for terminal polity chronology with surviving institutions, jurisdiction handoff across conquest, administrative continuity with changed sovereign attribution; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Darius III, Stateira, Sisygambis, Memnon of Rhodes, Mazaeus, Ariobarzanes, Bessus/Artaxerxes V, Spitamenes, Alexander III, Parmenion, and regional officials and communities belong to different evidence traditions. Later Greek and Roman biographies dominate the narrative; Babylonian documents and Iranian evidence provide essential independent controls.<br>
**Major locations:** The Hellespont and Granicus, Issus, Tyre, Gaza, Egypt, Gaugamela, Babylon, Susa, the Persian Gates, Persepolis, Pasargadae, Ecbatana, Bactria, Sogdiana, and inherited regional routes. Conquest of a capital, death of a king, loss of a region, and end of resistance occur at different times.<br>
**Major works or records:** Babylonian astronomical diaries and king lists, late Achaemenid administrative reconstruction, coins, archaeology, later Arrian, Diodorus, Curtius, Plutarch, and Justin, and modern source-critical studies. No Persian royal inscription narrates Darius III's reign, making the Greek narrative imbalance a primary research fact.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Darius III's court, regional governors, commanders, garrisons, local dynasts, temples, cities, scribes, and fiscal systems continued unevenly under war. Alexander retained or replaced regional personnel and adapted satrapal structures, but changed sovereign, military oversight, extraction, and court authority; successor allocations later reassigned these jurisdictions again.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Achaemenid royal legitimacy, Ahuramazda-centered earlier inscriptional traditions, local cults, and Alexander's shifting claims to vengeance and succession intersect. Later narratives of fusion, orientalization, or legitimate succession are interpretations, not transparent evidence of community consent.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Multilingual archives, route knowledge, local law, taxation, accounting, astronomy, medicine, surveying, and administrative expertise survived unevenly because people and institutions continued. Knowledge continuity must identify custodian, office, language, changed authority, and loss.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Roads, relays, canals, treasuries, fortifications, ships, siege systems, tablets, papyri, seals, weights, coin, and regional infrastructure were captured, damaged, maintained, or reassigned. Reuse is not proof of unchanged administration or benign conquest.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Late Achaemenid palaces, reliefs, seals, coinage, court forms, and local material traditions persisted, were destroyed, appropriated, imitated, or recombined. Persepolis must remain a documented destruction and contested symbolic act, not a design motif stripped of violence.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Granicus, Issus, Tyre, Gaza, Gaugamela, the Persian Gates, Persepolis, Darius's pursuit and death, Bessus's claim, and Bactrian-Sogdian resistance expose multiple terminal thresholds. Victory, execution, burning, siege, massacre, enslavement, extraction, and forced service are harms, never Stonebend Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Imperial routes, royal stores, treasure, taxes, local markets, temple economies, ports, river systems, and military requisition continued under changing control. Plunder, appropriation, requisition, ration, tax, gift, pay, and voluntary exchange require separate provenance.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Darius's government and regional communities fought, negotiated with, defected to, resisted, or served Alexander's coalition under unequal conditions. Later successor states inherited portions of the territorial and institutional framework while local communities continued beyond every royal transition.<br>

## Sandmanor — Macedonia

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_06.sandmanor.macedonia`<br>
**Primary culture:** Macedonia<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full horizon / Referent transforms<br>
**Date relevance:** Alexander's Macedonian kingship and conquest, 336–323; joint kingship and regency claims, 323–310/305; competing Macedonian successor kings and courts through 281. `Macedonia` changes from kingdom and conquering court to an empire claimed in the names of Philip III and Alexander IV and then to rival successor monarchies. The inheritance concerns translated identity, contested succession, and coalition reconfiguration—not one continuous Macedonian state or a celebration of conquest.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-audience royal identity translation, contested succession and coalition record, foundation and renaming provenance across local communities; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Alexander III, Olympias, Philip III Arrhidaeus, Eurydice, Roxane, Alexander IV, Antipater, Perdiccas, Craterus, Eumenes, Polyperchon, Cassander, Antigonus I, Demetrius I, Lysimachus, Ptolemy I, Seleucus I, and many envoys, interpreters, soldiers, settlers, local rulers, civic communities, and resisting populations belong to changing coalitions.<br>
**Major locations:** Macedonia, Aigai, Pella, Greek cities, Asia Minor, the Levant, Egypt and Alexandria, Mesopotamia and Babylon, Iran, Bactria and Sogdiana, the Indus region, Triparadeisus, Gaza, Ipsus, and Corupedium. A named Alexandria or successor capital has a prior landscape and local community history.<br>
**Major works or records:** Babylonian chronicles and diaries, inscriptions, decrees, coins, papyri, archaeology, later Arrian, Diodorus, Plutarch, Curtius, Justin, and fragments of lost historians. Royal self-presentation, civic decree, adversarial narrative, and retrospective biography must remain separate.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Macedonian kingship, army assembly traditions, court and companions, satrapies, regencies, nominal joint kings, partition settlements, local rulers, cities, leagues, garrisons, and later independent royal courts distributed authority. The succession was not a clean legal transfer, and a conference allocation did not equal local consent or lasting control.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Greek and Macedonian cults, Egyptian pharaonic presentation, Babylonian temple recognition, Iranian royal forms, dynastic cult, heroic ancestry, and local ritual relationships served different audiences. Adopting a local title or ceremony was political translation, not proof of cultural synthesis or popular acceptance.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Campaign geography, multilingual interpretation, local expertise, surveying, administration, ethnography, medicine, natural inquiry, court history, and city records moved through conquest and patronage. The sources privilege rulers and literate elites and often silence coerced intermediaries.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Macedonian field and siege systems, ships, ports, roads, bridges, camps, mints, royal treasuries, new or renamed settlements, and inherited regional infrastructure supported conquest and successor war. Every capacity requires a dated phase, supply chain, labor status, and civilian-cost record.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Portraits, coins, royal imagery, court objects, funerary monuments, city plans, theaters, and local artistic traditions created several audience-specific identities. Alexander imagery was repeatedly reused by successors; reception and appropriation are part of provenance.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Alexander's campaigns and the successor wars depended on siege, battle, garrison, forced movement, settlement, plunder, recruitment, and coalition reversal. The Lamian War, regency conflicts, Gaza, Ipsus, and Corupedium mark relationship change but cannot become Sandmanor spectacle or virtue.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Treasure, taxes, grain, timber, horses, metals, coin, military pay, ships, animals, luxury goods, texts, and people moved through captured and reconfigured networks. Trade, tribute, royal gift, military requisition, confiscation, plunder, enslavement, and settlement must remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Macedonian courts and armies interacted with Achaemenid officials, Greek cities, Egyptians, Babylonians, Iranians, Central and South Asian communities, Phoenicians, Cypriots, Thracians, and many local authorities through translation, negotiation, marriage, settlement, coercion, revolt, alliance, and war. `Fusion` is not an adequate account of these unequal relationships.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Ptolemaic

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_06.glaushouse.ptolemaic`<br>
**Primary culture:** Ptolemaic<br>
**Temporal fit:** Egyptian precursor 332–323 / Satrapy 323–305 / Kingdom 305–281<br>
**Date relevance:** Alexander and Macedonian administration in Egypt from 332; Ptolemy as satrap from 323; Ptolemy I as king from 305/304; early Ptolemy II phase begins 285/282. Ptolemaic does not exist at the 336 opening. Early rule combined new court and administrative languages with Egyptian institutions, temples, scribes, settlements, and archives; Alexandria's Museum and Library are early Ptolemaic formations whose founder, date, access, and scope remain uncertain.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multilingual institutional handoff in Egypt, temple and local-service continuity under a new court, source-critical scholarly collection formation; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Alexander III, Cleomenes of Naucratis, Ptolemy I, Berenice I, Eurydice, Ptolemy II, Arsinoe II at the 281 boundary, Manetho, Demetrius of Phalerum, priests, Demotic and Greek scribes, local officials, soldiers, settlers, craftspeople, farmers, sailors, scholars, and subjects illuminate different institutions.<br>
**Major locations:** Alexandria and its sea and river harbors, Memphis, Saqqara, Buto, Naukratis, the Nile valley and Delta, Elephantine, the Fayum, Cyrenaica, Cyprus, Coele-Syria, and Aegean possessions. Alexandria's centrality did not erase older Egyptian centers or regional life.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Satrap Stela of 311/310, early Greek and Demotic papyri, hieroglyphic and temple inscriptions, coins, papyrus orders, archaeology, later literary accounts, and the uncertain institutional traditions of the Museum and Library. Later third- and second-century evidence cannot automatically document Ptolemy I's administration.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Ptolemy first governed as satrap for Alexander's heirs and later as king, working through a Macedonian-Greek court and military, regional officials, Egyptian temples, scribes, local authorities, cities, and fiscal systems. Royal presentation in Egyptian forms and confirmation of temple rights were negotiated political actions, not evidence of unchanged government or universal acceptance.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Egyptian temple cults, Macedonian and Greek practices, royal representation, Alexander's memory, and emerging dynastic forms coexisted. The later development of Sarapis and ruler cult must be dated carefully; religion cannot be reduced to an engineered blend.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Greek became a principal administrative language while Demotic and other Egyptian scripts and knowledge traditions continued. Court-supported scholarship, book collection, editing, medicine, geography, and translation developed around Alexandria, but foundation stories and later library myths require explicit uncertainty.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Nile and maritime transport, harbors, canals, irrigation, papyrus, writing systems, coinage, mints, temples, settlements, fortifications, ships, storage, and craft supported both local service and royal power. Maintenance, local expertise, taxation, labor status, and military use remain visible.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Egyptian, Greek, Macedonian, and recombined forms appear in sculpture, coinage, temples, funerary material, faience, architecture, and royal representation. Parallel styles and selective combinations must retain maker, patron, material, place, audience, and date.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Ptolemy secured Egypt, fought Perdiccas, contested Cyrenaica, Cyprus, Coele-Syria, and the Aegean, and participated in successor coalitions. Defense, naval reach, garrisons, seizure, and military settlement imposed costs; none becomes a gift of Integrity.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Nile agriculture, grain, papyrus, textiles, oil, wine, metals, timber, coin, taxes, port traffic, military supply, and eastern Mediterranean exchange linked court, temple, household, market, and army. Later administrative detail must not be backdated without evidence.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The early Ptolemaic state negotiated with and ruled Egyptian institutions and communities while competing with Perdiccas, Antigonus, Demetrius, Seleucus, Cassander, and Lysimachus and interacting with Cyrenaican, Cypriot, Levantine, Aegean, and Nubian networks. Local collaboration, continuity, resistance, and coercion varied by place.<br>

## Flynt — Seleucid

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_06.flynt.seleucid`<br>
**Primary culture:** Seleucid<br>
**Temporal fit:** Precursor 323–315 / Formation 312–305 / Kingdom 305–281<br>
**Date relevance:** Seleucus receives Babylonia in 321, loses it in 315, returns in 312/311; Seleucid Era and durable consolidation from 312/311; royal title from 305/304; Seleucus I dies in 281. Seleucid is not present in 336 and is not securely continuous from Seleucus's first satrapal appointment. Its relevant capacity grows through reassembly of Babylonian and Achaemenid routes, local institutions, settlement and coin networks, military recruitment, and changing regional command—under conquest, extraction, and recurrent war that remain harms, not Gifts.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for Babylon-centered imperial reassembly logistics, multi-region route and supply coordination, distributed command with phase and coercion audit; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Seleucus I, Apama, Antiochus I, Patrocles, Peucestas, Antigonus I, Demetrius I, Ptolemy I, Lysimachus, Cassander, Chandragupta Maurya, Babylonian temple officials, scribes, regional governors, settlers, soldiers, craftspeople, carriers, farmers, and local communities belong to distinct sources and operational roles.<br>
**Major locations:** Babylon, Seleucia-on-the-Tigris, Susa, Media, Persis, Bactria, Sogdiana, the Iranian plateau, Mesopotamian river and canal routes, northern Syria and the later Seleucis, Anatolia, Ipsus, and Corupedium. Territorial claims, actual control, route access, and local institutional continuity must be mapped separately.<br>
**Major works or records:** Babylonian chronicles, king lists and astronomical diaries, cuneiform economic and temple documents, coins, inscriptions, archaeological settlement evidence, later Diodorus, Appian, Plutarch, and Justin, and modern regional studies. Successor narratives are uneven and often court-centered; absence of documentation is not proof of administrative absence.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Seleucid kingship developed from satrapal command and successor conflict, using regional governors, royal officials, cities, temples, local elites, garrisons, fiscal systems, and eventually dynastic co-rule. Institutional continuity from Achaemenid and Babylonian practice was selective; later mature structures cannot all be assigned to 312–281.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Babylonian temple recognition and chronological practice, Greek-Macedonian kingship, Iranian traditions, city cults, dynastic representation, and local religions coexisted. Royal accommodation, title, coin image, and cult practice served different audiences and do not prove common belief or consent.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Babylonian scholarly and administrative traditions, Greek documentary practice, regional languages, route intelligence, geography, astronomy, finance, and military engineering supported the state. Custody and language remain local even when rulers coordinate their outputs.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Road and river movement, canals, ports, camps, siege systems, elephants, horses, weapons, settlements, mints, coin payments, tablets, seals, and urban foundations connected regions. Capacity depended on extraction, labor, recruitment, local institutions, and maintenance rather than royal command alone.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Coins, seals, inscriptions, urban foundations, royal images, Babylonian and local material traditions, and Greek-Macedonian forms expressed several audiences. `Hellenistic-Iranian synthesis` is too broad without maker, region, date, function, and power relationship.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** The Babylonian War, eastern campaigns, arrangements with Chandragupta, Ipsus, later conflict with Demetrius and Lysimachus, and Corupedium show state formation through recurrent war. Conquest, conscription, military settlement, plunder, elephant acquisition, and coercive extraction are evidence and harms, not Capacity Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Babylonia connected overland and river routes among the Gulf, Iran, Central and South Asia, Syria, Anatolia, and the Mediterranean. Agriculture, coin, taxes, military pay, animals, metals, textiles, grain, and information moved through royal, temple, city, household, and market channels under different conditions.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Seleucus's power developed through alliance and conflict with other successors and through negotiation, war, and exchange with Babylonian, Iranian, Central Asian, South Asian, Syrian, Anatolian, and Greek-Macedonian communities. The 281 endpoint enlarges Seleucus's claim but his death immediately exposes continued instability.<br>
