Boundary rationale: The supplied 281–146 BCE range is retained as a long phased coordinate. Antigonus II's durable Macedonian rule begins only in 277/276; the Antigonid monarchy ends at Pydna in 168 and Macedonia then passes through Roman reorganization and renewed war before 146. ## Historical summary

Antigonid Macedonia, politically varied Greek cities and federal states, Ptolemaic Egypt, and the Seleucid kingdoms formed a connected but repeatedly reconfigured Hellenistic system. The coordinate includes dynastic succession, civic and federal vitality, Syrian Wars, regional revolts, Roman intervention, territorial contraction, and institutional continuity. Rome's destruction of Corinth in 146 is decisive for mainland Greek and Mediterranean power, but neither ends Ptolemaic nor Seleucid rule and cannot serve as a universal four-assignment terminal event.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Mature Hellenistic World** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

The Atlas moves from [II:6 — Alexander and the Diadochi](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_06_alexander_and_the_diadochi.md) at the 281 successor-generation boundary, not into an already static three-kingdom balance. **Date relevance:** Greek poleis, koina, sanctuaries, festivals, schools, and inter-city networks throughout 281–146 BCE and beyond. `Hellenistic Greek` is a bounded umbrella for diverse mainland, Aegean, western Anatolian, and connected civic worlds, not one people, state, style, or culture. **Government and institutions:** Poleis retained assemblies, councils, magistrates, courts, laws, and civic finances in different forms; koina coordinated membership, assemblies, officials, defense, currency, sanctuaries, and diplomacy with different distributions of representation and power. **Religion and worldview:** Local and pan-Hellenic cults, ruler honors, mystery cults, household religion, philosophical ethics, civic festivals, and new associations coexisted. **Science and knowledge:** Philosophical schools, gymnasia, libraries, teachers, physicians, mathematicians, historians, poets, actors, and craftspeople circulated among cities and courts. **Technology and infrastructure:** Civic walls, harbors, roads, water systems, theaters, stoas, gymnasia, sanctuaries, mints, workshops, ships, siege equipment, clocks, instruments, and record media supported public and private life. Attribution must distinguish local works, royal benefaction, coerced labor, repair, and later Roman intervention.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Portraiture, sculpture, theater, poetry, mosaics, terracottas, coins, architecture, and locally distinct workshops travelled and changed through patronage and exchange. `Hellenistic style` is an analytical umbrella; maker, material, place, audience, date, and local reception remain attached.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Cities and leagues fought one another, kings, and Rome; mercenary service, garrisons, siege, piracy, enslavement, confiscation, and population movement were recurrent. Federal cooperation did not eliminate coercion, and the 146 destruction of Corinth must remain a human and civic rupture rather than a dramatic motif.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, wine, oil, metals, timber, ceramics, textiles, books, artworks, coin, credit, labor, enslaved people, and information moved through ports, markets, sanctuaries, households, associations, leagues, and royal systems. Shared currency or connectivity does not prove equal access or economic growth.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Greek cities and koina negotiated among themselves and with Antigonid, Ptolemaic, Seleucid, Attalid, Rhodian, Carthaginian, and Roman powers through decrees, envoys, treaties, honors, arbitration, patronage, alliance, neutrality, resistance, and war. `Freedom of the Greeks` was a contested political claim whose issuer and enforcement must always be named.<br>
