## Stonebend — Roman Republic

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_09.stonebend.roman_republic`<br>
**Primary culture:** Roman Republic<br>
**Temporal fit:** Working representative<br>
**Date relevance:** 146–27 BCE. Exact internal overlap and phase boundaries remain Working.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for late republican constitutional transition records, land, citizenship, and provincial obligation provenance, civil-war command and fiscal accountability records; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, Marius, Sulla, Pompey, Caesar, Cicero, Cleopatra VII, Mark Antony, and Octavian are central political actors; enslaved people, provincials, soldiers, women, allies, and displaced communities require separate evidence.<br>
**Major locations:** Rome, Italy, Sicily, Spain, Gaul, Greece, Asia Minor, Syria, Egypt, North Africa, provincial capitals, colonies, roads, ports, and civil-war battle landscapes.<br>
**Major works or records:** Senatorial and popular-law traditions, inscriptions, coinage, land surveys, military diplomas and roads, Cicero, Caesar, Sallust, Plutarch, Appian, and archaeology; much narrative is later, elite, and partisan.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Magistracies, Senate, assemblies, tribunes, provincial commands, courts, tax farming, colonies, and patronage continued while emergency commands and personal armies altered the republican balance. The 27 BCE transition is institutional transformation, not a clean constitutional replacement.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Mos maiorum, civic religion, triumph, household cult, philosophical schools, ruler honors, eastern religious practice, and competing claims of liberty and order shaped public memory.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Law, rhetoric, surveying, engineering, medicine, astronomy, geography, finance, military logistics, and historical writing circulated through Greek and Roman networks; knowledge was unevenly accessible and politically situated.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Roads, bridges, aqueducts, ports, siege works, concrete, surveying, coinage, ships, archives, and military camps linked civic and provincial administration, with labor and extraction made explicit.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Portraiture, coinage, triumphal display, temples, villas, funerary monuments, literature, and imported Greek forms negotiated ancestry, authority, grief, and political legitimacy.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** The Jugurthine and Social Wars, Sulla's march on Rome, Mithridatic campaigns, Caesar's civil war, the Alexandrian and Perusine wars, and Actium expose mobilization, atrocity, displacement, and command accountability; victory is not a Gift.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, slaves, tax, land, metals, wine, oil, credit, coin, luxury goods, and information moved across an increasingly integrated but coercive Mediterranean economy.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Rome interacted with Italian communities, Greek cities, Pontus, Parthia, Ptolemaic Egypt, Numidia, Judaea, Gaul, Spain, and North Africa through diplomacy, patronage, war, annexation, and negotiated local authority.<br>
