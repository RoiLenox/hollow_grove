# Age II / Point 2 — Kings, Prophets, and Assyrian Expansion

Status: Working<br>
Atlas ID: `atlas.age_02.point_02`<br>
Age: Classical Civilization<br>
Point number: 2<br>
Point title: Kings, Prophets, and Assyrian Expansion<br>
Date range: c. 900–722 BCE<br>
Point type: Sequential<br>
Boundary rationale: The supplied c. 900–722 BCE range is retained as a sequential Levantine–Assyrian interaction window. It begins when the Point I labels become better-attested kingdoms, city-states, and inscriptional communities. It ends with the supplied fall of Samaria, while Phoenician cities and Assyria continue and Aramaean polities are annexed unevenly. The exact attribution and sequence of Samaria's conquest and reorganization remain debated across Shalmaneser V, Sargon II, biblical, chronicle, and royal-inscription evidence.

## Historical summary

The northern Kingdom of Israel, independent Phoenician port-cities, locally distinct Aramaean polities, and the expanding Neo-Assyrian state are all well attested within this coordinate, but they do not share one political form or terminal date. Royal inscriptions, local inscriptions, archaeology, administrative records, and later biblical literature expose competing perspectives on state formation, alliance, tribute, resistance, annexation, and memory.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Kings, Prophets, and Assyrian Expansion** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [II:1 — Early Iron Age](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_01_early_iron_age.md) by changing evidence scale rather than replacing four peoples. Israel becomes the better-attested northern kingdom centered at Samaria; Phoenician ports retain separate identities while their settlement networks grow; locally distinct Aramaean polities and inscriptions become more visible; and Neo-Assyria enters a long expansion marked by internal interruption as well as conquest.

## Transition into next Point

The supplied fall of Samaria closes the Israel assignment before [II:3 — Assyrian Apex](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_03_assyrian_apex.md), though the conquest, deportation, provincial reorganization, and Sargon II succession evidence crosses the 722/721 boundary. Phoenician cities, Aramaic language communities, and Assyria continue on unequal trajectories; Point III changes all four House assignments and must not imply that the underlying populations or networks disappeared.

## Supplied transition note

**Terminal event:** Assyrian conquest of Samaria and the fall of the northern Kingdom of Israel.

## Stonebend — Israel

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_02.stonebend.israel`<br>
**Primary culture:** Israel<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Terminal<br>
**Date relevance:** northern Kingdom of Israel c. 900–722 BCE; major territorial reduction under Tiglath-pileser III in 732 BCE. The supplied `Israel` label refers primarily to the northern kingdom centered at Samaria, not the wider people-name, a united monarchy, or Judah. Omride through final-dynasty institutions are increasingly visible, but construction at Megiddo and related sites remains chronologically and dynastically contested.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-source state chronology, administrative collection records, attributed urban and water infrastructure; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Omri, Ahab, Jehu, Joash, Jeroboam II, Menahem, Pekah, and Hoshea belong to the northern royal sequence. Jezebel, Elijah, Elisha, Amos, and Hosea are important in later literary memory whose composition history remains attached. Mesha of Moab, Hazael and Rezin of Damascus, and successive Assyrian kings are independent or adversarial witnesses.<br>
**Major locations:** Samaria, Jezreel, Megiddo, Hazor, Dan, Bethel, Tirzah, Dothan, Dor, the Jezreel Valley, and the Samaria highlands; Kuntillet Ajrud supplies connected but geographically external inscriptional evidence. Building phases and royal attribution at Megiddo, Hazor, and Jezreel remain debated.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Mesha Stele, Tel Dan fragments, Kurkh Monolith, Black Obelisk, Assyrian annals and summary inscriptions, Samaria ostraca, seals, ivories, architecture, water systems, ceramics, faunal and botanical remains, Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, and the books of Kings, Amos, and Hosea read at their dates and genres.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Dynastic kingship operated from Samaria through royal households, regional centers, officials, scribes, military forces, land and produce collection, storage, building, and cult institutions. The Samaria ostraca expose deliveries and named places or persons but do not by themselves reconstruct one complete tax system.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Yahwistic worship coexisted with regional cult centers, royal religious policy, household practice, Baal and Asherah traditions, and prophetic criticism. Bethel and Dan are prominent in later texts; no uniform monotheism, one prophetic office, or transparent account of popular practice is projected across the kingdom.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Alphabetic writing, administrative notation, seals and weights, agricultural scheduling, building and water knowledge, oral and written royal memory, and prophetic literature appear in different archives. Later editorial preservation is distinguished from contemporaneous administration.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Ashlar and mud-brick building, fortifications, gates, storage installations, concealed water systems, terracing, agricultural processing, pottery, metal tools and weapons, ivory working, textiles, chariots, horses, roads, and coastal access supported an unequal state and its communities.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Samaria ivories, seals, fine wares, architecture, cult objects, inscriptions, and imported or Phoenician-made goods reveal elite patronage and regional exchange. They do not define one national style or authorize luxury and royal display as Stonebend values.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Ahab joined the Qarqar coalition in 853; conflict and alliance shifted among Israel, Damascus, Moab, Judah, and Assyria. Jehu paid tribute in 841, Tiglath-pileser III annexed northern territory in 732, and Shalmaneser V and Sargon II sources frame the terminal conquest and reorganization differently. War, deportation, and territorial claim are cautions, not Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, oil, wine, livestock, horses, timber, metals, ivory, ceramics, textiles, and luxury goods moved among highland, valley, coastal, Phoenician, Transjordanian, Aramaean, Egyptian, and Assyrian networks. Tribute, tax, booty, market exchange, and household production remain distinct categories.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Israel alternately allied with and fought Damascus, interacted with Phoenician cities and Judah, contested Moab and other neighbors, and moved from coalition partner to tributary, reduced vassal, and conquered territory under Assyrian pressure. The Atlas terminal label does not settle whether Shalmaneser V, Sargon II, or successive actions should receive sole credit for Samaria's fall.<br>

## Sandmanor — Phoenicia

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_02.sandmanor.phoenicia`<br>
**Primary culture:** Phoenicia<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** independent Phoenician city-states throughout c. 900–722 BCE and beyond. Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad, and other cities pursued distinct interests while sharing language and connected craft and maritime networks. Assyrian pressure ranged from tribute and negotiated advantage to intervention; western settlements developed unevenly and did not create one Phoenician state.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for city-specific diplomacy under asymmetric power, networked settlement and port relationships, alphabetic and craft transmission with named intermediaries; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Tyrian rulers such as Ithobaal I, Mattan I, Pygmalion, and Elissa/Dido belong partly to later transmitted king lists and foundation traditions whose chronology and historicity require review. Assyrian sources more directly name tribute-bearing or vassal rulers, including Baal-Eser and Hiram of Tyre; rulers must be attached to a city, date, and source rather than treated as Phoenician sovereigns.<br>
**Major locations:** Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad, Sarepta, Beirut, Kition and other Cypriot centers, and developing settlements in North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, and Iberia. Carthage's traditional 814/813 foundation date and its archaeological emergence are related but not identical evidence.<br>
**Major works or records:** City-specific Phoenician inscriptions, foundation deposits, tomb and votive texts, the Nora and early Cypriot inscriptions under continuing date review, Assyrian annals, tribute scenes and correspondence, the Balawat Gates, archaeology of harbors, workshops, sanctuaries, burials and settlements, and later biblical, Greek, and Roman accounts kept at their own dates.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Each city maintained its own king, council or elite bodies where evidenced, temples, households, merchants, workshops, harbor authorities, dependent settlements, and diplomatic relationships. Similar language, craft, and seafaring do not establish a federal constitution.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Local cults centered different forms and combinations of Melqart, Astarte, Eshmun, Baalat Gebal, Baal, and other Levantine deities. Votive, funerary, royal, and later narrative evidence differs by city and cannot become one Phoenician theology or Sandmanor ritual.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Alphabetic scribal practice, multilingual encounter, navigation, seasonal and route knowledge, accounting, weights, craft recipes, timber selection, ship knowledge, and object biography traveled through people and institutions. The spread of script is treated as adaptation and transmission, not sole invention.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Seagoing ships, harbors and anchorages, woodworking, cedar construction, murex dye production, textiles, metal and ivory work, glass, pottery, stone building, weights, seals, and alphabetic inscription supported city-specific economies and wider networks.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Ivories, metal bowls, textiles, jewelry, sculpture, seals, architecture, ceramics, and funerary or votive objects combined local and mobile forms through named or recoverable materials, workshops, patrons, routes, and audiences. The Atlas preserves those mediations rather than inventing a generic Phoenician style.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Fortified island and coastal cities, ships, tributary diplomacy, local rivalries, Assyrian campaigns, and resistance shaped the period. Submission scenes and royal annals are imperial representations; western settlement and Assyrian alliance are not romanticized as peaceful commerce or civilizing expansion.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Timber, purple-dyed textiles, wine, oil, metals including Cypriot copper, ivory, glass, pottery, crafted luxuries, food, people, and information moved through maritime and inland routes. Assyrian tribute, taxation, monopolies, protection, market access, and direct exchange must be separated.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The cities competed and cooperated with one another; interacted with Cyprus, Israel, Aramaean and Anatolian polities, Egypt, Assyria, and Greek-speaking communities; and established settlements through locally varied relationships. Tyre's advantages under Assyrian power did not represent every city or make asymmetric treaties consensual partnership.<br>

## Glaüshouse — Aram

**House:** [Glaüshouse](../../houses/GLAUSHOUSE_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_02.glaushouse.aram`<br>
**Primary culture:** Aram<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full regional horizon / Uneven terminal<br>
**Date relevance:** multiple Aramaean and mixed Syrian polities c. 900–722 BCE; Arpad annexed 740, Aram-Damascus 732, Hamath's final annexation after revolt in 720. `Aram` remains an umbrella for locally distinct polities, languages, and material traditions. Old Aramaic inscriptions become substantially clearer, but Aram-Damascus, Hamath, Bit Agusi/Arpad, Sam'al, and Guzana must not be merged into one state or one uniform ethnicity.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for local-polity inscription and bilingual witness, distributed treaty and boundary memory, language continuity across annexation and displacement; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Hadadezer/Adad-idri of Damascus and Irhuleni of Hamath led coalition forces at Qarqar; Hazael and Bar-Hadad continued Damascus's power; Rezin was defeated in 732. Zakkur of Hamath/Luash, Kilamuwa and Panamuwa at Sam'al, Mati'el of Arpad, and Had-yith'i at Guzana belong to different local records and cannot represent all Aramaeans.<br>
**Major locations:** Damascus, Hamath, Arpad, Sam'al/Zincirli, Guzana/Tell Halaf, Sikkan/Tell Fekheriye, Til Barsip, Aleppo, the Khabur and Middle Euphrates, and neighboring Luwian or Hittite-successor centers whose identities and languages overlap unevenly.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Tell Fekheriye bilingual inscription, Tel Dan fragments, Zakkur Stele, Sefire treaty stelae, Sam'al royal inscriptions, local sculpture and architecture, Assyrian royal inscriptions and reliefs, archaeology, and later biblical texts. Date, find context, language version, genre, speaker, damage, and reconstruction remain explicit.<br>
**Government and institutions:** City-kings, dynastic houses, councils or elders where attested, temples, officials, military coalitions, local governors, and treaty relationships varied by polity. Assyrian annexation replaced some kingdoms with provinces at different times; it did not end Aramaic-speaking communities or all local institutions at once.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Hadad and other local deities, ancestral commemoration, royal dedication, treaty oaths, curses, funerary practice, and older Syrian traditions appear in distinct corpora. Bilingual or shared divine language does not prove uniform belief.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Old Aramaic inscription, local scribal practice, bilingual Akkadian-Aramaic presentation, treaty formulation, royal and boundary memory, agricultural and route knowledge, and the preservation of names across external archives become substantially clearer. Later standardized or imperial Aramaic remains a subsequent development.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortified cities, citadels, orthostats and sculpture, roads and river routes, water and agricultural systems, metalwork, horse and chariot equipment, seals, stone stelae, alphabetic inscription, and bilingual monumental display developed in local and regional combinations.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Monumental sculpture, relief, stelae, inscriptions, architecture, ivories, metalwork, and seals participate in a northern Syrian cultural field with Aramaean, Luwian, Assyrian, Levantine, and older local strands. Polity and workshop precede umbrella ethnic labeling.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Coalitions resisted Assyrian campaigns at Qarqar and afterward; Damascus, Israel, Hamath, Arpad, and other states changed alliances and fought one another. Arpad fell after siege in 740 and Damascus in 732; Hamath's terminal revolt and annexation belong around 720, beyond the Point endpoint. Siege, tribute, massacre claims, and annexation remain cautions.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Agriculture, pastoral production, textiles, metals, timber, horses, craft, and people moved through Syrian river, steppe, mountain, and urban routes. Assyrian texts often record tribute or booty rather than ordinary exchange, while the idea of all Aramaeans as caravan merchants remains an unsupported simplification.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Local polities allied, competed, intermarried, exchanged, and fought with Assyria, Israel, Phoenician cities, Urartu, Anatolian states, Babylonia, and one another. Aramaic language persisted and spread through mobility, administration, and resettlement even as individual states ended, but no single mechanism explains that continuity.<br>

## Flynt — Assyria

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_02.flynt.assyria`<br>
**Primary culture:** Assyria<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Neo-Assyrian polity throughout c. 900–722 BCE, continuing into Point III. The supplied `Assyria` label is interpreted operationally as Neo-Assyria. The Point spans expansion, capital building, civil war and magnate power, plague and revolt, Tiglath-pileser III's annexations, and Shalmaneser V's terminal reign; it cannot be represented as uninterrupted administrative uniformity or celebrated conquest.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for eponym chronicle synchronization, distributed provincial and correspondence records, system-wide failure and coercion audit; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Adad-nirari II, Tukulti-Ninurta II, Ashurnasirpal II, Shalmaneser III, Shamshi-Adad V, Sammu-ramat, Adad-nirari III, the field marshal Shamshi-ilu, Shalmaneser IV, Ashur-dan III, Ashur-nirari V, Tiglath-pileser III, and Shalmaneser V expose changing distributions of royal and magnate power. Sargon II begins after the Point boundary but is essential to the disputed terminal reconstruction.<br>
**Major locations:** Assur, Nineveh, Arbela, Kalhu/Nimrud, Imgur-Enlil/Balawat, Til Barsip, the Habur and Euphrates corridors, annexed Syrian and Levantine provinces, Urartian and Zagros frontiers, Babylonia, and Samaria. Dur-Sharrukin belongs to Sargon II after the endpoint.<br>
**Major works or records:** RIA 4–5 and RINAP 1 royal inscriptions, eponym lists and chronicles, the Assyrian King List, the Black Obelisk and Balawat Gates, palace reliefs and standard inscriptions, building records, provincial and palace archives, letters, legal and administrative tablets, treaties, seals, archaeology, and the Babylonian Chronicle.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kingship worked through palaces, temples, governors, magnates, annual eponyms, military commanders, deputies, scribes, provincial officials, client rulers, and annexed territories. Civil war, long-lived magnates, and Tiglath-pileser III's accession and reorganization demonstrate that authority and implementation changed over time.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Ashur, Ishtar, Ninurta, Nabu, and other deities grounded royal and institutional claims. Palace images and inscriptions presented divinely sanctioned world rule; ritual and ideology are evidence to be analyzed, not Law of Capacity or moral authorization.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Eponym chronology, astronomical observation including the 763 BCE eclipse, route and campaign geography, correspondence, multilingual scribal practice, omen scholarship, accounting, archives, and building records supported administration while also preserving interruption, revolt, plague, and bias.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortified centers, roads and river crossings, relay and messenger systems, canals and agricultural works, storage, siege equipment, chariots and cavalry, iron and bronze weapons, large building platforms, stone reliefs, tablets, seals, weights, and workshops operated through varied labor regimes.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Kalhu palace reliefs, colossal guardians, painted carving, ivories, metalwork, seals, stelae, obelisks, gate bands, and repeated royal inscriptions joined architecture to controlled political narrative. They are not a Flynt decoration palette, and represented violence remains adversarial evidence.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Repeated campaigns, siege, vassalage, tribute, massacre rhetoric, deportation, resettlement, annexation, conscription, and extraction built and maintained imperial reach. Qarqar, internal revolt, western annexations, Damascus, and Samaria reveal limits and costs as well as operations. Coercion and conquest never become Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Agricultural produce, textiles, timber, metals, horses, livestock, crafted goods, tribute, taxes, booty, and labor moved through core, provincial, vassal, Phoenician, Anatolian, Zagros, Babylonian, and Levantine networks. Administrative movement must be classified by consent and legal status rather than called trade automatically.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Assyria fought, allied with, taxed, annexed, resettled, and exchanged with Aramaean and Phoenician cities, Israel and Judah, Urartu, Babylonia, Anatolian states, Medes, Arabs, and Egypt-linked networks. The fall of Samaria closes the supplied Point, not Assyrian expansion or the Neo-Assyrian period.<br>
