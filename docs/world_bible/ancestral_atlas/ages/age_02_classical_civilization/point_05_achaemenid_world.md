Boundary rationale: The supplied 539–336 BCE range is retained. Cyrus's capture of Babylon opens an Achaemenid-centered coordinate, although Saite Egypt remains independent until 525 and no single event in 539 creates the Greek or Macedonian assignments. The 336 endpoint marks Philip II's death and Alexander III's accession, not the Achaemenid Empire's fall: Darius III and the empire continue until Alexander's campaigns end Achaemenid kingship in 330. ## Historical summary

The Achaemenid Empire connected and contested a vast West Asian, Egyptian, eastern Mediterranean, and Central Asian world while Greek communities remained politically plural, Egypt passed through Saite, Persian, independent, and renewed Persian phases, and Macedonia became a decisive power only in the coordinate's final decades. This is a long interaction horizon with explicit phases, not a synchronous four-state tableau.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Achaemenid World** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

Cyrus's capture of Babylon closes [II:4 — Neo-Babylonian World](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_04_neo_babylonian_world.md) and opens this Achaemenid-centered coordinate without erasing Babylonian institutions, Median people, or distributed Judahite communities. Saite Egypt remains independent until 525; Greek communities and Macedonia do not begin in 539 and enter the comparison through their own longer histories.

## Transition into next Point

Philip II's death and Alexander III's accession in 336 open [II:6 — Alexander and the Diadochi](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_06_alexander_and_the_diadochi.md), but Achaemenid kingship continues under Darius III until 330 and Late Egyptian second Persian rule continues until 332. Macedonia changes from a late-dominant Flynt comparison into Point VI's conquest-centered Sandmanor assignment, while Ptolemaic and Seleucid states emerge only after Alexander's death; the handoff is therefore phased rather than instantaneous.

## Supplied transition note

**Major transition:** Philip II consolidates Macedonia; Alexander becomes king.

## Stonebend — Achaemenid

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_05.stonebend.achaemenid`<br>
**Primary culture:** Achaemenid<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Continues into Point VI<br>
**Date relevance:** Achaemenid rule from Babylon's capture in 539 through 336 BCE; empire and Darius III continue to 330. The label spans Cyrus II, Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I, later fifth-century kings, and the fourth-century sequence through Darius III's accession. Satrapal government, road and ration systems, local institutions, languages, archives, taxation, rebellion, and coercion varied by region and reign; plural administration is not modern pluralism or constitutional consent.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for delegated regional administration with local jurisdiction records, multilingual imperial document provenance, road relay and ration-supported coordination; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Cyrus II, Cambyses II, Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I, Darius II, Artaxerxes II, Artaxerxes III, Arses, and Darius III define the royal sequence. Atossa, Parysatis, Amestris, satraps, estate managers, scribes, workers, travelers, local rulers, temple personnel, and rebels expose institutions beyond the king. Croesus, Udjahorresnet, Themistocles, Cyrus the Younger, Tissaphernes, and Evagoras belong to specific relationships, not one imperial elite.<br>
**Major locations:** Pasargadae, Persepolis, Susa, Ecbatana, Babylon, Sardis, Memphis, Elephantine, Behistun, Naqsh-e Rostam, royal and regional roads, and centers across Anatolia, the Levant, Egypt, Mesopotamia, Iran, Central Asia, and the Indus frontier. A ceremonial capital, archive catchment, satrapal residence, temple city, and road station served different functions.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Cyrus Cylinder, Bisitun inscription and its versions, Achaemenid royal inscriptions, Persepolis Fortification and Treasury archives, Babylonian legal and temple archives, Aramaic documents including Elephantine papyri, Demotic and hieroglyphic Egyptian records, coinage, seals, archaeology, and Greek authors including Herodotus and Xenophon read according to genre, date, language, audience, and political interest.<br>
**Government and institutions:** The Great King, royal family and court, high officials, regional governors conventionally called satraps, garrisons, local dynasts, city and temple institutions, scribes, judges, estate managers, tax and tribute systems, and several legal traditions shared implementation unevenly. `Satrapy` and administrative uniformity require source-specific use; local continuity did not equal autonomy or consent.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Royal inscriptions privilege Ahuramazda and a moral vocabulary of truth, order, and loyalty, while the empire contained many locally maintained cults and institutions. Evidence does not support one imposed imperial religion, a modern policy of toleration, or an uncomplicated Zoroastrian orthodoxy across every reign and region.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Elamite, Babylonian, Old Persian, Aramaic, Egyptian, Greek, and other scribal practices supported royal representation, provisioning, travel, law, accounting, astronomy, medicine, surveying, and local scholarship. Multilingual evidence must preserve who wrote, translated, stored, copied, and could read each record rather than treating trilingual monuments as mass communication.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Roads, ferries, bridges, relays, travel authorizations and rations, canals, qanat-related systems where securely evidenced, fortifications, ships, siege equipment, palaces, terraces, stonework, glazed brick, tablets, papyri, seals, weights, and coinage depended on regional skills, supplies, and varied labor statuses.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Persepolis and Susa architecture, reliefs, glazed brick, metalwork, seals, textiles, vessels, royal tombs, and locally made objects joined imperial representation to regionally diverse workshops. Delegation scenes project ordered participation but cannot prove harmonious equality; Hollow Grove receives provenance and coordination logic, not replica capitals or imperial costume.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Conquest under Cyrus and Cambyses, Darius's suppression of revolts, Scythian and Aegean campaigns, the Ionian Revolt, Persian Wars, Egyptian revolts, dynastic conflict, the revolt of Cyrus the Younger, regional rebellions, and Artaxerxes III's reconquests reveal both reach and recurrent coercive crisis. Conquest, tribute, execution, deportation, and forced service never become Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, dates, wine, livestock, timber, stone, metals, textiles, horses, crafted goods, silver, coin, rations, taxes, tribute, diplomatic gifts, labor, and information moved through royal, temple, estate, market, military, and household channels. Movement type, legal status, source institution, and coercion remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The empire ruled, allied with, taxed, recruited from, exchanged with, and fought Babylonian, Egyptian, Anatolian, Levantine, Iranian, Central Asian, South Asian, Cypriot, Phoenician, and Greek communities. Greek–Persian conflict is one relationship among many, and Greek communities also lived within imperial territories and served in imperial networks.<br>

## Flynt — Macedonia

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_05.flynt.macedonia`<br>
**Primary culture:** Macedonia<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full kingdom horizon / Late-dominant<br>
**Date relevance:** Argead Macedonia throughout 539–336 BCE with uneven early evidence; decisive consolidation and expansion especially under Philip II, 359–336. Macedonia is not backdated as Philip II's mature system across two centuries. Aigai, Pella, royal succession, regional resources, diplomacy, and neighboring pressure precede Philip; army expansion, force integration, mining revenue, sieges, and hegemonic institutions belong primarily to the final decades and remain inseparable from conquest and civilian harm.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for phase-attributed force integration, resource-to-logistics coordination, coalition command with coercion audit; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Amyntas I, Alexander I, Perdiccas II, Archelaus, Amyntas III, Eurydice, Alexander II, Perdiccas III, Philip II, Olympias, Parmenion, Antipater, and the young Alexander III belong to different phases. Paeonian, Illyrian, Thracian, Epirote, Thessalian, Chalcidian, Athenian, Theban, and Persian actors shaped Macedonian choices; later literary accounts require source-distance labels.<br>
**Major locations:** Aigai, its cemetery and late palace, Pella, Dion, Mieza, Amphipolis, Pydna, Methone, Olynthus, Philippi, Upper Macedonian regions, river plains, mountain routes, mines, timber zones, harbors, and frontiers with Paeonia, Illyria, Thrace, Epirus, Thessaly, and the Chalcidice. **Major works or records:** Archaeology at Aigai, Pella and regional sites; tombs and burials; inscriptions, treaties, decrees, coinage, weapons, fortifications, pottery, and architecture; Achaemenid and Greek evidence for early relations; Thucydides, Xenophon, Demosthenes, Diodorus, Theopompus fragments, and later Alexander historians read with genre, agenda, and chronological distance attached.<br>

**Religion and worldview:** Royal ancestry claims, Greek cults and sanctuaries, local and regional traditions, funerary practice, festivals, and dynastic display supported belonging and legitimacy. **Science and knowledge:** Terrain, horses, agriculture, mining, timber, road and harbor access, siegecraft, fortification, diplomacy, court education, coinage, and combined field operations developed through regional and imported expertise. **Technology and infrastructure:** Iron weapons, shields, cavalry equipment, the sarissa in Philip's late army, siege engines, fortifications, roads, harbors, ships, mines, mints, palaces, theaters, masonry, mosaics, storage, and agricultural systems depended on specialists, raw materials, revenue, labor, and territorial control.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Aigai burials, metalwork, painting, mosaics, palace and theater architecture, coin types, pottery, and imported and locally made objects show changing royal and regional participation in the Greek world. **Warfare and strategic capacity:** Macedonia faced Persian, Paeonian, Illyrian, Thracian, Greek, and internal dynastic pressures. Philip expanded and integrated infantry and cavalry, used siege operations, secured resources, intervened through diplomacy and war, destroyed Olynthus, won at Chaeronea in 338, and organized the League of Corinth. Expansion, city destruction, enslavement, coercive alliance, and royal success never become Capacity Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Timber, grain, livestock, horses, metals, gold and silver, pitch, weapons, pottery, luxury goods, coin, military pay, taxes, and information moved through inland, river, Aegean, royal, civic, and military networks. Resource control funded capacity but also intensified extraction and conquest.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Macedonia interacted with the Achaemenid Empire, Greek poleis and leagues, Paeonians, Illyrians, Thracians, Epirotes, Thessalians, Chalcidians, and coastal cities through tribute, alliance, marriage, hostage-taking, trade, arbitration, siege, annexation, and hegemonic settlement. Philip's death and Alexander's accession close the Point; Alexander's campaigns belong to Point VI.<br>
