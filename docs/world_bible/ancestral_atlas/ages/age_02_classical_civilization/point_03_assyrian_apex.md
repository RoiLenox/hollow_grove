Point type: Sequential<br>
Boundary rationale: The supplied 722–612 BCE range is retained. Its opening follows Samaria's fall and Sargon II's accession; its closing is the supplied destruction of Nineveh. The endpoint is a major Assyrian capital and heartland collapse, not the final disappearance of Assyrian kingship: Ashur-uballit II continued from Harran until 609. ## Historical summary

The Sargonid Assyrian state reached exceptional territorial, infrastructural, and archival scale before civil conflict and Babylonian–Median campaigns destroyed its heartland capitals. Judah survived the 701 campaign as a reduced Assyrian client and later navigated imperial retreat; Kushite and Saite rulers contested and recomposed authority in Egypt; and Babylon moved through local, Chaldean, Assyrian, and finally Nabopolassar's rule. The coordinate is an apex-to-collapse system, not one stable century.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Assyrian Apex** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [II:2 — Kings, Prophets, and Assyrian Expansion](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_02_kings_prophets_and_assyrian_expansion.md) after the northern Kingdom of Israel falls, but not after the regional interaction system disappears. Judah receives migrants and grows under Assyrian dominance; Kushite Egypt becomes a direct imperial rival; Babylon remains a contested kingship and scholarly center; and Sargon II begins the Sargonid phase of Neo-Assyria.

## Transition into next Point

The fall of Nineveh closes this supplied Point before [II:4 — Neo-Babylonian World](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_04_neo_babylonian_world.md), but the transition is staggered. Assyrian resistance continues from Harran until 609; Nabopolassar's Babylonian dynasty, Judah, and Saite Egypt cross the 612 boundary; and Point IV's new assignments must distinguish capital destruction, polity end, and continuing communities.

## Stonebend — Judah

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_03.stonebend.judah`<br>
**Primary culture:** Judah<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Kingdom of Judah throughout 722–612 BCE, continuing to the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE. Judah grows institutionally and demographically after Samaria's fall, suffers severe territorial destruction in Sennacherib's 701 campaign while Jerusalem survives, remains under shifting Assyrian dominance, and enters a late seventh-century transition associated with Josiah. Biblical composition and reform claims require source-layer review.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-source infrastructure and campaign concordance, stamped storage and administrative provenance, source-layered legal and historical compilation; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Hezekiah, Manasseh, Amon, and Josiah form the royal sequence most relevant to the Point. Sennacherib is an independent adversarial witness; Sargon II, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal, Psamtik I, and Necho II shape the wider system. Isaiah, Micah, Zephaniah, Nahum, and the royal steward named in the Siloam tomb inscription belong to different contemporary or transmitted evidence layers.<br>
**Major locations:** Jerusalem, Lachish, Azekah, Ramat Rahel, Arad, Beersheba, the Shephelah, the Benjamin plateau, the Siloam water system, and Assyrian Nineveh. Destruction, rebuilding, administrative use, and royal attribution must be attached to site and phase.<br>
**Major works or records:** Sennacherib's inscriptions and Lachish reliefs, `lmlk` and related seal impressions, the Siloam inscription, the Siloam tomb or royal-steward inscription, ostraca, bullae and seals, weights, architecture, waterworks, destruction layers, burials, and Kings, Isaiah, Chronicles, and prophetic collections read at their composition and transmission dates.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Davidic kingship worked through Jerusalem's palace and temple, officials, scribes, estates, fortified and administrative centers, storage and collection systems, military levies, local households, and Assyrian vassal obligations. The scale and purpose of individual stamped-jar systems and reforms remain debated.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Yahwistic temple, household, funerary, and local practices coexisted with multiple divine symbols and cult places. Biblical accounts of Hezekian or Josianic reform are historically important but cannot be treated as transparent decrees, universally completed programs, or Stonebend constitutional law.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Alphabetic inscription, seals, weights, collection marks, architectural and hydraulic planning, agricultural accounting, scribal copying, legal formulation, and historical-theological compilation expanded. The dates and editorial layers of biblical works remain explicit.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortifications, broad walls, gates, rock-cut water channels and tunnels, storage jars, olive and wine production, terraces, roads, iron tools and weapons, ceramics, seals, and administrative buildings supported a small kingdom under changing imperial pressure.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Seals, stamp impressions, carved inscriptions, tomb architecture, ivories, figurines, ceramics, and manuscript traditions show official, household, and regional expression. Aniconic or iconoclastic claims require phase-specific evidence and never define one uniform Judahite style.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Judah participated in regional revolt after Sargon II's death; Sennacherib captured Lachish and many fortified places in 701 while Jerusalem survived under tribute. Later Assyrian withdrawal and Egyptian intervention altered strategy. Siege destruction, forced movement, and royal resistance are evidence and cautions, not Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, oil, wine, livestock, pottery, textiles, metals, timber, aromatics, and tribute moved through highland, Shephelah, Negev, coastal, Arabian, Egyptian, and Assyrian networks. Tax, royal storage, tribute, household production, and market exchange remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Judah interacted with Assyria, Egypt, Philistine cities, Edom, Moab, Ammon, former Israelite territories, Arabian networks, and Babylonia. Its survival after 701 involved loss, submission, internal growth, and changing memory rather than simple victory or isolation.<br>

## Sandmanor — Egypt, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Sixth Dynasties

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_03.sandmanor.egypt_twenty_fifth_and_twenty_sixth_dynasties`<br>
**Primary culture:** Egypt, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Sixth Dynasties<br>
**Temporal fit:** Dynasty 25 full / terminal; Dynasty 26 partial / continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Kushite Twenty-Fifth Dynasty within Egypt to 656 BCE; emerging Saite Twenty-Sixth Dynasty from 664 BCE and continuing beyond 612. The supplied composite label crosses rival and overlapping dynastic claims. Kushite rulers joined Nubian and Egyptian institutions and withdrew after Assyrian invasions; Psamtik I consolidated Saite rule through a different network. Neither dynasty is a simple national restoration or one uniform style.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for cross-regional dynastic identity, selective artistic citation with source context, intermediary diplomacy across changing regimes; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Piye supplies essential pre-Point dynastic context; Shabaka, Shebitku, Taharqa, and Tanutamani belong to Kushite rule. Necho I and Psamtik I belong to the Saite transition. Shepenwepet II, Amenirdis I, and Nitocris I show the political and ritual importance of the God's Wife of Amun; Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal are adversarial Assyrian witnesses.<br>
**Major locations:** Napata and Gebel Barkal, Memphis, Thebes and Karnak, Sais, Tanis, the Delta, Nubian royal cemeteries at el-Kurru and Nuri, Levantine corridors, and Assyrian capitals. `Egypt` cannot erase the Kushite homeland or different Upper and Lower Egyptian power centers.<br>
**Major works or records:** Kushite royal stelae and temple inscriptions, Taharqa's monuments and objects, God's Wife adoption and donation texts, Saite royal and private inscriptions, Theban tombs, statues, Assyrian annals and reliefs, later king lists and classical accounts, and archaeological evidence from Egypt and Sudan.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Pharaonic kingship operated through temples, regional rulers, military commanders, officials, estates, the God's Wife institution, and local dynastic networks. Kushite and Saite control differed by region and moment; Assyrian appointment claims do not by themselves prove direct uniform rule.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Amun traditions linked Gebel Barkal, Napata, Thebes, and royal legitimacy, alongside many local cults and temple institutions. Kushite participation in Egyptian religious forms was active selection and recomposition, not cultural imitation or proof of one identity.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Hieroglyphic and hieratic writing, temple scholarship, administrative records, genealogies, artistic study of older monuments, calendrical practice, healing traditions, and multilingual diplomacy persisted through institutions whose access and regional survival varied.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Stone and mud-brick building, temple renewal, funerary construction, river transport, irrigation and agricultural works, metallurgy, faience, sculpture, textiles, chariots, ships, and military equipment joined Nile, Nubian, Mediterranean, and Levantine resources.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Kushite royal images combined Egyptian conventions with distinct Nubian regalia and portrait choices. Kushite and Saite artists selectively cited older Egyptian forms while also innovating; later erasure or alteration of Kushite names and regalia exposes contested memory.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Kushite rulers supported Levantine resistance and fought Assyrian invasions; Esarhaddon captured Memphis in 671, and Ashurbanipal's campaigns drove Taharqa and then Tanutamani south. Psamtik I's consolidation relied partly on military intermediaries. Conquest and mercenary service are cautions, not Sandmanor Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Gold, livestock, ivory, ebony, stone, grain, textiles, metalwork, crafted goods, and information moved along Nile, desert, Red Sea, Mediterranean, Levantine, and Aegean routes. Tribute, temple revenue, diplomatic gift, mercenary payment, and trade require separate labels.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Kushite and Saite rulers negotiated with Egyptian regional elites, temples, Assyria, Judah and Levantine states, Greek and Carian intermediaries, and Nubian communities. The shift between dynasties is a contested recomposition of relationships, not one national restoration narrative.<br>

**Date relevance:** Babylon as city, cult center, scholarly community, and contested kingship throughout 722–612 BCE; Nabopolassar's dynasty from 626 BCE continues into Point IV. The label is not one continuous Babylonian regime. Marduk-apla-iddina II, Assyrian kings, appointed rulers, Shamash-shuma-ukin, and Nabopolassar belong to different political phases; Sennacherib destroyed Babylon in 689 and Esarhaddon sponsored rebuilding.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for city-temple continuity across regime change, destruction-and-rebuilding provenance, chronicle and scholarly witness comparison; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>

Scholars, temple personnel, merchants, and local families must not disappear behind royal succession.<br>
**Major locations:** Babylon, Esagil, Etemenanki, Borsippa, Sippar, Kutha, Nippur, Uruk, the Sealand and Bit-Yakin, Assur, Nineveh, Elam, and Median frontiers. Southern Mesopotamia was a field of cities, tribes, wetlands, estates, and temples, not a city-state under one continuous administration.<br>
**Major works or records:** Babylonian chronicles and king lists, legal and administrative tablets, letters, contracts, temple and family archives, scholarly tablets and colophons, omen reports, Assyrian royal inscriptions, Esarhaddon's Babylon inscriptions, building deposits, archaeology, and later copies read with custody and political genre attached.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Babylonian kingship, Assyrian direct rule, appointed kings, local city assemblies and elders where attested, temple households, governors, tribal leaders, merchant and landholding families, and military coalitions shared or contested implementation. A king list is succession evidence, not a complete constitution.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Marduk and Esagil held exceptional civic and royal importance alongside Nabu, Ishtar, Shamash, and other city cults. Destruction and rebuilding accounts use divine anger and reconciliation as political theology; Glaüshouse records that language without converting it into clinical causation.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Cuneiform legal, administrative, lexical, divinatory, medical, mathematical, astronomical, calendrical, historical, and literary traditions continued through Babylonian and Assyrian scholarly networks. Exact tablet date, copy history, city, profession, and patron remain attached.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Canals, quays, walls, gates, temples, domestic buildings, agricultural estates, boats, storage, brick production, metal and textile craft, tablets, seals, and measurement systems required continuing labor and maintenance across repeated political rupture.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Inscribed bricks, seals, relief fragments, cult objects, temple furnishings, glazed materials, sculpture, and scribal layout survive unevenly. Royal destruction and rebuilding narratives cannot substitute for archaeological damage and repair sequences.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Babylonian, Chaldean, Aramaean, Elamite, Assyrian, and Median forces formed changing coalitions. Siege, sack, starvation, destruction in 689, the 648 civil-war ending, and the 626–612 revolt expose severe civilian and institutional cost. These are cautions, never Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, dates, livestock, reeds, textiles, metals, timber, stone, crafted goods, land income, taxes, temple offerings, people, and information moved through canals, rivers, Gulf, Iranian, northern Mesopotamian, and Levantine routes under multiple legal statuses.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Babylon's actors negotiated, rebelled, allied, and fought with Assyria, Elam, Chaldean and Aramaean groups, other Babylonian cities, and the Medes. Cultural and scholarly continuity did not imply political agreement, while Assyrian rebuilding did not erase responsibility for destruction.<br>

## Flynt — Neo-Assyria

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_03.flynt.neo_assyria`<br>
**Primary culture:** Neo-Assyria<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Nineveh-terminal; polity continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Sargonid Neo-Assyria from Sargon II's accession in 722/721 through Nineveh's fall in 612 BCE; remnant kingship at Harran to 609. The Point contains expansion, several capitals, provincial and scholarly networks, civil wars, rebellion, contraction, and heartland destruction. Nineveh's fall closes the supplied Point but not the last Assyrian political resistance, and imperial violence or forced resettlement never becomes a Gift.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for state correspondence and field-report comparison, multi-capital infrastructure dependency mapping, collapse sequence beyond capital loss; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal, Shamash-shuma-ukin, Ashur-etel-ilani, Sin-sharra-ishkun, and Ashur-uballit II expose succession and fragmentation. Taharqa, Tanutamani, Hezekiah, Marduk-apla-iddina II, Nabopolassar, and Cyaxares provide independent or adversarial relationships.<br>
**Major locations:** Dur-Sharrukin, Nineveh, Assur, Kalhu, Arbela, Harran, Babylon, major provincial centers and road corridors, Judah and the Levant, Anatolian and Zagros frontiers, Egypt, Elam, and Babylonia. Capital sequence is an operational and political choice, not interchangeable monumental scenery.<br>
**Major works or records:** RINAP 2–5 royal inscriptions, State Archives of Assyria letters and administrative texts, eponym lists, treaties, legal records, scholarly reports, palace and temple archives, Ashurbanipal Library tablets, reliefs and captions, Babylonian chronicles including the Fall of Nineveh Chronicle, and archaeology.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Kings ruled through queens and royal households, succession arrangements, palaces, temples, magnates, governors, military commanders, deputies, scribes, scholars, provincial institutions, client rulers, treaties, and resettled specialists. Correspondence reveals delay, disagreement, petition, and local variation behind royal claims.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Ashur, Ishtar, Nabu, Sin, Shamash, and other deities framed kingship, scholarship, ritual, treaty, and war. Omens and expert reports were institutional knowledge practices; royal divine authorization is evidence, not Law of Capacity.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Letters, reports, archives, omen scholarship, medicine, lexical and literary copying, astronomy, geography, accounting, translation, engineering records, and collected tablets linked experts from Assyria and Babylonia. Ashurbanipal's libraries were assembled through several forms of acquisition that require provenance review.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Roads, relays, bridges, canals, aqueducts and waterworks, walls, gates, large capitals, palaces, storage, siege machinery, cavalry, chariots, weapons, tablets, seals, and workshops depended on regional materials, skilled personnel, maintenance, and often coerced labor.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Palace reliefs, colossal guardians, wall paintings, ivories, metalwork, seals, stelae, prisms, tablets, gardens, and planned vistas joined technical skill to royal narrative. Represented domination, deportation, and lion hunts remain critical evidence, never a Flynt style kit.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Campaigns, sieges, annexation, tribute, forced resettlement, civil war, the 701 destruction in Judah, invasions of Egypt, war with Elam, Babylonian rebellion, and Babylonian–Median attacks reveal both capacity and catastrophic failure. Civilian harm, extraction, and conquest never become Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Taxes, tribute, booty, agricultural produce, textiles, timber, stone, metals, horses, crafted goods, specialist labor, deportees, and ordinary exchange moved through unequal networks. Every movement must retain legal status, coercion, custody, and local effect.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Assyria administered, allied, corresponded, traded, taxed, invaded, resettled, and fought across Babylonia, Egypt, Judah, Phoenician and Anatolian cities, Urartu, Elam, Arabian and Iranian regions, and its own provinces. Nineveh's fall was a major terminal event, but Harran proves that capital loss and polity end were not simultaneous.<br>
