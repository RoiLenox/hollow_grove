Point type: Sequential<br>
Boundary rationale: The supplied 612–539 BCE range is retained. The opening is Nineveh's fall, although Assyrian resistance continues to 609 and Nabopolassar's dynasty began in 626. The closing is Cyrus's capture of Babylon and the end of the Neo-Babylonian dynasty. Judah's kingdom ends conventionally in 587/586, independent Median kingship around 550, and Saite rule only in 525; the Point therefore records four different transitions rather than one synchronized imperial block.

## Historical summary

After Nineveh, Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar II consolidated the Neo-Babylonian Empire while Judah moved through Egyptian and Babylonian pressure, deportations, Jerusalem's destruction, and differently situated homeland and Babylonian communities. Saite Egypt remained an independent Nile and Mediterranean power; Median power helped destroy Assyria before Cyrus defeated Astyages; and Babylon's capture in 539 transferred imperial rule without ending Babylonian urban, temple, archival, or scholarly life.

## Reason this Point exists

This Point carries the approved four-House coordinate for **Neo-Babylonian World** and adds a reviewable inheritance layer without making any source culture a literal predecessor state of Hollow Grove. Any departure from the supplied baseline is recorded in [Chronology Revision 1](../../CHRONOLOGY_REVISION_01_REPORT.md).

## Transition from previous Point

The Atlas moves from [II:3 — Assyrian Apex](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_03_assyrian_apex.md) through staggered survival and succession. Assyrian resistance continues to 609 after Nineveh; Nabopolassar's dynasty already rules Babylon; Judah and Saite Egypt cross 612 as continuing states; and Median coalition power is visible in Assyria's destruction. The boundary changes the comparative center rather than beginning four new societies.

## Transition into next Point

Cyrus's capture of Babylon opens [II:5 — Achaemenid World](../../ages/age_02_classical_civilization/point_05_achaemenid_world.md) as an Achaemenid-centered coordinate, but the handoff remains uneven. Babylonian urban, temple, archival, and scholarly institutions continue; Median people and elites remain within Persian systems after Astyages's earlier defeat; Judahite communities do not all return at once; and Saite Egypt remains independent until 525. The Greek, Late Egypt, and Macedonia assignments are parallel comparison choices, not direct replacements created in 539.

## Stonebend — Judah

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_04.stonebend.judah`<br>
**Primary culture:** Judah<br>
**Temporal fit:** Kingdom partial / terminal; communities continue<br>
**Date relevance:** Judah as a kingdom, 612–587/586 BCE; communities in Judah, Babylonia, Egypt, and elsewhere through 539 BCE. The supplied label changes referent after Jerusalem's destruction. It includes the remaining homeland population, deported and migrant communities, and transmitted legal, prophetic, administrative, and historical records—not a kingdom in uninterrupted exile and not a claim that all Judahites were deported.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-stage state rupture chronology, distributed community and household legal records, portable textual and institutional memory; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Josiah closes the preceding phase in 609; Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, Zedekiah, Gedaliah, Nebuchadnezzar II, Nabuzaradan, and Cyrus II belong to the political sequence. Jeremiah and Ezekiel preserve differently located literary voices whose composition and transmission histories remain explicit.<br>
**Major locations:** Jerusalem, Lachish, Azekah, Ramat Rahel, Mizpah/Tell en-Nasbeh, the Benjamin region, Babylon, settlements represented in the Al-Yahudu corpus, and Egyptian destinations. A textual place-name does not create a securely excavated site.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Babylonian Chronicles, ration tablets naming Jehoiachin, Lachish ostraca, seals and bullae, destruction and settlement archaeology, the Al-Yahudu and related cuneiform archives, and Kings, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Lamentations, and other biblical texts read according to genre, date, and editorial history.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Davidic kingship ended through staged Babylonian intervention. Vassal administration, palace and temple officials, military officers, scribes, local elders and households, a Babylonian-appointed governor at Mizpah, deportee communities, and imperial land-service arrangements preserved different pieces of public and communal organization.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Jerusalem temple practice, local and household worship, prophetic interpretation, lament, covenant and legal traditions, and exilic reformulation responded differently to defeat. Destruction is not divine diagnosis, and later canonical authority does not become Stonebend constitutional law.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Alphabetic and cuneiform recordkeeping, letters, seals, legal contracts, ration lists, naming practice, scribal transmission, calendrical dating, historical compilation, lament, and cross-language adaptation preserve several communities' knowledge under rupture.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortifications, gates, watch and signal systems, storage, ceramics, seals, writing materials, agriculture, roads, siege works, and Babylonian canal settlements expose both destroyed infrastructure and continuing household production.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Inscribed ostraca, seals, bullae, ceramics, architectural fragments, lament poetry, prophetic imagery, and transmitted manuscripts preserve expression across destruction and displacement. No single exilic style or uniform identity is inferred.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Egyptian-Babylonian competition, Carchemish in 605, Babylonian campaigns, Judahite revolts, the 597 surrender, and the 587/586 siege caused death, deprivation, destruction, deportation, and political collapse. Resistance and conquest are evidence and cautions, never Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, oil, wine, livestock, ceramics, metals, timber, taxes, tribute, rations, land-service obligations, and household goods moved through Judahite, Egyptian, Babylonian, and exilic settings. Deportation and assigned service must never be mislabeled voluntary exchange.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Judah's rulers and communities interacted with Egypt, Babylonia, Edom, Moab, Ammon, Phoenician and Philistine cities, homeland neighbors, and other deported groups. Cyrus's 539 conquest closes the Point but does not by itself describe every community's return or later status.<br>

## Sandmanor — Saite Egypt

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_04.sandmanor.saite_egypt`<br>
**Primary culture:** Saite Egypt<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Saite Twenty-Sixth Dynasty throughout 612–539 BCE, continuing until the Persian conquest in 525 BCE. Necho II, Psamtik II, Apries, and Amasis belong to different military, diplomatic, temple, and Mediterranean phases. Naukratis was a multi-community Egyptian port, not simply a Greek colony; foreign military service and exchange must retain named intermediaries and unequal conditions.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for multi-community port and sanctuary provenance, military and diplomatic intermediary networks, selective revival with present authorship; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Necho II, Psamtik II, Apries, and Amasis are the principal rulers; Psamtik I dies near the opening and Psamtik III belongs after the endpoint. Nitocris I and Ankhnesneferibre represent the God's Wife of Amun institution. Nebuchadnezzar II, Cyaxares, Astyages, Croesus, and Mediterranean military and trading intermediaries shape external relations.<br>
**Major locations:** Sais, Memphis, Thebes and Karnak, Naukratis, the western Delta and Canopic branch, Elephantine, the eastern Delta, Mediterranean harbors, Nubian campaign zones, and Levantine corridors. Daphnae/Tell Defenneh and other identifications retain archaeological debate.<br>
**Major works or records:** Royal and private inscriptions, Serapeum and donation stelae, God's Wife records, statues and tomb programs, demotic and hieratic documents, Naukratis dedications and archaeology, Greek and Carian inscriptions, later Herodotean accounts, and Babylonian records read with date, audience, and source distance attached.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Pharaonic kingship worked through royal households, temples, regional officials, military commanders, land and revenue administration, the God's Wife institution, and port and foreign-service intermediaries. Dynastic unity did not erase local institutions or contested succession between Apries and Amasis.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Amun, Neith, Ptah, Osiris, Apis, and many local cults structured institutions and public identities. Egyptian and non-Egyptian communities maintained and shared different sanctuaries at Naukratis; participation and adaptation did not dissolve distinct names or authority.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Hieroglyphic, hieratic, demotic, Greek, and Carian writing, temple and legal scholarship, genealogy, medicine, calendrical practice, artistic study, translation, navigation, and geographic intelligence circulated through unequal institutions and intermediaries.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Nile transport, river harbors, sea-going ships, irrigation, mud-brick and stone construction, metalwork, faience, sculpture, pottery, textile work, military equipment, and sanctuary building joined local and imported materials.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Saite artists selectively cited older Egyptian forms while producing new portraits, tomb programs, statues, bronzes, faience, and inscriptions. Naukratis objects show Egyptian, Greek, Cypriot, and Phoenician makers and practices; source form, maker, patron, and new context remain attributable.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Necho II campaigned in the Levant before Babylonian victory at Carchemish; Psamtik II campaigned in Nubia; Apries intervened in the Levant and Libya; and civil conflict brought Amasis to power. Foreign soldiers served under varied conditions. Campaigning and service are evidence, not Pattern Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, wine, oil, metals, timber, stone, ceramics, faience, textiles, incense, animals, and coined or weighed value moved along Nile, desert, Red Sea, Levantine, Aegean, Cypriot, and Phoenician routes. Port access, sanctuary offering, tax, military pay, diplomatic gift, and commerce remain distinct.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Saite rulers negotiated and fought with Babylonia, Judah and other Levantine polities, Kush, Libya, Lydia, Cyprus, Greek cities, Phoenician communities, and internal Egyptian institutions. Mediterranean exchange was mediated, regulated, and sometimes coercive rather than frictionless cosmopolitanism.<br>

**Resources and trade:** Grain, dates, wool, textiles, livestock, metals, timber, stone, wine, oil, silver, land income, taxes, temple offerings, rations, and labor moved through river, canal, Gulf, Iranian, Arabian, Anatolian, and Levantine networks under different legal and coercive statuses.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** The empire fought, ruled, deported, taxed, exchanged, and negotiated across Judah and the Levant, Egypt, Arabia, Anatolia, Elam, and Iranian powers. Cyrus's 539 takeover ended the dynasty but reused Babylonian titles, institutions, personnel, and religious idioms.<br>

## Flynt — Medes

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_04.flynt.medes`<br>
**Primary culture:** Medes<br>
**Temporal fit:** Partial / Terminal; legacy continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Median power under Cyaxares and Astyages, 612–c. 550 BCE; Median people, elites, and regional institutions continue within Cyrus's expanding polity to 539. The Medes are securely involved in Assyria's fall and Astyages is attested in the Nabonidus Chronicle, but the size and structure of a Median 'empire,' Herodotus's earlier dynasty, Ecbatana's material record, and the ethnic attribution of western Iranian sites remain disputed.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for coalition campaigning with independent witnesses, fortified highland node planning with attribution limits, operational continuity through regime integration; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Cyaxares and Astyages are the securely relevant royal names. Nabopolassar and Cyrus II provide Babylonian and Persian relationships. Herodotus's Deioces, Phraortes, Harpagus, Mandane, and dynastic narrative are later Greek traditions whose historical components cannot be assumed.<br>
**Major locations:** Western Iran and the central Zagros, Hamadan/Ecbatana, Tepe Nush-i Jan, Godin Tepe, Assur and Nineveh, Harran, Anatolian and Lydian frontiers, and Anshan. No Median-period remains are securely established at Hamadan, and other sites require attribution labels rather than ethnic certainty.<br>
**Major works or records:** The Fall of Nineveh Chronicle, Nabonidus Chronicle, Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions, later Herodotus and other classical accounts, archaeology from Nush-i Jan and western Iran, Achaemenid references to Media and Medes, and modern debates over empire, hegemony, and continuity. No native Median archive survives.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Cuneiform sources support named leaders and collective military action but do not reveal a full constitution, provincial system, or archive. Models range from kingdom or hegemony to looser domination. Later Achaemenid prominence of Medes does not prove unchanged institutions from Cyaxares.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Highland ritual buildings, personal names, later accounts of Magi, and broader Iranian traditions supply partial evidence. Specific Median doctrine, Zoroastrian orthodoxy, hereditary priesthood, or fire-temple identification cannot be securely projected across the Point.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Route knowledge, coalition timing, highland resource coordination, building practice, craft, animal management, and cross-polity intelligence are plausible operational fields. The absence of a Median written corpus sharply limits claims about formal science, doctrine, or administration.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Fortified mud-brick complexes, columned halls, storage, local craft, agriculture, animal husbandry, weapons, and route-linked highland settlements are archaeologically visible in western Iran, but individual forms cannot automatically be labeled Median state technology.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Architecture, ceramics, metalwork, seals, and dress associated by scholars with Median-period western Iran remain heterogeneous and difficult to assign ethnically. Later Achaemenid representations of Medes are evidence for imperial identity, not transparent portraits of the pre-550 polity.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Median forces joined the destruction of Assur and Nineveh; later traditions attribute wider conquests and cavalry reform, but their details require caution. Cyrus defeated Astyages around 550. Siege, conquest, and imperial domination remain evidence and failure conditions, not Capacity Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Agriculture, pastoral resources, horses, metals, crafted goods, tribute or gifts, and overland exchange moved through Zagros and plateau routes. Sparse records make it difficult to separate royal extraction, coalition supply, local exchange, and long-distance trade.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Medes interacted with Babylonia, Assyria, western Iranian groups, Scythians and other mobile powers, Urartu, Lydia, Elam, and Persians. The Babylonian alliance and Cyrus's takeover are firm transitions; the breadth of Median control between them remains a research question.<br>
