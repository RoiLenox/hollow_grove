## Stonebend — Roman Republic

**House:** [Stonebend](../../houses/STONEBEND_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_08.stonebend.roman_republic`<br>
**Primary culture:** Roman Republic<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Expansion through war<br>
**Date relevance:** Roman Republic from the First Punic War in 264 through the destruction of Carthage and Corinth in 146. Rome's Italian alliance system, magistracies, Senate, citizenship gradations, roads, colonies, legions, naval adaptation, provinces, and negotiated or coercive incorporation expand unevenly. Republican institutions and infrastructure are primary inheritance; conquest, enslavement, provincial extraction, and triumphal memory are not Gifts.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for allied citizenship and treaty incorporation records, road colony and provincial infrastructure provenance, republican command and fiscal accountability records; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Fabius Maximus, Scipio Africanus, Cato the Elder, Scipio Aemilianus, Roman magistrates, senators, assembly voters, allied communities, Italian and provincial soldiers, engineers, enslaved people, and subject communities belong to distinct evidence settings.<br>
**Major locations:** Rome, Latium, Capua, Sicily, Sardinia, Spain, North Africa, Carthage, Corinth, Italy's allied communities, roads, colonies, harbors, and battle landscapes. Municipal, allied, colonial, provincial, and military status must remain separate.<br>
**Major works or records:** Senate and popular-law traditions, treaties, colonies, inscriptions, roads, milestones, coinage, military and legal texts, Polybius, Livy, and archaeology. Much republican narrative is later and elite-centered; material and epigraphic evidence require independent provenance.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Consuls, praetors, Senate, assemblies, tribunes, magistrates, allied treaties, colonies, provincial commanders, taxation, and military commands changed as Rome expanded. Citizenship and legal status were graded; provincial administration and public finance were not one uniform system.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Roman civic religion, mos maiorum, triumph, patronage, household cult, allied ritual, Greek philosophical influence, and provincial religious practice coexisted. Triumph and freedom language are political performances, not proof of moral legitimacy.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Law, surveying, logistics, bridge and road engineering, naval adaptation, military medicine, accounting, diplomacy, geography, and rhetoric supported expansion. Practical knowledge moved through soldiers, contractors, allied communities, and local specialists.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Roads, bridges, colonies, ports, siege works, camps, ships, harbors, weapons, aqueduct precursors, surveying tools, milestones, coin, and records linked infrastructure to military and fiscal power. Labor, extraction, maintenance, and local custody remain explicit.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Triumphs, monuments, coins, inscriptions, temples, portraiture, funerary works, and imported Greek art expressed civic and elite identity. Republican appropriation and later Roman framing must not erase makers, patrons, or conquered communities.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** The three Punic Wars, campaigns in Spain and Greece, the Macedonian and Seleucid wars, siege, naval adaptation, allied manpower, enslavement, displacement, and provincial extraction transformed the Mediterranean. Organizational success and conquest are not permanence Gifts.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, slaves, metals, timber, wine, oil, ceramics, coin, tax, indemnity, military supply, and information moved through Italian, Sicilian, Iberian, African, Greek, and eastern Mediterranean routes.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Rome interacted with Carthage, Greek cities and leagues, Macedonia, the Seleucids, Ptolemaic Egypt, Iberian and North African communities, and Italian allies through treaty, war, arbitration, colonization, commerce, patronage, and coercion.<br>

## Sandmanor — Greek World

**House:** [Sandmanor](../../houses/SANDMANOR_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_08.sandmanor.greek_world`<br>
**Primary culture:** Greek World<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Politically plural / Continues<br>
**Date relevance:** Greek cities, leagues, sanctuaries, schools, and Hellenistic civic networks encountered Roman and Carthaginian expansion throughout 264–146. Greek World is a connected but diverse set of poleis, koina, courts, sanctuaries, and communities. Roman proclamations of freedom, alliance, arbitration, cultural prestige, and destruction must remain situated by speaker, beneficiary, excluded group, and enforcement.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for cross-polity diplomacy and freedom-claim provenance, festival sanctuary and civic memory networks, war displacement and cultural-reception records; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Polybius, Philopoemen, Aristaenus, Flamininus, Antiochus III, Macedonian and Achaean officials, civic envoys, priests, teachers, artists, merchants, displaced people, and unnamed residents represent different civic and social positions.<br>
**Major locations:** Corinth, Athens, Sparta, Argos, Megalopolis, Rhodes, Delphi, Delos, Achaea, Aetolia, Macedonia, Thessaly, Asia Minor, and Roman military and diplomatic sites. Each city's membership, autonomy, and archive require separate treatment.<br>
**Major works or records:** Federal decrees, treaties, arbitration, honorific inscriptions, hostage and citizenship evidence, Polybius, later Livy and Plutarch, philosophical and theatrical texts, coins, sanctuaries, and destruction archaeology.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Poleis and koina retained differing assemblies, councils, magistrates, laws, finances, and diplomatic mechanisms. Rome could promise freedom, supervise, reward, constrain, garrison, or destroy; no declaration represents all Greeks equally.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Civic cults, sanctuaries, festivals, ruler honors, philosophical ethics, household religion, Roman ritual, and local memory coexisted. Cultural admiration and political subordination could occur together.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Rhetoric, philosophy, history, theater, medicine, mathematics, teaching, translation, and diplomatic practice circulated through cities, courts, sanctuaries, and schools. Roman adoption does not erase Greek custodianship or local innovation.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Walls, roads, harbors, theaters, gymnasia, sanctuaries, ships, siege equipment, inscriptions, coinage, and civic archives supported public life and war. Destruction and rebuilding require place-specific provenance.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Drama, sculpture, portraiture, coins, architecture, mosaics, sanctuaries, and public inscriptions were shaped by local makers, royal patrons, Roman patrons, and changing audiences. `Greek culture` is not one transferable style.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** Macedonian, Achaean, Aetolian, Seleucid, Roman, and local wars produced siege, hostage-taking, displacement, enslavement, confiscation, and civic destruction. The destruction of Corinth in 146 is a civic rupture.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, wine, oil, metals, timber, books, artworks, coin, labor, credit, and information moved through ports, sanctuaries, leagues, households, and Roman military routes.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Greek communities negotiated with Rome, Macedonia, Carthage, Ptolemaic Egypt, Seleucid Syria, Rhodes, Pergamon, and one another through embassies, treaties, honors, arbitration, alliance, resistance, and war.<br>

## Flynt — Carthage

**House:** [Flynt](../../houses/FLYNT_ANCESTRY.md)<br>
**Assignment ID:** `atlas.age_02.point_08.flynt.carthage`<br>
**Primary culture:** Carthage<br>
**Temporal fit:** Full / Terminal polity<br>
**Date relevance:** Carthaginian state, ports, fleets, North African and western Mediterranean networks through the Third Punic War and destruction of Carthage in 146. Carthage is treated as a republic and maritime power with civil and military institutions, commercial and agricultural networks, and diverse subject and allied communities. Naval strategy, logistics, and port systems are design inputs; colonization, slavery, war, and destruction remain cautions and historical costs.<br>
**Historical role:** Reviewed inheritance lens for maritime logistics and naval supply coordination, commercial port and agricultural hinterland networks, republican command and defeat-consequence audit; see the linked dossier for evidence, interpretation, and adaptation boundaries.<br>
**Major figures:** Hannibal Barca, Hamilcar Barca, Hasdrubal, Mago, Carthaginian sufetes, councils, generals, merchants, farmers, sailors, allied and subject communities, mercenaries, and Roman commanders belong to different evidence layers.<br>
**Major locations:** Carthage, its harbors, Byrsa, North African agricultural districts, Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, Iberia, Malta, coastal Libya, and western Mediterranean routes. Metropolitan, colonial, allied, and subject status require separate records.<br>
**Major works or records:** Treaties, coinage, archaeology, harbors, ship facilities, inscriptions, Punic and Neo-Punic evidence, Greek and Roman narratives, and agricultural and settlement remains. Roman sources are substantial but adversarial and retrospective.<br>
**Government and institutions:** Sufetes, councils, generals, citizen bodies, merchant and landed interests, allied communities, subject territories, and military commands shaped policy. Civil and military authority were not identical, and command accountability is historically contested.<br>
**Religion and worldview:** Punic religious traditions, local North African practices, Greek and Sicilian contacts, dynastic and civic memory, and military oaths coexisted. Later Roman moralizing cannot stand in for Carthaginian belief.<br>
**Science and knowledge:** Navigation, agriculture, harbor management, finance, diplomacy, siege, shipbuilding, languages, and route intelligence supported Carthage's western networks. Evidence is uneven and often mediated by outsiders.<br>
**Technology and infrastructure:** Harbors, shipyards, ships, roads, storage, agricultural estates, fortifications, weapons, coinage, and port infrastructure connected Carthage to its hinterlands and overseas partners. These depended on labor, extraction, and local relationships.<br>
**Art and cultural expression:** Punic stelae, coins, jewelry, ceramics, architecture, harbor works, funerary material, and hybrid Mediterranean forms express local and connected identities. Avoid treating the tophet or any single site as a total portrait of Carthage.<br>
**Warfare and strategic capacity:** The First, Second, and Third Punic Wars, the Mercenary War, Sicilian and Iberian campaigns, naval operations, siege, allied mobilization, enslavement, and Roman destruction shaped the polity. Strategic sophistication and defeat are both historically bounded, not Gifts of superiority.<br>
**Resources and trade:** Grain, olive products, metals, timber, wine, ceramics, slaves, coin, tribute, agricultural rents, ships, and information moved through ports, estates, colonies, allies, and western Mediterranean routes.<br>
**Major conflicts and relationships:** Carthage interacted with Rome, Greek Sicily, Syracuse, Iberian communities, Numidian kingdoms, Sardinia, Corsica, North African peoples, Ptolemaic Egypt, and Hellenistic powers through treaty, commerce, colonization, alliance, war, and coercion.<br>
